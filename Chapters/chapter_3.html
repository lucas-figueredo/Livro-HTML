3.1 GEOPHYSICS

In the initial phase of exploration, those rock formations that outcrop on the surface as a result of anticline deformations and faults are mapped through aerial photographs.

From direct observations of rocks exposed on the surface or from samples taken from wells drilled for this purpose, studies are conducted on the original succession and age of stratified rocks, as well as their forms, distribution, lithology (chemical composition, density, porosity, etc.), and paleontological content (species of animals and plants), aiming to infer their environment of origin and geological history. Geologists often use the term facies to define physical aspects of rocks, such as stratum geometry, sedimentary structures, colors, and the fossils present. Stratigraphy is the branch of geology that deals with rock strata in time and space, studying their physical attributes and interpreting them in terms of their genesis and temporal arrangement. Stratigraphic charts are created to represent, in a didactic way, the chronology, lithology, etc.

However, rarely can an adequate evaluation of the subsurface (or technically more correct, the subsurface) be obtained by simply extrapolating the characteristics of rocks found on the surface. Advances in geological knowledge of the subsurface are achieved through the use of indirect methods based on measurements of the physical properties of rocks, such as seismic, gravimetric, and electromagnetic methods, to interpret their results in geological terms. This activity is called geophysics, which can be defined as the "study of the Earth using measurements of its physical properties."

Measurements of Earth's gravitational and magnetic fields are known as potential methods, and their intensities are related to the properties of subsurface rocks.

The differences in the densities of materials forming the Earth's interior cause different values of gravitational acceleration, g. These variations are called gravimetric anomalies. The interpretation of gravimetric data is essentially the determination of an internal density distribution that can explain the differences in measured gravity, that is, the observed anomaly. The mass of geological material, i.e., the geological body that produces this gravimetric anomaly, is then modeled to determine its geometric characteristics (size, shape, and depth). The gravimetric method allows estimates of the thickness of sediments in a sedimentary basin and indicates the presence of rocks with anomalous densities, such as metamorphic rocks and salt domes.

Electromagnetic methods aim to measure small variations in the intensity of Earth's magnetic field, a consequence of the irregular distribution of magnetized rocks in the subsurface. They measure electromagnetic properties of rocks, which are closely related to the presence of fluids or metallic minerals. The variations in magnetic properties produced by different rocks can be compared, by analogy, with the magnetite content (iron-based ore) in them. For example, it is known that silica-based rock formations have low magnetite content and therefore present low magnetic susceptibility values.

3.2 SEISMIC METHOD

The basic principle of the seismic method involves measuring the propagation time and amplitude of pressure waves reflected at the interface of different rock formation layers with the objective of mapping their structure. The reflection at the interface between rock formations arises from the difference in their physical properties, particularly density. The seismic wavefront generated at a source (*) propagates through the subsurface, as shown in Figure 1(a). The reflection at a certain point causes part of the wave's energy to return to the surface. The ray is an alternative and very convenient representation of the seismic wave's path. In Figure 1(b), we see the reflected wavefront at a specific point on an interface towards the receiver, as well as the path of the corresponding ray. The recording of the reflected signal is carried out by a device called a seismograph.

An alternative representation of the ray is shown in Figure 2(a). In Figure 2(b), we see the seismic signals of the reflections captured by the receiver. Based on the time delay of these signals, it is possible to infer the position of the interfaces.

Note that reflections farther from the surface have progressively lower amplitudes. This occurs due to the spherical spreading of the signal. This energy dissipation can be estimated, and the signal can be corrected to have the same amplitude as the others, as shown in Figure 3(a). By repeating this process at different positions, the set of reflections can be used to create a map of the subsurface structures. In Figure 3(b) below, we see the result of a sequence of five seismic signals. Geologists often color the upper part of the waves black to facilitate visualization.

The types of sources and receivers vary according to the terrain conditions being surveyed. The most commonly used seismic energy sources on land are dynamite or vibrators. At sea, airguns are used, consisting of compressed air inside a chamber that, when released into the sea, generates pressure pulses.

3.2.1 Rock Properties

A seismic wave is a mechanical wave that propagates in a solid medium. The theory of seismic wave propagation is based on the classical mechanics wave equation. Solutions to the wave equation can be obtained if we consider the medium to be homogeneous, isotropic, and elastic.

A rock is considered homogeneous if it is identical throughout its extent. It is assumed that dividing it into small pieces will retain its physical and chemical properties. This is not true for sedimentary formations since their small structures are formed by rock fragments, and the porous volume is occupied by fluids such as hydrocarbons and water.

A rock is isotropic if its physical properties remain the same in any direction. Since the propagation velocity of seismic waves depends on the density of the medium, variations in this property will affect wave propagation. Oil-bearing rocks are anisotropic because they vary in properties both horizontally and vertically.

There is no perfectly rigid body in nature. All materials undergo deformation to some extent, depending on the applied force. Deformations in solids can be reversible or irreversible and depend on how the external force is applied.

Plastic deformation persists even after the forces that caused it are removed, while elastic deformation disappears once the forces are withdrawn. Therefore, an elastic solid is one in which deformation is proportional to the applied stress. Stress is a force applied perpendicular or parallel to the surface of the body, divided by the surface area: \(\sigma = \frac{F}{A}\) deformation is the change in dimensions caused by stress, per unit of the original dimension.

Hooke's law states that deforming forces are proportional to the elastic deformations produced:

\[\begin{equation} F = -Kx \label{eq:3.1} \end{equation}\]

Next, we will look at some intrinsic properties of homogeneous and isotropic materials.

Young's Modulus (E). Also known as the modulus of elasticity, it is defined as the ratio of the force per unit area to the rate of change in the solid's length per unit of length. It provides a measure of the stiffness of a solid material.

\[\begin{equation} E = \frac{\frac{F}{A}}{\frac{\Delta \ell}{\ell}} = \frac{\sigma}{\frac{\Delta \ell}{\ell}} \label{eq:3.2} \end{equation}\]

Bulk Modulus (K). It is defined as the ratio of the normal force per unit area to the rate of change in volume per unit of volume. It describes the tendency of a material to deform in all directions when subjected to uniform pressure (Figure 4).

\[\begin{equation} K = \frac{\frac{\Delta F}{A}}{\frac{\Delta V}{V}} = V \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta V} \label{eq:3.3} \end{equation}\]

In reservoir studies, it is important to understand how the rock formation expands or contracts under external forces (or pressure). In this case, we adopt the effective compressibility of the formation, defined as:

\[\begin{equation} c = -\frac{1}{V} \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta p} \label{eq:3.4} \end{equation}\]

Note that this is the inverse of the bulk modulus K. The effective pore compressibility is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} c_{p} = \frac{1}{V} \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta p} \label{eq:3.5} \end{equation}\]

Note that there is no negative sign, meaning that when pressure decreases, the porous volume decreases, assuming c remains constant.

Shear Modulus (G). It deals with deformation caused by stress applied orthogonally to the surface where the force acts. The resulting deformation is \(\frac{dx}{dy}\) but it can be approximated by \(\theta\) when this value is small (Figure 5).

\[\begin{equation} G = \frac{\frac{F}{A}} {\frac{dx}{dy}} = \frac{\frac{F}{A}}{\theta} = \frac{\sigma}{\theta} \label{eq:3.6} \end{equation}\]

Poisson's Ratio (\(\sigma\)). It measures the transverse deformation (relative to the longitudinal direction of load application).

\[\begin{equation} \sigma = -\frac{\frac{\Delta x}{x}}{\frac{\Delta y}{y}} \label{eq:3.7} \end{equation}\]

The negative sign in Poisson's ratio is adopted because transverse and longitudinal deformations have opposite signs.

3.2.2 Types of Seismic Waves

There are four types of seismic waves. Two of these, P-waves and S-waves, are called body waves. The P-wave travels in the direction of propagation, while S-waves are generated by shear forces and, therefore, move orthogonally to P-waves. Their velocity is approximately half that of P-waves.

Surface waves occur at the interface between different materials. They are Rayleigh and Love waves. Rayleigh waves cause ellipsoidal motion, similar to ocean waves on a beach, combining vertical and radial movement. They are important in land seismic studies as they are the first to reach receivers and must be discarded during signal processing. Love waves propagate at a slower velocity than Rayleigh waves.

The propagation of P and S waves can be understood by imagining solids as a set of particles connected by springs (Figure 6), as deformations are elastic, meaning forces act to restore the solid to its initial condition.

When subjected to an initial force, the transmission of pressure will be faster if the solid is less compressible (stiffer springs) and less dense.

The velocity of seismic wave propagation is a function of the bulk modulus \(K, \text{ shear modulus } G, \text{ and density } \rho\), or the elastic constants of the solid, as shown in the equations below. The equations can also be written in terms of Young's modulus \(E \text{ and Poisson's ratio } \sigma\), as they are related to compression and shear forces.

\[\begin{equation} v_{P} = \sqrt{\frac{K + \frac{4}{3}G}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{E(1 + \sigma)(1 - 2\sigma)}{\rho(1 - \sigma)}} \label{eq:3.8} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} v_{S} = \sqrt{\frac{E}{2\rho(1 + \sigma)}} \label{eq:3.9} \end{equation}\]

Where

\({v}_{p}\) = P-wave velocity

\({v}_{S}\) = S-wave velocity

Note that although the equation indicates that velocity decreases with increasing density, in practice, denser rocks are also those with higher elastic constants such as K and G. Dense and rigid rocks like gneiss or granite tend to have higher propagation velocities compared to sedimentary rocks.

Figure 7 shows wave propagation velocity values for different types of rocks. Note that at 5000 m/s, we may have shales, sandstones, limestones, salt, or dolomites.

The propagation velocity of seismic waves in sedimentary formations is affected by the porosity (amount of fluids in the pores) of the rocks, as well as their compaction and cementation. Wyllie proposed the following equation for velocity as a function of porosity:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{v} = \frac{(1 - \phi)}{v_{m}} + \frac{\phi}{v_{f}} \label{eq:3.10} \end{equation}\]

Where:

\({v}_{f}\) = velocity in the pore fluid

\({v}_{m}\) = velocity in the rock matrix

\(\phi\) = porosity

v = seismic wave propagation velocity in the rock

In sedimentary rocks, the grain bonding strength results from compaction over millions of years. This suggests that the age and depth of the formation can be used to correlate this strength and, ultimately, the seismic wave velocity. However, it is necessary to know the formation's burial history.

Gassmann Equation.

Gassmann (1951) sought to develop a formula that, based on the physical properties of rocks and fluids, could predict changes in seismic wave velocity caused by fluid saturation changes. Several assumptions are involved in deriving and applying Gassmann's equation, the main ones being:

  • The porous material is isotropic, elastic, composed of a single mineral, and homogeneous.

  • The porous space is connected and in pressure equilibrium.

  • The medium is a closed system, meaning no movement of the fluid through its boundaries.

  • There is no chemical interaction between the fluids and the rock matrix (the shear modulus is constant).

One form of Gassmann's equation is presented as:

\[\begin{equation*} K_{S} = K_{d} + \Delta K_{d} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \Delta K_{d} = \frac{K_{0}(1 - \frac{K_{d}}{K_{0}})^{2}}{1 - \phi - \frac{K_{d}}{K_{0}} + \phi \frac{K_{d}}{K_{0}}} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} G_{S} = G_{d} \end{equation*}\]

Where:

\({K}_{0}\) = bulk modulus of the mineral

\({K}_{f}\) = bulk modulus of the fluid

\({K}_{d}\) = bulk modulus of the dry rock

\({K}_{S}\) = bulk modulus of the saturated rock

\({G}_{S}\) = shear modulus of the saturated rock

\({G}_{d}\) = shear modulus of the dry rock

\(\Delta K_{d}\) = increase in bulk modulus resulting from fluid saturation in dry rock

In most practical cases, many of these assumptions are not valid, such as anisotropy. Despite the limitations of Gassmann's equation, it is the most widely used relation for calculating changes in seismic velocity for different fluid saturations in reservoirs. Brown and Korringa (1975) worked on modifying the equation to account for anisotropy.

3.2.3 Mathematical Representation of Reflection

To correctly process seismic signals, it is necessary to understand how they propagate. Consider a geological structure composed of several parallel, homogeneous layers of rock strata. To simplify the discussion, we initially look at the convolutional model for representing the seismic signal \(s(t)\), resulting from the reflection of a wave \(w(t)\) in one of these layers.

\[\begin{equation} s(t) = w(t) * r(t) + n(t) \label{eq:3.11} \end{equation}\]

In this equation, \(w(t)\) is the seismic signal from the source (wavelet), \(r(t)\) is the reflectivity, and \(n(t)\) corresponds to noise. The convolution operation is represented by \(*\). Let's assume for now that \(n(t) = 0\). From the definition of convolution (see Appendix), we have:

\[\begin{equation} s(t) = w(t) * r(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} w(\tau) r(t - \tau) d\tau \label{eq:3.12} \end{equation}\]

The convolution of an impulse by a wave results in a temporal shift and amplitude change, as shown in Figure 8. This is a very convenient property, which is why we seek to represent \(w(t) \text{ and } r(t)\) in a form suitable for convolution.

Seismic waves have various shapes, as illustrated in the figure below. In the mathematical literature, they are referred to as wavelets (Figure 9).

There are various ways to mathematically represent a wavelet. Ricker derived the expression below from the Gaussian function:

\[\begin{equation} w(t) = \left(1 - 2\pi^{2} f^{2} t^{2}\right) e^{-\pi^{2} f^{2} t^{2}} \label{eq:3.13} \end{equation}\]

Its shape can be modified by changing the frequency \(f\), as shown in Figure 10, where the corresponding Gaussian function is shown as a dashed line.

The fraction of energy reflected at the interface between surfaces of different lithologies is given by the reflection coefficient, which can be estimated from the acoustic impedance defined as \(v \rho\), where v is the velocity of the P-wave and \(\rho\) is the specific mass of the soil. For the simplified case of two lithologies in contact along a flat surface, the reflection coefficient is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} R = \frac{v_2{\rho }_2 - v_1{\rho }_1}{v_2{\rho }_2 + v_1{\rho }_1} \label{eq:3.14} \end{equation}\]

This coefficient relates the amount (power) of reflected energy to the emitted energy. Note that the form in which the above equation was written restricts the reflectivity values to the range \(-1 < R < 1\).

Not all of the wave energy is reflected; part of it is transmitted. The transmission coefficient indicates what fraction of the wave is transmitted.

\[\begin{equation} T = 1 - R \label{eq:3.15} \end{equation}\]

Figura 10 Wavelet de Ricker

To relate the fraction of reflected energy with the depth (\({z}_{k}\)) of the interface, reflectivity is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} r(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} R_{k} \delta (z+z_{k}) \label{eq:3.16} \end{equation}\]

Where \(\delta (z+z_{k})\) is the Dirac delta function, and \({R}_{k}\) corresponds to the reflection coefficient of the k-th interface. Reflectivity measures the relative contrast between impedances before and after \({z}_{k}\).

Reflectivity is represented in depth and must be converted to time representation to be used in the convolution equation above. For the vertical path, the double travel time of the reflection at the first reflector will be:

\[\begin{equation} t_1 = 2 \frac{z_1}{v_1} \label{eq:3.17} \end{equation}\]

Generalizing for k layers, we have:

\[\begin{equation} t_k = 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\Delta z_{k}}{v(z_{k})} \label{eq:3.18} \end{equation}\]

Thus, reflectivity can be rewritten as a function of time:

\[\begin{equation} r(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} R_{k} \delta \left(t + t_{k}\right) \label{eq:3.19} \end{equation}\]

Finally, the convolution equation for the seismic signal response becomes:

\[\begin{equation} s(t) = w(t) * \sum_{k=1}^{n} R_{k} \delta \left(t + t_{k}\right) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} w(t) * R_{k} \delta \left(t + t_{k}\right) \label{eq:3.20} \end{equation}\]

3.2.4 Reflection Time in Single-Layer Soil

After the signal is emitted by the source, all receivers will receive part of the energy propagated by these rays according to their reflections. So far, we have seen the mathematical modeling to describe vertical reflections. However, in seismic work, receivers receive signals reflected at different angles.

It also happens that the same depth is recorded multiple times by different receivers. This multiplicity of records of the same interface is called the common depth point (CDP). When the source and reflector are symmetrically arranged around the point, the seismic processing is called the common mid-point (CMP). This is because the position on the x-axis of the arrival of the reflected signal will be halfway between the source and the receiver, as shown in Figure 18.

In Figure 19(a) below, we see an example of CMP in seismic work carried out at sea. In red, we see ray paths reflecting at the same point. In this case, the CDP is identical to the CMP. However, if the reflector is not horizontal, the CMP will not be the same as the CDP, as we can see in Figure 19(b).

Despite the difference between CMP and CDP, the processing technique for these signals is generally referred to as CDP. By averaging the records, this technique allows for noise filtering, as they are random. Additionally, CDP processing of this group of signals allows continuous coverage of interfaces even when there are obstacles.

Here, it is also necessary to correct these signals to align them according to the depth of the reflecting surface.

Travel Time. The travel time for a seismic wave to reflect off an interface at depth \(z\) and return to the surface can be determined from the propagation velocity. For vertical reflection, the double travel time of the seismic signal is:

\[\begin{equation} t_{0} = 2 \frac{x}{v} \label{eq:3.26} \end{equation}\]

Considering that the source is positioned at the origin of the xz-axis and the receiver is positioned at z=0 and at any point \(x\), we can use Pythagoras' theorem to obtain:

\[\begin{equation} t(x) = \frac{\sqrt{\left( \frac{x}{2} - 0 \right)^{2} + z^{2}}}{v} + \frac{\sqrt{\left( x - \frac{x}{2} \right)^{2} + z^{2}}}{v} \label{eq:3.27} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} t(x) = \frac{\sqrt{x^{2} + 4z^{2}}}{v} \label{eq:3.28} \end{equation}\]

This equation shows that the reflection of flat surfaces will appear as a hyperbola, as seen in the figures above. Therefore, by estimating v in the different layers, it is possible to make the necessary correction to the signal. From this equation, it is possible to determine the reflection time of CMP or CDP signals.

Snell's Law. So far, our discussion has focused on the reflection process. However, refraction also needs to be considered. It corresponds to the change in direction of waves as they pass from one medium to another, as shown in Figure 22(a) below. In Figure 22(b), we see a representation of the seismic ray path in a medium with three layers. Note that there is a relationship between the angle of refraction and the difference in velocity between the media.

Based on Fermat's principle, which states that the path of a ray will be the one with the shortest travel time, it is possible to define the relationship between the angles formed by the rays and their velocities. This relationship is known as Snell's law.

Snell's Law for the Reflected Ray. Consider a ray with velocity \(v_1\) that departs from position \(A = (a_1, a_2)\), strikes point \(X(x, 0)\), and is reflected to point \(B(b_1, b_2)\), as shown in Figure 22.

Using Pythagoras' theorem, we can determine the times \(t_{AX} \text{ and } t_{XB}\):

\[\begin{equation} t_{AX}(x) = \frac{\sqrt{(x - a_1)^{2} + a_{2}^{2}}}{v_1} \label{eq:3.29} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} t_{XB}(x) = \frac{\sqrt{(b_1 - x)^{2} + b_{2}^{2}}}{v_1} \label{eq:3.30} \end{equation}\]

The total time \(t(x)\) is:

\[\begin{equation} t(x) = t_{AX}(x) + t_{XB}(x) = \frac{\sqrt{(x - a_1)^{2} + a_{2}^{2}}}{v_1} + \frac{\sqrt{(b_1 - x)^{2} + b_{2}^{2}}}{v_1} \label{eq:3.31} \end{equation}\]

The point where reflection occurs, called the critical point \(x_c\), is the one that minimizes the travel time. To determine this point, we first need to calculate the derivative of the time with respect to \(x\):

\[\begin{equation} \frac{dt(x)}{dx} = \frac{(x - a_1)}{v_1 \sqrt{(x - a_1)^{2} + a_2^{2}}} - \frac{(b_1 - x)}{v_1 \sqrt{(b_1 - x)^{2} + b_2^{2}}} \label{eq:3.32} \end{equation}\]

Setting the derivative to zero, we obtain an expression for the critical point \(x_c\):

\[\begin{equation} \frac{(x_c - a_1)}{v_1 \sqrt{(x_c - a_1)^{2} + a_2^{2}}} = \frac{(b_1 - x_c)}{v_1 \sqrt{(b_1 - x_c)^{2} + b_2^{2}}} \label{eq:3.33} \end{equation}\]

Observing the figure above, it follows that:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\sin(\theta_1)}{v_1} = \frac{\sin(\theta_2)}{v_1} \label{eq:3.34} \end{equation}\]

In this case, since the velocities are identical, the angles will also be equal.

Snell's Law for the Transmitted (Refracted) Ray. Consider a ray that departs from \(A = (a_1, a_2)\), strikes point \(X(x, 0)\), and is transmitted to \(C(c_1, c_2)\), as shown in Figure 24.

The times \(t_{AX} \text{ and } t_{XC}\) are:

\[\begin{equation} t_{AX}(x) = \frac{\sqrt{(x - a_1)^{2} + a_2^{2}}}{v_1} \label{eq:3.35} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} t_{XC}(x) = \frac{\sqrt{(c_1 - x)^{2} + c_2^{2}}}{v_2} \label{eq:3.36} \end{equation}\]

The total time is:

\[\begin{equation} t(x) = t_{AX}(x) + t_{XC}(x) = \frac{\sqrt{(x - a_1)^{2} + a_2^{2}}}{v_1} + \frac{\sqrt{(c_1 - x)^{2} + c_2^{2}}}{v_2} \label{eq:3.37} \end{equation}\]

Differentiating with respect to \(x\), we obtain:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{dt}{dx} = \frac{(x - a_1)}{v_1 \sqrt{(x - a_1)^{2} + a_2^{2}}} - \frac{(c_1 - x)}{v_2 \sqrt{(c_1 - x)^{2} + c_2^{2}}} \label{eq:3.38} \end{equation}\]

Setting the derivative to zero, we obtain the expression for the critical point \(x_c\):

\[\begin{equation} \frac{(x_c - a_1)}{v_1 \sqrt{(x_c - a_1)^{2} + a_2^{2}}} = \frac{(c_1 - x_c)}{v_2 \sqrt{(c_1 - x_c)^{2} + c_2^{2}}} \label{eq:3.39} \end{equation}\]

Observing the figure above, it follows that:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\sin(\theta_1)}{v_1} = \frac{\sin(\theta_2)}{v_2} \label{eq:3.40} \end{equation}\]

3.2.5 Reflection Time in Multi-Layered Soil

The subsurface is composed of various layers, for which there are formulations for calculating the transit time adapted to different geological models. For this, it is necessary to determine formulas that relate the reflection transit times to the distance from the sources to the receivers. Next, we will analyze a medium with different homogeneous horizontal layers, each with its own velocity, as shown in Figure 25.

Starting from Snell's law:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\sin(\theta_1)}{v_1} = \frac{\sin(\theta_2)}{v_2} = ... = \frac{\sin(\theta_n)}{v_n} = p \label{eq:3.41} \end{equation}\]

The equalities allow us to define the constant \(p\), from which the following relation is deduced:

\[\begin{equation} \sin(\theta_k) = v_k pb\label{eq:3.42} \end{equation}\]

The length of each ray trajectory can then be defined as:

\[\begin{equation} \ell_k = \frac{\Delta z_k}{\cos(\theta_k)} = \frac{\Delta z_k}{\sqrt{1 - \sin^2(\theta_k)}} = \frac{\Delta z_k}{\sqrt{1 - (v_k p)^2}} \label{eq:3.43} \end{equation}\]

The total time \(t_T\) will be:

\[\begin{equation} t_T = \frac{2\ell_1}{v_1} + \frac{2\ell_2}{v_2} + ... + \frac{2\ell_n}{v_n} \label{eq:3.44} \end{equation}\]

Thus, the total time as a function of parameter \(p\) will be:

\[\begin{equation} t_T(p) = \frac{2\Delta z_1}{v_1 \sqrt{1 - (v_1 p)^2}} + \frac{2\Delta z_2}{v_2 \sqrt{1 - (v_2 p)^2}} + ... + \frac{2\Delta z_n}{v_n \sqrt{1 - (v_n p)^2}} \label{eq:3.45} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} t_T(p) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{2\Delta z_k}{v_k \sqrt{1 - (v_k p)^2}} \label{eq:3.46} \end{equation}\]

For vertical reflection \(p = 0\), the total time \(t_0\) will be:

\[\begin{equation} t_T(0) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{2\Delta z_k}{v_k} = t_0 \label{eq:3.47} \end{equation}\]

Thus, the time for vertical reflection to travel through each layer \(t_{0,k}\) will be:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta t_k(0) = \frac{2\Delta z_k}{v_k} = t_{0,k} \label{eq:3.48} \end{equation}\]

The half-offset \(x\) between the source and the receiver can be expressed as:

\[\begin{equation} x = \ell_1 \sin(\theta_1) + \ell_2 \sin(\theta_2) + ... + \ell_n \sin(\theta_n) \label{eq:3.49} \end{equation}\]

From equations \eqref{eq:3.42} and \eqref{eq:3.43}, this can be rewritten as a function of \(p\):

\[\begin{equation} x(p) = p \frac{v_1 \Delta z_1}{\sqrt{1 - (v_1 p)^2}} + \frac{v_2 \Delta z_2}{\sqrt{1 - (v_2 p)^2}} + ... + \frac{v_n \Delta z_n}{\sqrt{1 - (v_n p)^2}} \label{eq:3.50} \end{equation}\]

The above equation is implicit in \(p\). Defining:

\[\begin{equation} q(p) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{v_k \Delta z_k}{\sqrt{1 - (v_k p)^2}} \label{eq:3.51} \end{equation}\]

Then:

\[\begin{equation} x(p) = p q(p) \label{eq:3.52} \end{equation}\]

As with the derivation of Snell's law, we also need to find the relationship between the transit time \(t_T\) and the offset \(x\). Since the above equation is not explicit in \(p\), we can define a composed relation of the ray transit time of the form:

\[\begin{equation} t(x) = t_T(p(x)) \label{eq:3.53} \end{equation}\]

We can use the Taylor approximation to obtain an expression for the transit time as a function of the offset \(x\). Considering the Taylor series up to the second-order term, we have a parabolic approximation for the transit time:

\[\begin{equation} t_{parabolic}(x) = t(x_0) + \left. \frac{dt}{dx} \right|_{x=x_0} (x - x_0) + \frac{1}{2} \left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=x_0} (x - x_0)^2 \label{eq:3.54} \end{equation}\]

The approximation around \(x_0 = 0\) leads to:

\[\begin{equation} t_{parabolic}(x) = t(0) + \left. \frac{dt}{dx} \right|_{x=0} x + \frac{1}{2} \left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=0} x^2 \label{eq:3.55} \end{equation}\]

We see that when the offset is zero, \(x = 0\) and \(p = 0\), so we define \(t_0\) as follows:

\[\begin{equation} t(0) = t_T(p(0)) = t_T(0) = t_0 \label{eq:3.56} \end{equation}\]

Thus, the equation can be rewritten as:

\[\begin{equation} t_{parabolic}(x) = t_0 + \left. \frac{dt}{dx} \right|_{x=0} x + \frac{1}{2} \left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=0} x^2 \label{eq:3.57} \end{equation}\]

To determine \(\left. \frac{dt}{dx} \right|_{x=0}\), we apply the chain rule to obtain:

\[\begin{equation} \left. \frac{dt}{dx} \right|_{x=0} = \left. \frac{dt_T}{dp} \right|_{p=0} \left. \frac{dp}{dx} \right|_{x=0} \label{eq:3.58} \end{equation}\]

Since \(x = 0 \text{ implies } p = 0\), the derivative \(\frac{dt_T}{dp}\) is obtained from equation \eqref{eq:3.57}:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{dt_T}{dp} = 2p \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{v_k \Delta z_k}{\left[1 - (v_k p)^2\right]^{3/2}} \label{eq:3.59} \end{equation}\]

At \(p = 0\), the value is zero:

\[\left. \frac{dt_T}{dp} \right|_{p=0} = 0\]

Thus:

\[\left. \frac{dt}{dx} \right|_{x=0} = \left. \frac{dt_T}{dp} \right|_{p=0} \left. \frac{dp}{dx} \right|_{x=0} = 0\]

The parabolic time equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} t_{parabolic}(x) = t_0 + \frac{1}{2} \left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=0} x^2 \label{eq:3.60} \end{equation}\]

Now, we need to determine \(\left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=0}\). Applying the chain rule, we obtain:

\[\begin{equation} \left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=0} = \left. \frac{d^2 t_T}{dp^2} \right|_{p=0} \left[\left( \left. \frac{dp}{dx} \right|_{x=0} \right)^2 + \frac{dt_T}{dp} \right|_{p=0} \frac{d^2p}{dx^2} \bigg|_{x=0}\bigg] \label{eq:3.61} \end{equation}\]

How:

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{dt_T}{dp} \bigg|_{p=0} = 0 \end{equation*}\]

The equation \eqref{eq:3.61} then becomes:

\[\begin{equation} \left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=0} = \left. \frac{d^2 t_T}{dp^2} \right|_{p=0}\bigg[\left( \left. \frac{dp}{dx} \right|_{x=0} \right)^2\bigg] \label{eq:3.62} \end{equation}\]

From equation \eqref{eq:3.59}, we deduce:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{d^2 t_T}{dp^2} = 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{v_k \Delta z_k}{\left[1 - (v_k p)^2\right]^{3/2}} + 6p \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{v_k^3 \Delta z_k}{\left[1 - (v_k p)^2\right]^{5/2}} \label{eq:3.63} \end{equation}\]

At \(p = 0\), we have:

\[\begin{equation} \left. \frac{d^2 t_T}{dp^2} \right|_{p=0} = 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n} v_k \Delta z_k \label{eq:3.64} \end{equation}\]

Defining:

\[\begin{equation} q_0 = q(0) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} v_k \Delta z_k \label{eq:3.65} \end{equation}\]

Then:

\[\begin{equation} \left. \frac{d^2 t_T}{dp^2} \right|_{p=0} = 2q_0 \label{eq:3.66} \end{equation}\]

Now we only need to determine \(\frac{dp}{dx}\). Although there is no explicit relation between \(p \text{ and } x\), applying the chain rule to \(x(p) = p q(p)\) (equation \eqref{eq:3.52} ) gives an implicit relation:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{dx}{dp} = p \frac{dq}{dp} + q \label{eq:3.67} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \frac{dp}{dx} = \frac{1}{q + p \frac{dq}{dp}} \label{eq:3.68} \end{equation}\]

For \(x = 0\), this results in:

\[\begin{equation} \left. \frac{dp}{dx} \right|_{x=0} = \frac{1}{q(0) + p(0) \left. \frac{dq}{dp} \right|_{x=0}} = \frac{1}{q_0} \label{eq:3.69} \end{equation}\]

Substituting into equation \eqref{eq:3.62}, we have:

\[\begin{equation} \left. \frac{d^2 t}{dx^2} \right|_{x=0} = 2 \frac{1}{q_0} \label{eq:3.70} \end{equation}\]

Finally, substituting into equation \eqref{eq:3.60}, we get:

\[\begin{equation} t_{parabolic}(x) = t_0 + \frac{x^2}{q_0} \label{eq:3.71} \end{equation}\]

Similar arguments (Portugal 2012) are used to derive the hyperbolic approximation, whose final expression is:

\[\begin{equation} t_{hyperbolic}(x) = \sqrt{t_0^2 + \frac{4x^2}{\overline{v^2}}} \label{eq:3.72} \end{equation}\]

Where \(\overline{v^2}\) is the weighted average of the square of the velocity, often referred to as RMS (root mean square).

\[\begin{equation} \overline{v^2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n} \Delta t_{0,k} v_k^2}{\sum_{k=1}^{n} \Delta t_{0,k}}} \label{eq:3.73} \end{equation}\]

3.2.6 Fourier Series.

In a simplified way, the representation of a mass-spring system simulates the behavior of solid particles when subjected to a seismic pulse. Consider a mass attached to a spring as shown in the figure below. Also, consider that after being pulled to a position \(z_0\), the mass is released to oscillate (Figure 26).

According to Newton's second law F = ma, we have:

\[\begin{equation*} m \frac{d^2 \ell}{dt^2} = mg - K \ell \end{equation*}\]

At rest, \(mg = K\ell_0\):

\[\begin{equation*} m \frac{d^2 \ell}{dt^2} = K\ell_0 - K\ell \end{equation*}\]

Letting \(z = \ell - \ell_0\), then:

\[\begin{equation} m \frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} + Kz = 0 \label{eq:3.74} \end{equation}\]

The above equation is called homogeneous since its right-hand side is zero. By applying the Laplace transform2 we obtain the following characteristic equation:

\[\begin{equation*} ms^2 + K = 0 \end{equation*}\]

Where \(s\) is the Laplace variable. The roots of this equation are:

\[\begin{equation*} s_{1,2} = \pm i \sqrt{\frac{m}{K}} = \pm i\omega_0 \end{equation*}\]

Where \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{K}{m}}\) is called the natural frequency of the undamped system. The solution to the above equation is obtained by the sum of the exponentials:

\[\begin{equation*} z_h(t) = A_1 e^{s_1 t} + A_2 e^{s_2 t} \end{equation*}\]

Or equivalently: \[\begin{equation*} z_h(t) = A_1 e^{i\omega_0 t} + A_2 e^{-i\omega_0 t} \end{equation*}\]

Where \(A_1 \text{ and } A_2\) are constants, and the subscript \(h\) indicates that this is the solution to a homogeneous differential equation. Assuming the initial conditions (at \(t = 0\)) that the mass is at a position \(z_0\) away from equilibrium, and that the velocity is \(v_0\), then:

\[\begin{equation*} z_h(0) = z_0 \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \left. \frac{dz_h}{dt} \right|_{t=0} = v_0 \end{equation*}\]

Applying these conditions to the equation for \(z_h(t)\), we obtain:

\[\begin{equation*} A_1 + A_2 = z_0 \end{equation*}\] \[\begin{equation*} A_1 - A_2 = \frac{v_0}{i\omega_0} \end{equation*}\]

From this, we conclude: \[\begin{equation*} A_1 = \frac{z_0}{2} + \frac{v_0}{i\omega_0}, \quad A_2 = \frac{z_0}{2} - \frac{v_0}{i\omega_0} \end{equation*}\]

Substituting into \(z_h(t)\):

\[\begin{equation*} z_h(t) = \left( \frac{z_0}{2} + \frac{v_0}{i\omega_0} \right) e^{i\omega_0 t} + \left( \frac{z_0}{2} - \frac{v_0}{i\omega_0} \right) e^{-i\omega_0 t} \end{equation*}\] \[\begin{equation*} z_h(t) = \frac{z_0}{2} \left( e^{i\omega_0 t} - e^{-i\omega_0 t} \right) + \frac{v_0}{i\omega_0} \left( e^{i\omega_0 t} - e^{-i\omega_0 t} \right) \end{equation*}\]

Using Euler's relation \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\), we convert the exponentials into cosines:

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = z_0 \cos(\omega_0 t) + \frac{v_0}{\omega_0} \sin(\omega_0 t) \label{eq:3.75} \end{equation}\]

Which can be rewritten using the relationship between sines and cosines:

\[\begin{equation} a \cos(\omega_0 t) + b \sin(\omega_0 t) = c \cos(\omega_0 t + \theta) \label{eq:3.76} \end{equation}\]

Where: \[\begin{equation*} \tan\theta = -\frac{b}{a} \end{equation*}\] And: \[\begin{equation*} c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \end{equation*}\]

Thus, the system's response will be:

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = c \cos(\omega_0 t + \theta) \label{eq:3.77} \end{equation}\]

Where: \[\begin{equation*} \tan\theta = -\frac{v_0}{\omega_0z_0} \end{equation*}\] And: \[\begin{equation*} c = \sqrt{z_0^2 + \bigg(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\bigg)^2} \end{equation*}\]

We see that the response of the system without perturbation (homogeneous) and without damping is a periodic function called harmonic oscillation. Since it does not have damping, the system will oscillate indefinitely, which is an idealization.

If we redo the exercise above with an initial velocity of zero, \(v_0 = 0\), the response will be:

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = \frac{z_0}{2} \left( e^{i\omega_0 t} + e^{-i\omega_0 t} \right) = z_0 \cos(\omega_0 t) \label{eq:3.78} \end{equation}\]

When \(v_0\) is different from zero, the values of \(\theta \text{ (phase) and } c\) (amplitude) will change, altering the shape of the response as shown in Figure 27.

Note that the responses are out of phase with each other.

In practical terms, we must consider that all systems have damping. Now consider the mass attached to a plate of negligible mass, immersed in a fluid that only resists motion, as shown in Figure 26 (b). In this case, the force balance leads to:

\[\begin{equation} m\frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} + C\frac{dz}{dt} + Kz = 0 \label{eq:3.79} \end{equation}\]

Where \(C\) is called the damping coefficient. By applying the Laplace transform, we obtain the following characteristic equation: \[\begin{equation*} ms^2 + Cs + K = 0 \end{equation*}\] The roots of this equation are: \[\begin{equation*} s_{1,2} = -\frac{C}{2m} \pm \frac{1}{2m} \sqrt{C^2 - 4mK} = -\frac{C}{2m} \pm \sqrt{\frac{C^2}{4m^2} - \frac{K}{m}} \end{equation*}\]

Note that depending on the values of \(m, C, \text{ and } K\), we will have three types of responses: identical real roots, different real roots, and complex roots. We will have identical roots when:

\[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{\frac{C^2}{4m^2} - \frac{K}{m}} = 0 \end{equation*}\]

This condition is known as "critical damping," where:

\[\begin{equation*} C_{\text{critical}} = 2\sqrt{Km} \end{equation*}\]

The relationship between the system's damping \(C\) and the critical damping \(C_{\text{critical}}\) is called the damping factor \(\xi\):

\[\begin{equation} \xi = \frac{C}{C_{\text{critical}}} = \frac{C}{2\sqrt{Km}} \label{eq:3.80} \end{equation}\]

It is a measure of the degree of damping in a system. Both the characteristic equation and its roots can be rewritten in terms of \(\xi\) and \(\omega_0\) (the natural frequency of the undamped system):

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{m}{K} s^2 + \frac{C}{K} s + 1 = 0 \end{equation*}\]

Based on the definitions of \(\xi = \frac{C}{2\sqrt{Km}}\) and \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{K}{m}}\), we have:

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{\omega_0^2} s^2 + \frac{2\xi}{\omega_0} s + 1 = 0 \end{equation*}\] The roots are: \[\begin{equation*} s_{1,2} = -\xi \omega_0 \pm \omega_0 \sqrt{\xi^2 - 1} \end{equation*}\]

We have three distinct situations: identical real roots \(\xi = 1\), different real roots \(\xi > 1\), and complex conjugate roots \(\xi < 1\).

For \(\xi = 1\):

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = (A_1 + A_2 t) e^{-\omega_0 t} \label{eq:3.81} \end{equation}\]

For \(\xi > 1\):

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = e^{-\xi \omega_0 t} \left( A_1 e^{\omega_0 \sqrt{\xi^2 - 1} t} + A_2 e^{-\omega_0 \sqrt{\xi^2 - 1} t} \right) \label{eq:3.82} \end{equation}\]

For \(\xi < 1\):

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = e^{-\xi \omega_0 t} \left( A_1 e^{i\omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \xi^2} t} + A_2 e^{-i\omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \xi^2} t} \right) \label{eq:3.83} \end{equation}\]

Where \(\omega_0 \sqrt{\xi^2 - 1} = i \omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \xi^2}\).

We conclude that a system with \(\xi > 1\) is over-damped and will have a non-oscillatory response. A system with \(\xi < 1\) has little damping (is under-damped) and will have an oscillatory response. When \(\xi = 1\), we are at the boundary between these two situations, known as critical damping.

The case where \(\xi < 1\) is of most practical interest and will be examined in greater detail. As in the undamped system, where \(\omega_0\) was defined as the natural frequency, it is intuitive to define the natural frequency of the damped system as:

\[\begin{equation} \omega_d = \omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \xi^2} \label{eq:3.84} \end{equation}\]

Note that if there is no damping (C= \(0\) ), then \(\xi = 0 \text{ and } \omega_d = \omega_0\). We can then rewrite the equation as:

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = e^{-\xi \omega_0 t} \left( A_1 e^{i \omega_d t} + A_2 e^{-i \omega_d t} \right) \label{eq:3.85} \end{equation}\]

The combination of exponentials \(e^{-\xi \omega_0 t + \omega_d t}\) and \(e^{-\xi \omega_0 t - \omega_d t}\) can be converted into sines and cosines using Euler's relation:

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = e^{-\xi \omega_0 t} \left( a \cos(\omega_d t) + b \sin(\omega_d t) \right) \label{eq:3.86} \end{equation}\]

The constants can be determined from the initial conditions of the problem, similarly to the undamped case. In this case, too, the homogeneous solution is obtained using the relation between cosines and sines:

\[\begin{equation} z_h(t) = c e^{-\xi \omega_0 t} \cos(\omega_d t + \theta) \label{eq:3.87} \end{equation}\]

Equation \eqref{eq:3.87} indicates that the response corresponds to a cosine oscillation \(\cos(\omega_d t + \theta)\), phase-shifted by \(\theta\) degrees, which decays \(\left( e^{-\xi \omega_0 t} \right)\) to zero over time, i.e., a transient response, as shown in Figure 28. Note that \(\xi\omega_d\) will always be positive since C, K, and m are positive quantities.

Comparing the roots of the characteristic equations of the problems with and without damping, we see that as \(C\) decreases, the frequency \(\omega\) tends to the natural frequency of the system \(\omega_0\).

Example. Consider a mass-spring system with \(m = 10 \text{kg, } K = 1.000 \, {kg/s}^2\). Determine its response after pulling the weight to 5 meters below its equilibrium position (\(z_0 = 5 m\)) and with \(v_0 = 0\). Consider three different situations:

\(C = 0\)

\(C = 100\)

\(C = 300\)

Solution: Since \(v_0 = 0, \theta = 0\).

For \(C = 0,\omega_0 = 10 \, \text{rad/s, }\xi = 0, z_k(t) = 5 \cos(10t)\)

For \(C = 100,\omega_d = 8,66 \, \text{rad/s, } \xi = 0,5, z_h(t) = e^{-4,33t} 5 \cos(8,66t)\)

The cases above correspond to free motion, without any external force acting on the body. To associate this problem with a practical case, such as a ship subjected to ocean waves, we can initially assume that these waves are represented by a sinusoidal wave of the type \(F \cos(\omega t)\). In this way, the differential equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} m \frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} + C \frac{dz}{dt} + Kz = F \cos(\omega t) \label{eq:3.88} \end{equation}\]

The solution to the differential equation is given by the sum of the homogeneous solution \(z_h(t)\) and a particular solution \(z_p(t)\). We already have the homogeneous solution, now we need a particular solution. Since it is intuitive to believe that the system will respond by oscillating at the same frequency as the perturbation, a particular solution would be:

\[\begin{equation} z_p(t) = c \cos(\omega t + \theta) \label{eq:3.89} \end{equation}\]

Substituting into the differential equation, we verify that this is indeed a particular solution, and we can determine \(c \text{ and } \theta\) as follows:

\[\begin{equation*} c = \frac{F}{\sqrt{m^2 (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \omega^2 C^2}} \end{equation*}\] and \[\begin{equation*} \tan \theta = \frac{\omega C}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)} \end{equation*}\]

Finally, the solution for the system is:

\[\begin{equation} z(t) = z_h(t) + z_p(t) = e^{-\alpha t} \left(a \cos(\omega t) + b \sin(\omega t)\right) + c \cos(\omega t + \theta) \label{eq:3.90} \end{equation}\]

Since the first term on the right tends to zero over time, after this transient period the system will oscillate continuously as \(c \cos(\omega t + \theta)\). This is called the "steady-state response," which is of interest in many practical cases. The constants \(c\) and \(\theta\) can be determined from \(F, m,\omega_0 \text{, and } C\).

We conclude that an underdamped system (\(\xi < 1\)) subjected to a perturbation \(F \cos(\omega t)\) will have a steady-state response:

\[\begin{equation} z(t) = c \cos(\omega t + \theta) \label{eq:3.91} \end{equation}\]

represented by Figure 30.

Fourier Series

The Ricker wavelet is a very simplified representation of seismic waves. These waves undergo several distortions during their propagation. To determine the position of reflectors, there are mathematical tools based on the theory of wave propagation in solid media that analyze field data very conveniently. These are the Fourier series.

The representation of the perturbation as a sinusoidal wave is an idealization. In practice, periodic oscillations may appear as in Figure 31.

Jean Baptiste Fourier showed that it is possible to represent periodic functions by combining sinusoidal functions3 (\(\cos(\omega t)\) and \(\sin(\omega t)\)) called harmonic oscillations with frequencies equal to integer multiples of the frequency \(\omega_0\).

\[\begin{equation} f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( a_n \cos(nt) + b_n \sin(nt) \right) \label{eq:3.92} \end{equation}\]

This is the additive property of linear systems, where the response to a combination of sinusoidal perturbations is the sum of the individual responses.

Therefore, if the perturbation is not a cosine or sine function but a periodic function with a fundamental frequency \(\omega_0\), the system's response will be the sum of the individual responses. This property of Fourier series allows various engineering problems to be solved.

In the limit, when the number of sine waves and their frequencies approach infinity, the lateral peaks move to infinity, and only one peak remains at t = 0. 3.2.7 Seismic Signal Processing

So far, we have shown some examples of reflections to provide an introduction to seismology. As presented, we see that the raw data obtained from seismic surveys require various corrections to ensure the proper representation of the subsurface. In practice, there are faults, inclined surfaces, noise, etc., that affect the seismic signal and require processing for correct subsurface representation. Seismic signal processing can be divided into several stages.

Preprocessing. This step corrects distortions caused by recording equipment, the difference in relative position (elevation) between the emitter and receiver, etc.

Amplitude Correction. This corrects the energy dissipation caused by the spherical spreading of the signal and inelastic processes such as internal friction and fluid movement in the pores of rocks, which result in the absorption of the higher-frequency components of the signal. Practical experience shows that just 3 seconds of travel time are enough for frequencies above 60 Hz to be filtered by the subsurface.

It is found that the limit of vertical resolution corresponds to a quarter of the dominant wavelength. Since \(v = f \lambda\), we have \(\lambda/4 = v/4f\), where \(\lambda\) is the wavelength, \(v\) is the velocity between the two reflectors, and \(f\) is the dominant frequency. The following relationship between velocity and vertical resolution is obtained:

Table 4

As depth increases, both the propagation velocity increases and frequency decreases, leading to a reduction in resolution. This restriction on higher frequencies imposes limitations on seismic investigations.

It is noted that even reflections of only 6% (or \(R = 0,06\)) can be detected using the seismic reflection method. Therefore, to predict whether two lithologies will generate a detectable reflection, it is necessary to know the propagation velocities of seismic waves and the densities of the rock formations, in addition to having very sensitive receiver instruments. Since the reflected signal is a small fraction of the original wave, it is often contaminated with noise, requiring filtering.

Deconvolution. Deconvolution seeks to increase vertical resolution by compressing the wavelet. Deconvolution provides a quantitative estimate of the distribution of various physical parameters capable of characterizing subsurface geology.

Predictive deconvolution suppresses multiple reflections and, optionally, alters the input data spectrum to increase resolution.

CDP Stacking. Families of seismic traces from common points are grouped, and travel time corrections are made to align these. Subsequently, these traces are summed, and the average is adopted. The traces are then placed side by side to form a seismic section.

3.3 EXPLORATORY PROGRAM

The mapping of sedimentary basins, along with their respective geological ages, is one of the first steps in evaluating the potential for oil accumulation in a region. In Figure 39, we see the main Brazilian sedimentary basins.

In the auction process for an exploratory area, regulatory agencies provide the mapping that may have varying degrees of precision. After acquiring the exploration rights for an area (or block), oil companies establish an exploratory program that consists of defining indirect and direct methods of formation evaluation. The first stage of an exploratory program is conducting a geological study to reconstruct the conditions of hydrocarbon formation and accumulation in the region. In Figure 40, we see an example of the stages followed in field exploration.

Today, almost all sedimentary basins have already been explored in some form. Therefore, modern interpretation work involves correlating the information acquired from the exploratory well logs with the seismic section, topics we will explore later.

3.3.1 Exploratory Wells

During the exploration phase, both stratigraphic wells, aimed at deepening geological and geophysical knowledge, and wildcat wells, aimed at discovering deposits based on the data already collected, are drilled.

When an exploratory well is drilled to obtain information about a new area or a poorly known region, it is unlikely that the well will be used as a production well. It can only be properly constructed when the necessary information for well engineering design, such as pore pressure, fluid presence, permeability, and reservoir rock consolidation, is available. This information is not available before the first well and is called geoengineering data. Therefore, the practice is for this type of well to be of minimum cost, aimed solely at obtaining information, and subsequently abandoned. However, there are cases where the area knowledge is reasonable, and oil companies choose to drill more complex wells capable of being used as producers during the field's productive phase.

The drilling of a well occurs through the rotational action of a drill column with a drill bit. Rock fragments are continuously removed by a drilling fluid called mud. The drilling fluid also serves to cool and lubricate the drill bit, support rock fragments, ensure the stability of the wellbore walls, and more. Steel pipes are used to line the well walls. Drilling along the well's trajectory is affected by the type and thickness of the various rock layers, as shown in Figure 41.

The increase in drilling speed, due to the greater fluidity of hydrocarbon-saturated sedimentary rocks, is one of the indications of the presence of oil. Upon discovering an accumulation, the goal is to estimate its top and base, thus determining the reservoir's height.

Since some reservoirs have a gas cap at the top and many have a water layer at the bottom, the gas-oil and oil-water contacts are often sought as a way to delimit the formation's height.

Some oil reservoirs have enough pressure energy to transport fluids to the surface. This natural flow is observed during drilling by the return of hydrocarbons to the wellbore.

Coring is the process of obtaining a rock sample during drilling. The most common method is hollow coring, where the sample is accommodated inside the drill column and later retrieved by the drilling rig operators.

Cores (Figure 42) are collected and analyzed for density, chemical composition, fossil identification, presence of hydrocarbons, etc.

3.3.2 Well Logging

During the drilling stage of exploratory wells, it is common to lower logging tools to measure various properties of the formation along the wellbore, a process known as well logging 4. This can be done after drilling the well or even during drilling. In the latter case, it is known as logging while drilling. These measurements are based on the electrical, acoustic, and radioactive properties of the rock formations.

The electrical log was the first logging process to provide relevant data for detecting hydrocarbon-bearing formations. It is based on the difference in electrical resistivity of oil, gas, and water. Formations saturated with water, for example, have lower resistivity compared to those with oil or gas since water is a better conductor of electricity. It is observed that the resistivity of a rock fully saturated with water is proportional to the resistivity of the water and inversely proportional to the porosity of the medium. Gus Archie proposed the following equation:

\[\begin{equation} R = \frac{aR_W}{\emptyset^m} \label{eq:3.94} \end{equation}\]

Where:

\(a\) = tortuosity coefficient

\(R_W\) = electrical resistivity of the water

\(\emptyset\) = porosity

\(m\) = cementation exponent

In a hydrocarbon-bearing formation with water, we must consider water saturation, so the equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} R = \frac{aR_W}{\emptyset^m S^n_W} \label{eq:3.95} \end{equation}\]

Where \(n\) = saturation exponent.

The electrical resistivity of pure water is a known value, but since connate water contains salts that reduce resistivity (increase conductivity), a water sample is sought during drilling for experimental determination of this property. However, this is not always possible due to the potential mixing of connate water with drilling fluid. From cores, the coefficients \(a \text{ and }m\) are experimentally determined. In the absence of water samples and cores that can be used for experimental evaluation, typical values can be used.

One of the first methods used for estimating the volume of hydrocarbons in reservoirs was developed by Gus Archie around 1950. This geophysicist correlated data obtained from electrical resistivity logs and the porosity of formations derived from well logging to estimate the volume of hydrocarbons present in the reservoir, according to the equation below.

\[\begin{equation*} N = A \cdot h \cdot {\phi} \cdot (1 - S_{W}) \end{equation*}\]

Where:

\(N\) = total volume of hydrocarbons retained in the formation

\(\phi\) = porosity

\(A\) = area

\(h\) = height of the formation

\(S_{W}\) = water saturation

Example. Consider that, based on seismic, gravimetric, and electromagnetic analyses of an area, geologists indicate an extension of \(2{,}5 \times 10^6 \, \text{m}^2\) with potential for oil accumulation. After drilling a well, the presence of hydrocarbons was confirmed. The electric log data indicate a water saturation of 15%, i.e., \(S_{W} = 0{,}15\), and core evaluation indicates a porosity of 25%. Gamma ray or magnetic potential logs indicate that the height of the formation is 270 m. Determine the volume \(N\) of hydrocarbons present under reservoir conditions.

\[\begin{equation*} N = 2{,}5 \times 10^6 \cdot 270 \cdot 0{,}25 \cdot (1 - 0{,}15) = 8{,}8 \times 10^5 \, \text{m}^3 \end{equation*}\]

The logs do not directly inform variables of interest such as porosity, permeability, or fluid saturation, but they generate a visual image along the well depth, allowing these properties to be inferred by combining different profiles. Below are some of the most common profiles.

Spontaneous Potential - SP. This measures resistivity or resistance to electrical current. It is one of the oldest logs still in use today. Since hydrocarbons are poor conductors of electricity while connate water is a good conductor, this profile helps infer the amount and salinity of the water contained in a rock layer. Note that since freshwater is also a poor conductor, this profile will show high resistivity values, which may lead to incorrect conclusions about the presence of hydrocarbons. This profile is affected by water-based drilling fluids.

Induction - ILD. When clay-based formations are encountered, they swell upon contact with water-based drilling fluids, undesirably reducing the rock's permeability. This issue is addressed by using oil-based, air-based, or foam-based (air and water mixture) drilling fluids. In these cases, magnetic field induction is used because obtaining an electrical resistivity log is compromised, as these fluids are poor electrical conductors.

Gamma Ray - GR. Gamma rays are emitted from the nucleus of unstable atoms. In nature, uranium, thorium, and potassium atoms commonly emit gamma rays. There are different ways to measure this profile. Clay-based formations such as shales have high natural radioactivity due to their high potassium-40 content. Carbonate formations and sandstones have low amounts of potassium isotopes.

Since uranium and thorium may also be present, modern tools can differentiate the radiation emitted by different substances. This profile is used to identify the amount of clay in a rock.

As the densities of formations are proportional to their gamma ray attenuation, a tool with a source and sensor can also determine the formation's density.

Neutron - NPHI. This method is based on the effects that a neutron beam has on hydrogen-containing molecules. Neutrons are uncharged particles about the size of a hydrogen nucleus and can penetrate matter to reach atomic nuclei. The attenuation of these beams is proportional to the number of hydrogen atoms present. Since water contains a relatively large number of hydrogen atoms compared to hydrocarbons, this method infers the water concentration. It is used to estimate porosity, lithology, and the presence of light hydrocarbons or gas.

Sonic - DT. This measures the transit time difference of a mechanical wave through rocks. It is based on the fact that the propagation of these waves varies according to the medium's density. Their velocity is higher in solids than in liquids and gases, respectively. Thus, for two similar rock samples, the wave propagation velocity will be higher in the one containing less gas and more liquid. Since this higher liquid content is related to the amount of water in the rock, this profile can be used to estimate porosity and rock compaction.

Density - RHOB. This profile is based on the reflection of a gamma ray beam to estimate density, porosity, and the presence of light hydrocarbons and gas. Below is an example of logs and how their combination allows the inference of some properties of rock formations near the well.

The profiles show many oscillations, not only due to changes in the formation's properties along its height but also because of the measurement methods' uncertainty. When interpreted based on results obtained from similar formations, these profiles allow only trends to be inferred, rarely providing absolute values for variables such as permeability, porosity, density, etc.

In Figure 43, we see an example where gamma ray and electrical resistivity logs were performed on a formation. In the upper part of the profiles, high electrical resistivity values indicate the presence of hydrocarbons, while low gamma radiation suggests a high-porosity formation, concluding that it is sandstone. In the middle section, the presence of shale is observed, as indicated by high gamma radiation and low electrical resistivity.

In the geophysical logging operation, petrophysical parameters are measured using sophisticated tools known as sondes or logging tools. These sondes carry various types of sensors into the wells, which capture signals during acquisition and transmit them to the surface. These signals are processed and generate geophysical logs, well logs, electrical logs, etc. The generated logs are then interpreted, and petrophysical properties are inferred from the responses of electrical, thermal, acoustic, and other sensors.

Although this method provides valuable information about the subsurface with good accuracy and resolution, it incurs high costs. Consequently, few wells are generally drilled.

3.4 FORMATION EVALUATION

Upon detecting hydrocarbons, it is common to perform a quick formation test (TF) without installing a casing in the well, which is why it is called an open-hole test.

Subsequently, short-term tests may be performed as part of the exploration process, aiming to delimit the reservoir. To declare the field's commercial viability, it is necessary to estimate the volume of recoverable reserves since only a fraction of the total volume of hydrocarbons in the reservoirs can be effectively produced. During this formation evaluation stage, the reservoir is mapped, and simulations are carried out using seismic data and the information obtained from the exploratory wells.

The compilation of available information is interpreted by a team of geologists and engineers in a continuous process of deepening the knowledge of the reservoir and reducing uncertainties. In this regard, oil companies may decide to conduct additional tests on exploratory wells or opt for the drilling of another well.

3.4.1 Well Tests

Tests performed after casing installation are called cased hole formation tests (CHFT). These tests can have various objectives, such as inferring the well's productivity, checking for formation damage, sampling fluids for evaluating oil, gas, and water content, etc.

When tests are conducted solely to gather additional information about a previously discovered reservoir, they are referred to as Reservoir Data Acquisition (RDA) tests.

One of the most important tests is the pressure check before and after the oil (or gas) flow, which serves to infer the reservoir's production potential. Before oil production begins, the pressure across the reservoir is uniform. This pressure is called static pressure \(p_{E}\). After oil flow starts, the pressure at the wellbore stabilizes at a value lower than the static pressure. This pressure is called wellbore pressure \(p_{W}\).

In CHFTs, the well is put into flow in two stages. The first flow is done to clean the well. The downhole pressure, called the first static pressure, is measured. The second flow is longer, and the downhole pressure is measured again, this time called the second static pressure. Since both tests are short, there is no substantial removal of mass from the reservoir. Therefore, unless the reservoir is very small, both the first and second static pressures should be close, corresponding to the reservoir's \(p_{E}\). A second static pressure lower than the first indicates a non-commercial accumulation.

After a commercial declaration is made, long-term tests are conducted to help delimit the field. These tests are short in duration when compared to the typical productive life of a reservoir, which is about 25 years. Significant pressure drops indicate that the reservoir is small or has low permeability. Large reservoirs with high permeability do not experience significant pressure drops during the test, as the volume produced is very small relative to the amount of oil present in the reservoir.

When the test duration is less than 72 hours, Brazilian law permits the burning of oil and gas. Beyond 72 hours, it is considered a long-term test (LTT), with a series of restrictions regarding burning, as we will see in the chapter on Fiscal and Legal Systems later.

3.5 INTERPRETATION

Indirect methods are a first approach in mapping structures with potential for oil accumulation. In geologically favorable areas, geologists propose geological models to explain the process of oil generation, migration, and accumulation in the region.

The integration of seismic results with those obtained from well logs allows for adjustments to the proposed geological model. Seismic data have the advantage of horizontal (area) resolution but are limited in vertical resolution. On the other hand, well logging provides "direct" information with good vertical resolution but limited horizontal coverage. In geological modeling, interpreted information from indirect evaluations and direct well data is used to construct the structural and stratigraphic framework of the reservoir.

Well logs efficiently reproduce correlation patterns between nearby wells and aid in constructing geological maps and defining the geometry of sedimentary bodies and environments.

In geological maps, areas composed solely of igneous and metamorphic rocks (non-sedimentary) are virtually eliminated, as are small basins with very thin sedimentary layers or without structures favorable for accumulation. In the early days of exploration, the focus was almost exclusively on anticlinal structures.

The geological model is continuously improved as more wells are drilled. Since the data are representative of the area where the wells were drilled, they generally do not apply to the entire reservoir, and extrapolation can be a potential source of error.

When no data are available for a given region, experience gained from similar reservoirs discovered in other locations with analogous geology and modes of hydrocarbon occurrence can be used. This is an important aid in generating geological models. Reservoirs can be considered analogous when, for example, they have the same geological formation age and sedimentary basin, similarity in their deposition history (rock type), architectural elements (such as unconformities), or similar rock properties and fluid characteristics. Analytical models are continuously being improved and developed to incorporate more information.

The entire indirect evaluation program (seismic, gravimetric, and magnetic potentials) provides a vast amount of technical information, with relatively small investments compared to the cost of drilling a single exploratory well. They serve as a reference for defining the ideal location for exploratory wells, which is the only certain way to verify the existence of hydrocarbons.

In an exploration campaign, it is essential to understand the production potential of an entire region. Hydrocarbons can be present in liquid or gaseous forms, as gas dissolved in water or coal beds, in shale, or other types of viscous hydrocarbon deposits. Accumulations can vary in number, size, and quality.

Despite increasing computer power and the development of various numerical methods for processing and correcting seismic data, human interpretation is still required. This is the case with 3D visualization. Software is constantly evolving to assist in this visualization, which is becoming more sophisticated and allows for a better understanding of reservoirs.

The decision to drill an exploratory well results from an assessment of the risks involved, especially in offshore environments due to the high costs.

The decision to undertake an exploration campaign also depends on the company's financial health, the contractual conditions of the exploratory block, the distance to supply sources, and future oil price prospects. Concession contracts for exploration rights with short expiration periods or those near other producing blocks may, for example, accelerate the decision to drill wells.

The decision-making process for an exploratory program also considers the probability of success and the commercial viability of the prospect, which depends on associated production costs. Commercial accumulations in onshore fields can, for example, be relatively smaller than those in offshore fields since both exploration and production costs are lower. However, there are exceptions, such as exploration in forest regions where both seismic surveys and the necessary infrastructure for production and oil and gas transportation can be very costly.

In addition to the total volume of hydrocarbons, attention must be paid to the size of the fields that may be discovered. There are cases where regions with the same total amount of hydrocarbons have very different numbers of fields. Greater fragmentation results in higher production costs, affecting the recoverable volume.

Recently, studies for defining areas favorable for oil accumulation have evolved to incorporate probabilistic analyses.

The historical exploration results of a region provide an important statistical basis for future discoveries. Statistical surveys can be conducted for variables such as porosity and oil saturation based on data from producing fields in the same producing region or analogous fields in other locations.

There are statistics for various parameters, but they generally do not apply to height and area. These are more related to the region's geological history. Initially, a probability distribution for height and area is often assumed based on the exploratory well logs and later revised as more wells are drilled and logged.

There are situations where the probabilities are not independent. In some regions, for example, the porosity of thick reservoirs (with great height) tends to be greater than that of thinner reservoirs. This interdependence between variables can be treated probabilistically if we consider dependent events, thus sophisticating the evaluation.

In practice, there is no rule or mathematical formulation that allows us to associate the historical frequencies of each variable with their probability distributions for an exploratory prospect. The determination of probabilities takes on a subjective character, based on the experience of geologists obtained in similar situations.
