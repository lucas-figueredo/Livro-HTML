CHAPTER 4 RESERVOIRS

Upon discovering an oil reservoir, one seeks to predict the volume of oil and gas present in it (volume of oil in place, VOIP), the production system necessary to extract it, the recovery factor (RF), and the prospect's economic viability. The evaluation of the production potential of the field is of utmost importance for decision-making in projects. The knowledge of the reservoir and its production potential advances gradually throughout the productive life of the field. Even for fields already in production, an accurate measurement of the amount of oil contained in them is impossible to perform; we can only estimate it. Understanding how the oil stored in the reservoir can flow to the surface, and what its production mechanisms are, is essential in the design of a production system.

In this and the following chapters, we will introduce the main engineering methods used in the evaluation and conceptualization of reservoirs and the design of these systems. The mass balances are based on the development carried out by Rosa (2007).

4.1 PRODUCTION MECHANISMS

For a reservoir to produce, it is necessary to have some form of "energy" to allow the oil to overcome the resistance posed by the porous channels and move towards the production wells. The flow of oil from the reservoir rock to the production well depends on the physical-chemical characteristics of the fluids (e.g., viscosity), the properties of the reservoir rock (porosity and permeability), and the temperature and pressure conditions of the reservoir.

4.1.1 Primary Recovery. Natural Flow

When the reservoir has the necessary energy for flow, it is referred to as primary energy. The recovery of oil using natural pressures is called Primary Recovery. The natural production mechanisms of reservoirs may occur through aquifer support, dissolved gas, or gas cap.

When the hydrocarbons in a reservoir are in contact with a large water body that drives the oil, the production mechanism is said to be aquifer drive (Figure 1).

This aquifer must be contained in rock whose permeability allows interaction with the oil. As oil is produced, the pressure drop is followed by the migration of water into the oil zone, resulting in more oil production. In designing wells for this type of reservoir, the completion should be done such that the producing zone is located some distance above the aquifer to avoid premature water production instead of oil.

When properly managed, these reservoirs exhibit minimal pressure drop and high recovery factors, typically between 30% and 40%. There are reports of reservoirs in the North Sea with recovery factors as high as 70%.

When the reservoir pressure is below the bubble point pressure of the oil, there is a gas cap within the reservoir. Similar to the aquifer drive mechanism, as the oil is produced, the gas expands and takes up the space previously occupied by the oil. The reservoir pressure drop is minimal as long as the gas is not produced. This effect is more significant the larger the gas cap (Figure 2) compared to the oil volume.

Here too, the well design must ensure that gas is not produced, as doing so would deplete the driving force for oil production. These reservoirs typically have lower recovery factors than those with aquifer drive.

When there is no gas cap or active aquifer in an oil accumulation, the production mechanism is called dissolved gas or solution gas drive (Figure 3). In these reservoirs, the pressure is above the oil's bubble point, so the entire hydrocarbon inventory is in the liquid phase. As oil is produced, the reservoir pressure drops, and consequently, the oil and connate water1 expand while the pores of the rock contract. When the pressure reaches the oil's bubble point, vaporization of the lighter oil components begins, which then expand and displace the liquid out of the porous medium. At this point, the solution gas drive mechanism effectively begins, as the release of this gas helps pressurize the reservoir. If reservoir pressure continues to drop, more gas is generated and produced along with the oil. Gas production is undesirable because it depletes the driving force for maintaining reservoir pressure. The pressure drop accelerates, hindering oil recovery. This type of reservoir typically has a lower recovery factor than those with aquifer or gas cap drive. In these reservoirs, water injection plays a crucial role in maintaining reservoir pressure above the oil saturation pressure, which can significantly increase the recovery factor.

In practice, oil reservoirs exhibit a combination of the mentioned production mechanisms. Oil fields frequently have an aquifer whose action varies depending on the reservoir's permeability, aquifer size, etc. These mechanisms delay the reservoir pressure drop, but they cannot always prevent it, and as oil is produced, field productivity tends to decline.

The average recovery factor of oil fields is around 30%, meaning approximately 70% of the oil remains trapped in the rock at the end of the production period. This is because the oil tends to be partially trapped by capillary forces, which reduce its mobility. These forces are primarily due to the attraction between oil, water, and reservoir rock and increase as rock porosity decreases and oil viscosity increases.

4.1.2 Secondary Recovery. Fluid Injection

To increase a reservoir's recovery factor, supplementary recovery processes are often employed.

Methods that aim to increase the production of trapped oil by sweeping it through fluid injection (immiscible with oil) in the reservoir are known as Secondary Recovery. The efficiency of this sweep depends on both the area reached by the fluid injection and its mobility relative to the oil, connate water saturation, and the rock.

The most common secondary recovery method is water injection. In offshore fields, seawater is used for injection, while in onshore fields, it is very common to reinject water produced from the production wells. Water helps sweep the reservoir, carrying oil towards the production wells. Sweep efficiency refers to the fraction of the reservoir volume invaded by the injected fluid. In this process, the injected water mixes with the connate (original) reservoir water. These are waters with distinct characteristics that can chemically interact due to the reaction between their components. Depending on the type of reservoir rock, there may also be chemical interaction between the injected water and the rock, with processes such as dissolution, re-precipitation, or clay swelling that can affect oil production. Therefore, it is also important to assess the interaction of the injected fluid with the reservoir rock.

A fluid injection project requires knowledge of the reservoir's production mechanism and its geological characteristics. The assumption that the injected fluid will displace the oil in a piston-like movement is an idealization that does not occur in practice, as reservoirs are not geologically homogeneous. Permeability, for example, may have different values depending on the direction taken and will certainly vary throughout the reservoir's volume. Fractures and layers of rocks with different lithologies are also examples of changes that cause heterogeneities in reservoirs.

The injected fluid must displace the oil from the rock formation toward the production well. It is necessary to avoid water preferentially flowing through high-permeability pathways and reaching the production well while leaving oil trapped in the rocks. In reservoirs with more viscous oils, this phenomenon is more critical because water, being less viscous, tends to penetrate faster into high-permeability paths, quickly reaching the production well.

In low permeability reservoirs, water injectivity may not be sufficient to maintain pressure. In such cases, water may be injected at pressures above the reservoir's fracture pressure. This facilitates injectivity but can cause serious issues with exudation and connection to existing fractures, which is why a complete geomechanical study of the reservoir is needed to predict and, if necessary, monitor the extent and direction of fracture propagation.

In onshore fields, it is very common to reinject produced water. As we will see in Chapter 8, onshore processing conditions allow for the treatment of produced water to achieve excellent quality (low oil and solids content). Additionally, legislation in some countries, such as Brazil, does not allow disposal in rivers, making reinjection a priority. An alternative to onshore reinjection is treatment and disposal at sea, but this requires the construction of a submarine outfall and transport of the water to the corresponding terminal. In offshore systems, the water used for injection is seawater, which must be treated to remove solids, marine life residues, etc.

When water injection is carried out in the water zone, that is, in the lower part of the reservoir, it is called bottom injection (Figure 4). Injection can also be performed laterally to the reservoir or directly into the oil zone. When gas is injected for secondary recovery, the injection is performed at the top of the reservoir, known as top injection.

After a few years of production, it is necessary to reperforate the production wells to adapt them to the oil-producing zone, taking into account the displacement of the oil-water contact (Figure 5).

4.1.3 Tertiary Recovery. Enhanced Recovery

Tertiary Recovery, also known as Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR), involves using thermal, chemical, miscible, and microbiological methods.

These methods can reduce water-oil interfacial tension, alter fluid viscosity, or change rock wettability. The sweep efficiency of an injected fluid is related to viscous forces, which depend on fluid viscosity and relative permeabilities of each phase. The displacement efficiency of oil in the swept area is related to capillary forces, which depend on the interfacial tension between oil and water.

Rock-wettability modifying agents aim to increase the hydrophilicity (water affinity) of rock surfaces.

Since the pressures applied to the reservoir are typically much lower than this value (1 to 2 psi/ft), oil droplets of this size will remain trapped, reducing the displacement efficiency of water injection.

The amount of oil that can be displaced in a reservoir is commonly related to the ratio between viscous and capillary forces, a dimensionless group known as the capillary number \(N_c\):

\[\begin{equation} N_c = \frac{\text{viscous forces}}{\text{capillary forces}} = \frac{v \mu_d}{\sigma} \end{equation}\]

where \(v\) is the velocity of the displacing fluid (m/s), \(\mu_d\) is the viscosity of the displacing fluid (N.s/m²), and \(\sigma\) is the interfacial tension (N/m).

Typically, in an oil reservoir, the capillary number is between 10\({}^{-7}\) and 10\({}^{-8}\). For a significant reduction in residual oil saturation to occur, at least a three-order magnitude increase in the capillary number is required (Dabous 1978). In most cases, this implies raising the pressure above the reservoir fracture pressure, making it impractical. An alternative is to adopt enhanced recovery methods.

There are currently many enhanced recovery methods in use and under research. Some notable methods are described below.

Gas Injection. The injection of gases such as natural gas or \({CO}_{2}\) is used to enhance oil recovery by dissolving these fluids in the oil (Figure 8). The solubilization of the injected gas into the oil reduces its viscosity and also its residual saturation, thus improving both sweep and displacement efficiencies. In miscible gas injection processes, an important parameter is the measurement of the Minimum Miscibility Pressure (MMP) in the laboratory, which determines the pressure above which the reservoir must be for the process to be efficient.

The use of water alternating gas injection (WAG) with natural gas is a method where the gas can be miscible or immiscible. In miscible gas injection, such as CO\({}_{2}\) under certain conditions, the gas displaces "new" oil not removed from the pores by water, altering the residual oil saturation. It also helps homogenize the water front, reducing the formation of preferential pathways by the water. Immiscible gas injection works to sweep the reservoir, pushing oil towards the production wells. Since gas has a lower density than oil, it tends to flow through the upper regions of the reservoir. Alternating water injection helps improve the sweep in the lower regions of the reservoir, as water is denser than oil. Water injection also contributes to the material balance of the reservoir when there is insufficient gas for injection, due to the low gas-to-oil ratio (GOR), unavailability of gas in the field region, or economic decisions that favor gas sales over its injection.

Solvent Injection. For heavy oil production, adding solvents (intermediate fractions of oil) can cause a substantial reduction in its viscosity. Gasoline and diesel are solvents that can be used. In some cases, the addition of 50% gasoline to heavy oil results in a 700-fold reduction in viscosity. However, solvent injection into the reservoir is only viable through "huff and puff" processes, injecting small volumes into the production well and then putting it into production. In these cases, the solvent dissolves into the oil around the well, temporarily increasing its productivity.

For all solvent mixing processes, a chemical compatibility study is necessary to assess the risk of precipitation of oil fractions such as asphaltenes.

Viscosified Water. Increasing the viscosity of injection water by adding polymers improves sweep efficiency by altering the mobility ratio between the fluids. The mobility (\(\lambdaup\)) of a fluid is defined as the ratio between its effective permeability (k) in the reservoir rock and its viscosity (\(\muup\)). The oil mobility is given by \(\lambdaup\)\({}_{o}\) = k\({}_{o}\)/\(\muup\)\({}_{o}\), and the water mobility by \(\lambdaup\)\({}_{w}\) = k\({}_{w}\)/\(\muup\)\({}_{w}\). The mobility ratio is defined by the ratio \(\lambdaup\)\({}_{w}\) /\(\lambdaup\)\({}_{o}\). The lower the mobility ratio, the more favorable the oil recovery.

The injection of water-soluble polymer solutions (synthetic or natural) is a way to increase the viscosity of the injection water to bring it closer to the oil's viscosity, resulting in a favorable mobility ratio between the phases, thereby improving sweep efficiency. In this sense, the polymer works by reducing water mobility by increasing the viscosity of the aqueous phase.

Among the various polymers studied, xanthan gum has shown the best results in terms of viscosity, even at low concentrations in aqueous solution. However, it has disadvantages such as high degradability and high cost, which is why it has not been widely used. Partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM) is an excellent alternative for enhanced oil recovery when applied in aqueous solutions of very low salinity due to its low cost, and it has been the most widely used polymer in EOR projects worldwide.

Some polymers can also be used to reduce water production or prevent premature water breakthrough by forming flow barriers when injected into production wells. These barriers can be blocking or permeability-reducing, characterizing blocking or selective systems, respectively. Selective systems primarily block water flow, causing little restriction to hydrocarbon flow. Also known as Relative Permeability Modifiers, these processes employ water-soluble polymers that adsorb onto the rock surface, forming a hydrophilic film that reduces water mobility, with little impact on oil permeability.

Some processes involve the use of polymeric solutions that crosslink over time, sealing high-permeability channels. The chosen polymer is such that, as the temperature of the injected fluid approaches the reservoir temperature, its particles undergo crosslinking bond breaks, absorb water, and increase in volume, forming a barrier to water flow.

Besides logistical and economic considerations, polymer injection projects must account for the fact that polymers will eventually be produced along with the water, impacting the primary oil processing (produced water treatment). The environmental impact of these products in water discharged into the sea should also be considered.

Surfactants. Surfactants are surface-active agents that reduce the interfacial tension between water and oil, promoting oil displacement efficiency and reducing residual oil saturation. The main limitation of this technique lies in the cost of these products, partial adsorption of the surfactant on the reservoir rock surface, and the stability of the surfactant in the presence of saline waters. Surfactants studied in EOR generally have a polar segment (Figure 9) such as carbohydrates, sulfates, sulfonates (anionic), quaternary ammonium salts (cationic), with non-polar hydrocarbon chains and ethoxylated phenols (non-ionic). Of these surfactants, nonylphenol ethoxylate and sodium dodecyl sulfate are the most used.

In addition to reducing interfacial tension, surfactants can interact with the rock (Figure 10). The rock can be preferentially water-wet or oil-wet, depending on the rock type and the oil present.

The wettability of the rock can be characterized by measuring the contact angle (ϴ) of the liquid with the surface (discussed in Chapter 1, Section on Wettability). Siliciclastic rocks (such as sandstones) are negatively charged surfaces and adsorb cationic surfactants. Carbonate rocks are positively charged and adsorb anionic surfactants.

Sandstone is water-wet because its surface is negatively charged, repelling the negative charges of oil components like naphthenic acids. Carbonate rock has positively charged surfaces that can interact with the negative charges of naphthenic acids, making it more oil-wet. Tests with ethoxylated alcohols have shown great effectiveness in altering the wettability of oil-wet reservoirs.

Some surfactants act in the water-gas mixture to form foam in the reservoir, making gas flow more difficult, particularly in large pores. This method is called FAWAG (Foam Assisted WAG).

Nanotechnology. In recent decades, nanofluids and nanoparticles have been studied as alternatives or improvements to other EOR techniques. The great appeal of nanoscale solutions is their inherently adaptive nature. Since many properties of nanometric materials depend on their size, they can be tailored for the desired application.

To minimize surfactant losses due to adsorption on the reservoir rock, surfactant-carrying nanoparticles that can enter the reservoir rock pores and release the surfactant only in the region of interest are being studied. This is the case with silica nanoparticles (Nascimento and Lachter). Laboratory studies indicate satisfactory results for this technique.

Controlled Salinity Water. Customizing the saline composition of injection water is one of the techniques for altering reservoir rock wettability.

In carbonate reservoirs, for example, replacing CaCO\({}_{3}\) (oil-wet) with CaSO\({}_{4}\) (water-wet) on the rock surface results in wettability alteration. This can be done by injecting seawater, which contains SO\({}_{4}\)\({}^{-2}\) ions. This substitution becomes more efficient if Na\({}^{+}\) and Cl\({}^{-}\) ions are removed from the injection water, as these hinder the mobility of SO\({}_{4}\)\({}^{-2}\) ions due to increased ionic density via the ionic double layer (Putervold, T., Stand, S., Ellouz, R., Austad, T., 2015). It can be even more effective by adding SO\({}_{4}\)\({}^{-2}\) to the injection water (Zhang and Austad, 2006).

It is important to note that SO\({}_{4}\)\({}^{-2}\) ions react with barium and strontium that may be present in the connate water, potentially causing scale formation in production lines and process equipment or even damaging the well if deposited at the reservoir interface. This is a concern related to flow assurance, which we will discuss later.

There are also several studies and projects on low-salinity water injection, which could, in some cases, depending on rock, oil, and reservoir temperature characteristics, reduce the residual oil saturation.

Steam. Steam injection is a process that involves both heat and mass transfer, primarily used for heavy oil recovery. Steam injection can be cyclic or continuous. In cyclic injection, steam is injected into the production well for a certain period. Then, the well is closed for a time called "soaking," during which the steam heat dissipates into the area around the well. After the soaking period, the well is put back into production. As the high temperature reduces the oil's viscosity, a production peak is observed. Over time, the temperature around the well decreases, and production declines. After some time, another steam injection may be carried out in this well.

In continuous steam injection, steam is injected into injector wells, and fluids are displaced towards producers drilled in specific patterns for this purpose. In general, heavy oil field production begins with cyclic injection, which provides a faster return, and then continuous injection is implemented. The recovery mechanisms involved in steam injection are complex and involve viscosity reduction, oil swelling, displacement by steam, thermal variation of capillary pressure and permeability, thermal expansion, and gravitational segregation.

In-Situ Combustion. In this in-situ combustion process, air or oxygen is supplied to the reservoir through injector wells. Ignition is necessary at the injector well to start combustion, forming a combustion zone within the reservoir. The heat generated by combustion reduces the oil's viscosity, and the gases produced can become miscible, reducing the residual oil saturation. The main appeal of this process is that the heat produced is much cheaper than heat obtained by steam generators. However, this process is extremely complex, as it involves chemical combustion reactions, in-situ gas generation, and is difficult to adequately model and control.

4.2 UNCONVENTIONAL PRODUCTION MECHANISMS

Some formations have characteristics that do not allow their hydrocarbons to be produced by the conventional methods presented so far. This is the case for reservoirs with highly viscous fluids such as bitumen and tar or those with extremely low permeability such as shale and schist. Consequently, reservoirs have been classified into two main groups: conventional and unconventional.

The high viscosity of some oils is due to their high concentration of kerogen. They may be geothermally immature, meaning that the formation has not reached temperatures and pressures high enough to transform its kerogen into oil and gas. In the oil generation system, formations rich in bitumen are oil-generating rocks.

The organic matter that originates bitumen includes algae remains, spores, pollen, fragments of herbaceous and woody plants, and other plant residues. In some oil shales, the organic matter is unstructured, shapeless, and amorphous. The origin of this amorphous material is likely a mixture of degraded algae or bacterial remains. Small amounts of plant resins and waxes also contribute to the organic matter. Fossil shell fragments and bones, composed of phosphate and carbonate minerals, are part of the rock's mineral matrix.

These rocks typically contain enough hydrocarbons to burn without any additional processing and are therefore known as "the rock that burns."

For these deposits to have economic potential, they must be close enough to the surface to be mined and processed or be heated in situ.

It is observed that when oil sands (such as the tar sands found in large quantities in Canada) are heated to a sufficiently high temperature, the organic material retained in the rocks thermally decomposes to form oil and gas.

As a result, the production of this oil is more complex and expensive than the recovery of conventional oil.

Schist. Unlike shale, schist is a metamorphic rock. In the production process of schist, the rock is broken, transported to the surface, and the hydrocarbons retained in it are extracted with solvents and heating (Figure 11).

Both shale oil and oil sands present significant environmental challenges. Each barrel of oil produced from these deposits requires approximately two barrels of water and generates about 1,2 to 1,5 tons of rock. Both the water and the rocks must be reused or discarded.

Shale - Folhelho. Recently, hydraulic fracturing technology has enabled the production of oil and gas from formations considered unproductive until recently. This is the case for ultra-low permeability reservoirs such as shales. In hydraulic fracturing, water is injected at a pressure high enough to cause fractures in the rock formation (Figure 12). A proppant fluid, usually sand, is injected to prevent the fracture from closing after the operation ends. Additives are also added to prevent shale swelling due to water. The fracturing increases the rock's permeability because fluids can flow through the fractures. The opened fissures create artificial porosity in the rock, allowing lighter hydrocarbons to migrate into these pores. Because they flow more easily, lighter hydrocarbons (gas and condensate) tend to be produced preferentially. While the fractures are open, the well is kept closed and pressurized. Finally, the well is opened to allow fluids to flow to the surface.

Fracturing is effective near the well, which is why wells are often abandoned after a few years of production. Therefore, a large number of wells must be drilled to enable commercial production from these deposits. Naturally, reservoirs close to the surface and with large thicknesses are ideal for this type of exploitation. In the U.S., productive accumulations are found at depths of 2.000 meters. Exploration at depths greater than 3.000 meters becomes more difficult due to the cost of well construction.

This process is water-intensive and requires both sources for injection into the reservoirs and disposal sites for treated water.

4.3 RESERVOIR MODELING

In designing oil production systems for a field, it is necessary to conceptualize and predict the performance of the entire system, including the reservoir, wells, pipelines, processing plant, etc.

Every mathematical model has its assumptions and limitations and cannot, by itself, determine the behavior of the system under study. They must be used in conjunction with experimental data. The consistency of the mathematical models used to estimate oil production is usually verified by comparing the results of their simulations with information obtained from the field, whether through laboratory core tests, well test results, or the history of an existing field.

Initially, a static reservoir model is developed with geological and geophysical information to determine the structural framework and estimate the VOIP.

In addition to the information obtained from well tests, laboratory tests performed on cores, and seismic data, dynamic models are developed to estimate the recovery factor (RF) of the field, forecast production per well, assess the effects of fluid injection on oil recovery, evaluate sweep efficiency, etc. This is done through a flow model that seeks to depict the flow of fluids in the porous medium of the reservoir. This model helps to determine the optimal well placement for defining the drainage grid of the field (Figure 13). This grid includes not only production wells but also injection wells. In a first approach for positioning injection wells, the principle is that each production well will drain a certain volume in its surroundings and that the majority of the oil produced comes from the sweep between the injector and the producer wells.

The definition of the drainage grid involves the entire production system and requires knowledge and consolidation of geological, geophysical, flow, and geomechanical models, as well as the sizing of the production system, including capacity, well type, riser diameter and type, platform type, etc. Ultimately, the goal should be to maximize the NPV of the project while considering the uncertainties involved. It is a complex task that requires collaboration among professionals from different specialties.

The recovery factor (RF) will result from the nature of the reservoir, the development plan (drainage grid and production facilities), and the quality of its management. For example, drilling additional wells in mature fields (infill drilling) is a commonly used strategy to increase the recovery factor. Reservoir management takes place throughout the productive life of the field and requires constant monitoring. For producing fields, operational data obtained from pressure and temperature sensors, flow meters, and well tests conducted by production units are used to feed reservoir simulators, enabling the tracking of "sweep efficiency" (oil drainage performance).

The complete modeling of a reservoir results from the combination of geological, geophysical, and flow models (Figure 14). These three areas are interconnected, exchanging information for improvement, calibration, and model adjustment.

A fourth model, complementary to the flow model, is the geomechanical model, which uses all available information to predict maximum injection pressures and optimal operational conditions that will avoid exudation, fracture reactivation, caprock failure, or reservoir subsidence.

Well profiles allow the estimation of several reservoir variables, such as stratum thickness, porosity, types of fluids in the rock pores, etc. These are point data related to the location of the wells. It is detailed information with high precision, and for this reason, it is considered high-frequency data. Often, wells for acquiring reservoir data (ADR) are drilled to assess the connectivity between reservoir areas, their production potential, etc. All of this information is used to build the reservoir model.

The field seismic data has the advantage of area (horizontal) resolution but is limited in vertical resolution, and therefore is considered low-frequency information.

The combination of field seismic results with those obtained from well profiles allows for the adjustment of the proposed geological model. Information interpreted from indirect evaluations (such as seismic) and direct evaluations (from drilled wells) is used to develop the reservoir's structural and stratigraphic framework.

The reservoir flow model deals with fluid flow in the porous medium and temporal changes during oil production. The flow model relates to the dynamic properties of the reservoir, such as permeability, fluid viscosity, pressure, temperature, etc. Flow modeling uses information from geological modeling, such as the reservoir's shape, faults, etc., and from the geophysical model, it obtains information about the reservoir's area extent, among others.

Modeling is said to be deterministic when its variables are not random and have no associated uncertainty. However, we know that the information used in simulations comes from data or estimates subject to various uncertainties. Even when performed rigorously, measurements from seismic, for example, carry uncertainties inherent to the time-to-depth conversion method used. On the other hand, well logging provides information (such as permeability, porosity, etc.) whose certainty is limited to the vicinity of the well. Analyzing core samples from a single exploratory well can inform us of the permeability at that location, but it does not allow us to assert how permeability varies throughout the reservoir.

Therefore, uncertainties exist in virtually all models related to oil production, varying in intensity depending on the phenomenon being described and the quality of available data. In its pursuit of improved investment decisions, the oil industry continually develops methodologies to quantify and incorporate uncertainties into its models.

Consider, for example, that we can associate a probability of occurrence with the oil saturation values and formation thickness of an oil field as shown in Figure 15.

If, for a given value of \(h\), we select some oil saturation values from this probability distribution and use Archie's equation \(N = A \cdot h \cdot \phi \cdot S_o\) we will obtain the corresponding oil in place volume \(N\). If we do the same for thickness, now keeping the oil saturation value constant, we will obtain another series of \(N\) values. In this way, each \(N\) value has an associated probability, and we can generate a probability distribution for \(N\), which now becomes a random variable rather than deterministic.

This process leads us to the Monte Carlo numerical technique, a basis for risk assessment simulations in both exploration and oil production. The technique acts on the likelihood of a result by randomly selecting input variable samples from their probability distributions and combining them to obtain possible outcomes. By repeating the random selection process many times, a probability distribution of results is obtained.

\[\begin{equation*} N = 10^{6} \cdot 27 \cdot 0,25 \cdot 0,21 = 5,25 \cdot 10^{4} \, m^{3} \end{equation*}\]

4.3.1 Geological Models

Geological modeling seeks to represent the heterogeneities and anomalies found in the subsurface. The complexity of these models has evolved in representing the physical, chemical, and biological processes involved in oil generation.

In the mathematical modeling of these phenomena, one way to represent the accumulation of mass is based on the continuity equation. This equation, also called the general diagenesis equation, is presented below for a component \(i\):

\[\begin{equation} \frac{Dc_{i}}{Dt} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left(D \frac{\partial c_{i}}{\partial x}\right)}_{\text{Diffusion + Dispersion}} - \underbrace{v \frac{\partial c_{i}}{\partial x}}_{\text{Advection}} + \underbrace{\sum_{k} \left(\frac{\partial c_{i}}{\partial t}\right)_{k}}_{\text{Reaction}} \end{equation}\]

Structural Geology. Structural geology aims to determine the sealing potential of faults, external geometry, and thicknesses. The structural framework seeks to establish continuity or discontinuity relationships between different structural domains, identify, and represent deformation features that may impact fluid flow.

Faults in the reservoir may be sealing, fully permeable, or exhibit an intermediate transmissibility condition. The original transmissibility of the fault in the reservoir must be calculated during geological modeling. However, throughout the productive life of the field, both depletion and overpressure can reactivate faults and alter their original transmissibility, meaning the fault can acquire induced transmissibility. Geomechanical fault reactivation studies aim to indicate pressure values above which faults may acquire induced transmissibility.

Pore compressibility is a property generally considered constant in flow modeling, despite varying over time. There are various estimation methods and analytical and numerical mathematical models available in the literature. Laboratory tests are also conducted to monitor the evolution of pore compressibility.

During oil field production, mechanical damage to the formation may occur. This can result from changes in the reservoir's original stress state, reducing permeability near the well and causing a decline in its productivity index (PI). Formation damage can be estimated through laboratory tests or by analytical and numerical mathematical models.

Geological Model Grid Cells. In Example 1 (page 23), the total volume of hydrocarbons in a formation was estimated based on a single value for porosity and oil saturation for the entire reservoir. This is a simplification, limited in its accuracy, as we know these values change throughout the reservoir.

The structural model must represent the reservoir's pressure equilibrium. A set of non-linear partial differential equations is obtained, which cannot be solved analytically. To solve them, computational algorithms based on numerical methods are used, taking advantage of the computer's capacity to execute complex mathematical operations. In most cases of reservoir modeling, finite difference numerical methods are used. In this case, the reservoir is subdivided into small cells, whose properties reflect the geological and geophysical knowledge of the reservoir.

One solution is to divide the reservoir into small parts and solve the equations for each one, representing the reservoir as a grid of discrete cells (Figure 17).

Once the grid is defined (Figure 18), each cell is populated with reservoir data such as porosity, saturation, Bo, etc. The shape of the volume and its limits (top and base, fluid contacts, etc.) are estimated from seismic data.

The number of cells in a simulation needs to be optimized, as increasing the number of cells improves simulation accuracy but also increases data processing time.

The geological model is continuously improved as more wells are drilled and more data on the reservoir's dynamic behavior becomes available. Since the data is representative of the regions where wells were drilled, it is generally not valid for the entire reservoir, and its extrapolation can be a potential source of errors. Thus, uncertainty must be addressed.

4.3.2 Numerical Methods Used in Reservoir Simulation

Commercial reservoir simulators such as ECLIPSE and CMG (IMEX, GEM, and STARS) solve the governing equations of multiphase flow through porous media using a combination of advanced numerical techniques. The numerical framework adopted by these simulators has become the industry standard for petroleum reservoir simulation.

Finite Volume Method

The spatial discretization is based on the Finite Volume Method (FVM), in which the reservoir is divided into a large number of control volumes (grid blocks). The conservation equations are integrated over each control volume, ensuring local conservation of mass.

The general mass conservation equation is

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \phi \rho S \right) + \nabla \cdot \left( \rho \mathbf{v} \right) = q \end{equation}\]

where

  • \(\phi\) is the porosity,

  • \(\rho\) is the fluid density,

  • \(S\) is the phase saturation,

  • \(\mathbf{v}\) is the Darcy velocity,

  • \(q\) represents sources or sinks associated with wells.

The finite volume formulation guarantees exact mass conservation within every computational cell, which is one of the primary reasons for its widespread adoption in reservoir simulation.

Darcy Flow Approximation

The flow between neighboring cells is computed using Darcy's law.

\[\begin{equation} q_{ij} = T_{ij} \lambda \left( P_i-P_j-\rho g(z_i-z_j) \right) \end{equation}\]

where

  • \(T_{ij}\) is the transmissibility between adjacent cells,

  • \(\lambda=k_r/\mu\) is the phase mobility,

  • \(P_i\) and \(P_j\) are the pressures,

  • \(z_i\) and \(z_j\) are the elevations.

The transmissibility is generally computed using harmonic averaging of permeability values to preserve flux continuity across interfaces.

Temporal Discretization

Commercial simulators employ an implicit time integration scheme based on the Backward Euler Method.

\[\begin{equation} \frac { U^{n+1}-U^{n} } { \Delta t } = F \left( U^{n+1} \right) \end{equation}\]

This fully implicit formulation provides unconditional numerical stability and allows relatively large time steps, making it suitable for simulations spanning several decades of reservoir production.

Nonlinear Solution Using Newton-Raphson

The governing equations are highly nonlinear because several physical properties depend on pressure, saturation, and composition.

Examples include

  • relative permeability,

  • capillary pressure,

  • fluid density,

  • viscosity,

  • phase behavior.

The nonlinear system

\[\begin{equation} F(x)=0 \end{equation}\]

is linearized through the Newton-Raphson method,

\[\begin{equation} J(x^k)\Delta x = - F(x^k) \end{equation}\]

where \(J\) denotes the Jacobian matrix.

The solution vector is updated according to

\[\begin{equation} x^{k+1} = x^{k} + \Delta x. \end{equation}\]

Iterations continue until convergence criteria based on residuals and variable increments are satisfied.

Linear Solver

Each Newton iteration generates a large sparse linear system

\[\begin{equation} A x=b. \end{equation}\]

Because modern reservoir models may contain millions of unknowns, direct Gaussian elimination becomes computationally prohibitive.

Instead, iterative Krylov subspace methods are employed, including

  • GMRES (Generalized Minimal Residual),

  • BiCGSTAB (Bi-Conjugate Gradient Stabilized),

  • Conjugate Gradient (for symmetric problems).

The convergence of these iterative solvers is significantly accelerated through preconditioning techniques such as

  • CPR (Constrained Pressure Residual),

  • ILU (Incomplete LU factorization),

  • AMG (Algebraic Multigrid).

Among these methods, CPR has become the standard preconditioner for large-scale reservoir simulation because it efficiently separates pressure and saturation error modes.

Variable Coupling Strategies

Different coupling strategies may be employed depending on the simulator and the physical model.

Fully Implicit Method (FIM)

The Fully Implicit Method simultaneously solves pressure, saturation, and compositional variables within a single nonlinear system.

Its principal advantages are

  • unconditional stability,

  • large allowable time steps,

  • superior robustness for highly nonlinear problems.

Consequently, FIM is the preferred approach for most industrial reservoir simulations.

IMPES Method

The IMPES (Implicit Pressure Explicit Saturation) formulation computes

  • pressure implicitly,

  • saturation explicitly.

Although computationally less expensive per iteration, the explicit saturation update imposes severe restrictions on the allowable time step.

Adaptive Implicit Method (AIM)

Adaptive Implicit Methods dynamically identify regions exhibiting strong nonlinear behavior and solve only those regions implicitly while maintaining explicit treatment elsewhere.

This hybrid strategy improves computational efficiency while preserving numerical stability.

Grid Representation

Modern simulators support multiple grid topologies, including

  • Cartesian grids,

  • Corner-point grids,

  • Local Grid Refinement (LGR),

  • Unstructured grids.

Corner-point grids are particularly suitable for representing complex geological structures such as faults and stratigraphic pinch-outs.

Multiphase Flow Formulation

Reservoir simulators simultaneously solve conservation equations for multiple fluid phases. Each phase conservation equation is coupled through thermodynamic equilibrium relationships, relative permeability functions, capillary pressure models, and constitutive equations.

Computational Algorithm

The numerical procedure executed during each simulation time step can be summarized as follows:

  1. Read reservoir properties and grid geometry.

  2. Compute intercell transmissibilities.

  3. Assemble the mass conservation equations.

  4. Apply fully implicit temporal discretization.

  5. Linearize the nonlinear equations using Newton-Raphson.

  6. Construct the sparse Jacobian matrix.

  7. Solve the linear system using Krylov iterative methods with CPR or ILU preconditioning.

  8. Update pressures, saturations, and compositions.

  9. Verify convergence criteria.

  10. Adapt the time step and advance to the next simulation time.

Geological Model Uncertainties. In the characterization of geological model uncertainties, geostatistics plays a key role, aiming to resolve the problem of characterizing the spatial distribution of the quantities that define the amount and quality of natural resources and assessing uncertainty measures based on the samples and observations taken. It consists of a set of statistical tools that quantify the spatial continuity of the studied quantity, through spatial interpolation models based on its structural variability, and stochastic simulation models that quantify the uncertainty associated with the spatial phenomenon.

In general, it is assumed that at the well locations, the data is accurate and precise, meaning that at the well, uncertainty is zero, and it increases as we move away from those points. The simulation must be adjusted to honor these data. Therefore, it is necessary to estimate the uncertainty of the variables in the intervals between wells. Figure 19 shows a graphical representation of the porosity range in a formation proposed by a specialist. Note that the further away from the wells, the greater the uncertainty. This is an interpretative process, heavily dependent on the vision and experience of the involved technicians, which is why it is recommended that several specialists be consulted when creating an uncertainty measure.

In probabilistic models, sample data is interpreted as coming from a random process. The assumption is that the data collected from a given population is spatially correlated, where nearby points tend to have more similar values than more distant points. For example, if the porosity at a well located at point \(u_1\) is \(\Phi(u_1)\), it is likely that nearby points will show results close to \(\Phi(u_1)\), and as we move further from point \(u_1\), the correlation is expected to decrease (Figure 20).

If we denote \(u_1\) as the geographical coordinates of a position in space, then \(z(u_1)\) is the realization of the random variable \(Z(u_1)\). The goal is to model the random function \(Z(u)\), which represents a certain reservoir attribute (porosity, permeability, etc.). The variogram measures the degree of spatial dependence between two samples. The magnitude of the variogram between two points depends on the distance between them, with smaller variograms for shorter distances and larger variograms for longer distances. In this case, the variogram and covariance between two points \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) are given by:

\[\begin{equation*} \gamma(Z(u_{1}), Z(u_{2})) = E\left[(Z(u_{1}) - Z(u_{2}))^2\right] \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} Cov(Z(u_{1}), Z(u_{2})) = E\left[(Z(u_{1}) - m(u_{1}))(Z(u_{2}) - m(u_{2}))\right] \end{equation*}\]

The set of random variables \(Z(u_i)\) correlated with each other represents a random function of which only realizations \(z(u_i)\) are known. We need to determine other statistical parameters for the function, but this is impossible with only one realization for each random variable. The solution adopted is to consider that all random variables have the same mean, so:

\[\begin{equation*} E(Z(u_{1})) = E(Z(u_{2})) = \cdots = E\left[Z(u_{i}\right] = m \end{equation*}\]

Thus, \(m\) becomes position-independent and can be estimated from the experimental data:

\[\begin{equation*} m = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} Z(u_i) \end{equation*}\]

The variogram relates the variance of pairs of observations \((Z(u_i)\) and \(Z(u_j)\)) to the distance \(h\) between them. It is also assumed that the correlation between two random variables depends only on the spatial distance \(h\) between them, independent of their location. This implies that the variogram and covariance between the variables \(Z(u_1)\) and \(Z(u_2)\) can be expressed as:

\[\begin{equation} \gamma(Z(u_{1}), Z(u_{2})) = E\left[(Z(u_{1}) - Z(u_{1} + h))^2\right] = \gamma(h) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} Cov(Z(u_{1}), Z(u_{2})) = E\left[(Z(u_{1}))(Z(u_{1} + h))\right] - (m(u_{1}) - m(u_{1} + h)) = Cov(h) \end{equation}\]

Admitting stationarity, we demonstrate that

\[\begin{equation} \gamma(h) = Cov(0) - Cov(h) \end{equation}\]

In this case, the curves behave as shown in Figure 21.

The values can be calculated using the following equations:

\[\begin{equation} \gamma(h) = \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ (Z(u_i) - Z(u_i + h))^2 \right] \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} Cov(h) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ Z(u_i)Z(u_i + h) \right] - \left[ m(u_i) m(u_i + h) \right] \end{equation}\]

Where:

\[\begin{equation} m(u_n) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} Z(u_n) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} m(u_n + h) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} Z(u_n + h) \end{equation}\]

Note that from a certain distance, the variogram no longer increases with distance, stabilizing at a value equal to the average variance. This region is called the sill. The distance between the beginning of the variogram and the start of the sill is called the range. When extrapolating the variogram curve to a zero distance, we may reach a non-zero value. This is known as the Nugget Effect.

Spatial autocorrelation can then be used to estimate the value of an attribute at any location \(u_0\), where there is no sample data. The estimated value \(Z^*(u_0)\) is interpreted as a realization of the random variable \(Z(u_0)\) of the random function \(Z(u)\). The question now is how to interpolate between sample points to generate \(Z^*(u_0)\). It is proposed that this is the result of the weighted average of \(Z(u_i)\), as shown:

\[\begin{equation} Z^*(u_0) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i Z(u_i) \end{equation}\]

Thus, this is the sought-after model. An important statistical characteristic of stochastic models (estimators) is that they must be unbiased. It is also necessary to consider preferential clustering of samples, which, without differential weighting, tends to bias the estimators.

Minimizing error variance is the classical way to determine the curve that best represents a stochastic process. The error is defined as:

\[\begin{equation*} e(u_0) = Z^*(u_0) - Z(u_0) \end{equation*}\]

The error variance is:

\[\begin{equation*} var(e(u_0)) = var\left(Z^*(u_0) - Z(u_0)\right) = E\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i Z(u_i) - Z(u_0)\right)^2\right] \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} var\{e(u_0)\} = E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \lambda_i \lambda_j Z(u_i)Z(u_j)\right] + E\left[Z(u_0)^2\right] - 2E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i Z(u_i)Z(u_0)\right] \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} var\{e(u_0)\} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \lambda_i \lambda_j E\left[Z(u_i)Z(u_j)\right] + E\left[Z(u_0)^2\right] - 2\sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i E\left[Z(u_i)Z(u_0)\right] \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} var(e(u_0)) = Cov(0) + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \lambda_i \lambda_j Cov(u_i u_j) - 2 \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i Cov(u_i u_0) \end{equation*}\]

It is also demonstrated that the non-bias condition implies that the sum of the weights is equal to 1.

\[\begin{equation} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i = 1 \end{equation}\]

Kriging: Krige (1951) proposed a methodology to determine weights that fulfill two criteria concerning the estimation error: non-bias of the error and minimum estimation variance. The non-bias condition is obtained as per the equation above. The minimum estimation variance implies minimizing the error variance \(\min var(e(u_0))\), achieved by setting the derivatives of the error variance with respect to each weight \(\lambda_i\) to zero and solving the resulting equations.

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial var(e(u_0))}{\partial \lambda_i} = 0 \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i = 1 \end{equation}\]

The above equations can be solved through the Lagrange formalism where the constraint \({}_{\sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} -1=0 }\) can be added to the equation to be solved by multiplying it by the Lagrange parameter, which in this case is convenient to use \({}_{2\eta }\). Thus, the equation to be solved becomes:

\[\begin{equation*} var\left\{e(u_{0} )\right\}+2\eta \sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} -1=Cov(0)+\sum _{i=1}^{N}\sum _{j=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} \lambda _{j} Cov(u_{i} u_{j} )-2\sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} Cov(u_{i} u_{0} )+2\eta \sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} -1 \end{equation*}\]

By deriving with respect to \({}_{\lambda _{i} }\) and \({}_{\eta }\) and equating to zero:

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial var\left\{e(u_{0} )\right\}}{\partial \lambda _{i} } =\frac{\partial \left\{Cov(0)+\sum _{i=1}^{N}\sum _{j=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} \lambda _{j} Cov(u_{i} u_{j} )-2\sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} Cov(u_{i} u_{0} )+2\eta \sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} -1\right\}}{\partial \lambda _{i} } =0 \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial var\left\{e(u_{0} )\right\}}{\partial \eta } =\frac{\partial \left\{Cov(0)+\sum _{i=1}^{N}\sum _{j=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} \lambda _{j} Cov(u_{i} u_{j} )-2\sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} Cov(u_{i} u_{0} )+2\eta \sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} -1\right\}}{\partial \eta } =0 \end{equation*}\]

We obtain:

\[\begin{equation*} \sum _{j=1}^{N}\lambda _{j} Cov(u_{i} u_{j} ) -Cov(u_{i} u_{0} )+\eta =0 \quad \text{for } i=1,\, 2, \ldots, N \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \sum _{i=1}^{N}\lambda _{i} = 0 \end{equation*}\]

The above equations can be rewritten in matrix form as shown below:

\[\begin{equation*} \underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{cccc} {\lambda _{1} Cov(u_{1} u_{1} )} & {\lambda _{2} Cov(u_{1} u_{2} )} & {\ldots } & {\lambda _{N} Cov(u_{1} u_{N} )} \\ {\lambda _{1} Cov(u_{2} u_{1} )} & {\lambda _{2} Cov(u_{2} u_{2} )} & {\ldots } & {\lambda _{N} Cov(u_{2} u_{N} )} \\ {\vdots } & {\vdots } & {\ldots } & {\vdots } \\ {\begin{array}{c} {\lambda _{1} Cov(u_{N} u_{1} )} \\ {1} \end{array}} & {\begin{array}{c} {\lambda _{2} Cov(u_{2} u_{N} )} \\ {1} \end{array}} & {\ldots } & {\begin{array}{c} {\lambda _{N} Cov(u_{N} u_{N} )} \\ {0} \end{array}} \end{array}\right]}_{Cov} \underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{c} {\lambda _{1} } \\ {\lambda _{2} } \\ {\vdots } \\ {\eta } \end{array}\right]}_{\lambda } =\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{c} {Cov(u_{1} u_{0} )} \\ {Cov(u_{i} u_{0} )} \\ {} \\ {Cov(u_{i} u_{0} )} \end{array}\right]}_{Cov_{0} } \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation} Cov \lambda = Cov_0 \end{equation}\]

To obtain the values of \({}_{\lambda }\), simply invert the \({}_{Cov}\) matrix and multiply by the \({}_{Cov_{0} }\) matrix:

\[\begin{equation} \lambda = Cov^{-1} Cov_{0} \end{equation}\]

Since then, kriging has been referred to as a group of methodologies for inference about the spatial behavior of the variable. The kriging method can be linear if its estimates are weighted linear combinations of existing data, unbiased if the mean of the errors (deviations between the actual and estimated values) is zero, and optimal if the estimation errors have minimal variance.

The weights should reflect the structural proximity of the samples to the point to be estimated and, at the same time, should have a disaggregating effect on any sample clusters, thus avoiding estimator bias.

The term "kriging" encompasses a set of methods, the most common of which are:

Simple Kriging: Assumes that local means are relatively constant and very close to the known population mean. The population mean is used for each local estimation, along with the necessary neighboring points for estimation.

Ordinary Kriging: Local means are not necessarily close to the population mean, and only neighboring points are used for estimation. This is the most commonly used method in environmental problems.

Co-Kriging: An extension of ordinary kriging for situations where two or more variables are spatially dependent, and the variable to be estimated is not sampled as intensively as the other dependent variables. The values and dependencies of the other variables are used to estimate the required variable.

Kriging adjusts a variogram model, which is a measure of the continuity and structural homogeneity of the spatial process, based on limited samples. Attention should be paid to structural discontinuities such as faults, soil changes, zonal anisotropies, etc. Therefore, an estimator may perform well in some parts of the field and poorly in others.

Assuming that uncertainties arising from different effects are independent events, the final uncertainty can be obtained by integrating the uncertainties related to each factor, as exemplified in Figure 24. The final uncertainty map is calculated by summing the independent uncertain attributes (Zahuczi, 2008).

Geological uncertainties depend on the level of knowledge of the area, data acquisition, and the studies and analyses carried out. The interpreter must seek to understand the possible sources of uncertainty in their study area and represent the reliability that underpins their interpretation, considering the data available at the time (Figure 25).

4.3.2 Geophysical Models

Reservoir geophysics systematically uses seismic data as the basis for constructing its models, whether static or dynamic. Despite its low-frequency characteristics and limited bandwidth, seismic data is extensively sampled throughout the reservoir area and is therefore suitable for defining the external geometry of the reservoir. The integration of geophysical data with geological data (sparsely acquired but with high frequency) allows the definition of the internal architecture, demanding an appropriate calibration strategy and upscaling. The main geological data considered are porosity, lithology, and facies defined in cores, as well as structural discontinuities such as faults and fractures.

The geophysical (or petrogeophysical) model of the reservoir results from the generation of the seismic velocity model and the time-to-depth conversion, acoustic and elastic inversion of the reservoir, definition of external geometry and reservoir architecture, generation of facies maps, and interpretation of attributes. There are various techniques and methodologies for analyzing, characterizing, and monitoring geophysical data.

One of the most interesting variables resulting from geophysical modeling is the total rock volume GRV (Gross Rock Volume), usually expressed in millions of cubic meters. GRV expresses the total rock volume (including porous space) computed from geophysical models and does not consider porosity or fluid saturations. When the modeling is conducted in depth-converted or imaged data, it is computed directly through the product of the total thickness map by the occurrence area of the accumulation.

Uncertainties in the Geophysical Model. To study geophysical uncertainties, it is initially necessary to assume some premises regarding the quality and reliability of the data and models being treated. Examples of premises that must be met include:

  • The phase of the seismic data is known;

  • The phase of the seismic data is invariant in the seismic volume of interest (otherwise, a sensitivity analysis must be performed to verify if the observed variations can impact the various modeling processes);

  • The seismic frequency band of the final migrated and stacked volume is adequate to resolve the top and base of the reservoir units/zones studied. If not met, the thickness estimate uncertainties should be explicitly stated below a certain threshold;

  • The well logs used for seismic-log correlation are adequate and have been previously corrected for, for example, environmental effects, invasion, borehole washout, etc.;

  • The seismic-well correlation is adequate and precise;

  • The velocity model has been interpreted on an appropriate scale to represent the area's velocity variations.

In representing the uncertainties identified in geophysical interpretation, the following attributes are commonly used:

  • Signal-to-noise ratio (S/N). It expresses the quality of the seismic data that supported a given interpretation. It represents its reliability or uncertainty. It can be computed for a 3D data volume and is usually represented in maps corresponding to the investigated volume;

  • Sum of absolute amplitude (energy) in a time window around the interpreted feature;

  • RMS (root-mean-square) Amplitude Maps: commonly used to identify areas with amplitude variations, for example, due to the presence of coherent noise (multiples, reverberations, etc.);

  • Effective coverage map: represents the effective coverage (in terms of the number of traces) resulting from the seismic acquisition in a given area. Any absence of traces resulting from, for example, the presence of operational obstacles during the acquisition of seismic data;

  • Kriging standard deviation map: represents the data grid density in the horizon interpolation;

  • Correlation map (or similarity) to the closest reference data (Thore et al., 2002).

Among the various factors impacting rock volume estimates in geophysical modeling, those related to determining external geometry and internal architecture play a significant role.

Recently, 4-D seismic (Johann) has been widely used in evaluating the performance of producing reservoirs. 4-D seismic is the repetition of a 3-D survey at long intervals (6 months to 2 years), maintaining the same acquisition and processing conditions. It serves to evaluate changes due to fluid extraction, water injection, or any other activity that occurred between surveys. It is used to update the reservoir model and determine, for example, the best water injection points, flow rate, etc.

Productivity Index Model

The productivity index (PI) is an alternative form of representing an available pressure curve. Its definition is:

\[\begin{equation} IP = \frac{q}{P_e - P_w} \end{equation}\]

For a constant PI and \(p_e\), the plot of well pressure (\(p_w\)) versus flow rate (\(q\)) is a straight line with a slope of \(-1 / IP\) (Figure 26).

\[\begin{equation*} p_W = -\frac{1}{IP} q +\, p_e \end{equation*}\]

The linear IP model applies reasonably well when pressures in the porous medium are above the saturation pressure (monophasic for oil). In the presence of gas (i.e., below the saturation pressure), due to the effect of relative permeability, the same \(\mathrm{\Delta}p\) results in a lower liquid flow rate.

In countries that adopt the metric system, the PI is usually measured in \(\frac{m^3/d}{kgf/{cm}^2}\), while in countries that adopt the English system, \(\frac{bbl/d}{psi}\) is used. The flow rate here refers to the volume of liquid produced and determined under the conditions of 1 atm and 20°C, so the generated gas is not considered.

4.3.3 Flow Models

There are several different ways to develop a reservoir flow model, which can be either numerical or analytical.

The reservoir flow model deals with the flow of fluids in the porous medium and the temporal changes during oil production. The flow model relates to the reservoir's dynamic properties, such as permeability, fluid viscosity, pressure, temperature, etc. Flow modeling uses information from geological modeling, such as reservoir shape, faults, etc., and from the geophysical model, it obtains information about the reservoir's area extension, among others. 4D seismic can also be very useful, as it can provide valuable information about areas where water or gas is passing, as well as regions that are depleted or overpressured.

In the absence of more detailed information, experience gained from analogous reservoirs is often the best available source for estimating the recoverable volume of a new field. It is common to use analogous fields to estimate productivity and injectivity indices of wells, the performance of natural production mechanisms, the rate of production decline, the pressure drop trend, the recovery factor, etc.

Besides the flow model in the porous medium, it is desirable to couple it to the flow models in the well's ducts up to the platform to allow the determination of the volumes produced by the system. The capacity of the processing plant is a constraint imposed on the simulation. The modeling of flow in ducts will be presented in the next chapter.

In general terms, the mathematical models used to estimate oil production are based on conservation laws (mass, energy, and momentum), equations of state, and equations of transport phenomena. These equations are combined in different ways, depending on the available information and the physical problem to be represented. They can be single-phase (only gas or only liquid), two-phase (gas and liquid), or three-phase (gas, oil, and water). When two phases (gas and liquid) are considered, the black oil model is typically used, which treats the liquid hydrocarbon phase as a single pseudo-component. For reservoirs with lighter oil, high GOR, or where gas injection processes are intended, it is recommended to use a compositional fluid model, in which the oil is described as several components or pseudo-components, meaning that the composition of the liquid and gas phases of hydrocarbons changes.

It is possible to obtain a simplified flow model in the reservoir towards a well, assuming that the static pressure \(P_{E}\) and well pressure \(P_{W}\) are fixed values, and the reservoir has a pizza-shaped geometry, as shown in Figure 27.

Let us consider the differential form of Darcy's equation:

\[\begin{equation} q = \frac{Ak}{\mu } \frac{dp}{dr} \end{equation}\]

Assuming constant flow rate and incompressible fluid and considering radial flow (note that the brown arrows indicate the flow of oil in the radial direction towards the well) through the area \(A = 2\pi rh\), we have:

\[\begin{equation} q = \frac{k 2\pi rh}{\mu } \frac{dp}{dr} \end{equation}\]

To solve this differential equation, we need the boundary conditions. In this case:

\[\begin{array}{l} r = r_w \quad p(r_w) = p_w \\ r = r_e \quad p(r_e) = p_e \end{array}\]

\[q \int_{r_w}^{r_e} \frac{dr}{r} = \int_{p_w}^{p_e} \frac{k 2\pi h}{\mu } dp\]

\[\begin{equation} q = \frac{k 2\pi h}{\mu } \frac{(p_e - p_w)}{\ln\left( \frac{r_e}{r_w} \right)} \end{equation}\]

Note that the flow rate will depend on pressures, the height (h) of the oil layer, permeability (k), viscosity (µ), and the diameter (or more precisely, radius \(r_w\)) of the well.

Example. Determine the flow rate produced by a well with the following characteristics:

Static reservoir pressure \(p_e = 300\) atm

Well pressure \(p_w = 273\) atm

External reservoir radius \(r_e = 200\) m

Well radius \(r_w = 4,5\) in

Oil viscosity \(\mu_o = 1,67\) cp

Oil formation volume factor \(B_o = 1,5\) m\(^3\)/m\(^3\) std

Permeability \(k = 250\) md

Formation height \(h = 20\) meters

Solution. From the equation \(q = \frac{2\pi kh(P_E - P_W)}{\mu \ln\left(\frac{r_E}{r_W}\right)}\frac{1}{B_o}\), we have:

\[q = \frac{2\pi (250 / 1000)(20 \times 100)(300 - 273)}{1,67 \ln\left( \frac{200}{4,5 \times 0,0254} \right)} \times \frac{1}{1,5} = 4671,98 \, \text{cm}^3/\text{s}\]

In the above development, it was assumed that there is no gas, and that there is external mass input, as if an aquifer is acting laterally, ensuring the maintenance of pressure \(P_E\). This is an idealization that serves for quick calculations, such as estimating the current flow of the field, but we would not be able to predict how this flow will behave over time if the reservoir pressure drops, nor how the pressure varies with the reservoir radius, etc.

A lot of effort has been dedicated to finding analytical models, but this is only possible if there is an analytical solution for the mathematical equations under the established boundary conditions. Although the mathematical models currently used are solved numerically, analytical solutions are important for understanding reservoir behavior and will therefore be presented here.

Hydraulic Diffusivity Equation

To describe the flow in a porous medium, it is necessary to adapt the continuity equation (derived in chapter 2). In this case, considering the porosity \(\phi\) of the medium, the mass of the volume \(V\) is \(m = V\phi \rho\) and the fluid velocity is \(v = \frac{q}{A\phi}\). Defining the apparent velocity as \(u = \frac{q}{A} = v\phi\), we have \(v = \frac{u}{\phi}\). Substituting into equation 2.73, the continuity equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial \phi \rho }{\partial t} = -\left( \frac{\partial }{\partial x} \rho u_{x} + \frac{\partial }{\partial y} \rho u_{y} + \frac{\partial }{\partial z} \rho u_{z} \right) \end{equation}\]

Here, \(u_{x}, u_{y}, u_{z}\) are apparent velocities. Note that \(u\), defined as \(u = q / A\), is not the actual fluid velocity since the real area available for its flow is smaller, as part of the space is occupied by the rock matrix.

Assuming that gravitational terms are negligible, we can use the following differential versions of Darcy's equation: \[u_{x} = -\frac{k_{x}}{\mu} \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}, \quad u_{y} = -\frac{k_{y}}{\mu} \frac{\partial p}{\partial y}, \quad u_{z} = -\frac{k_{z}}{\mu} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z}\]

Substituting these equations into the continuity equation, we obtain:

\[\frac{\partial \phi \rho}{\partial t} = - \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \frac{k_{x}}{\mu} \rho \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{k_{y}}{\mu} \rho \frac{\partial p}{\partial y} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \frac{k_{z}}{\mu} \rho \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} \right) \right)\]

The compressibility of any material (equation 1.4 from chapter 3 on Exploration) can be represented as a function of its density, as shown below:

\[\rho = \frac{m}{V}, \quad V = \frac{m}{\rho}, \quad \frac{\partial V}{\partial p} = \frac{m}{\rho^2} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p}, \quad c = \frac{m}{V} \frac{1}{\rho^2} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p}, \quad c = \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p}\]

Thus, we obtain:

\[\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial x} = c \rho \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}, \quad \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = c \rho \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\]

From the compressibility equation of the formation \(c_f = \frac{1}{\phi} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial p}\), we obtain:

\[\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = c_f \phi \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\]

Expanding the derivative on the left and substituting the terms:

\[\frac{\partial \phi \rho}{\partial t} = \rho \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \phi \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}\]

\[\frac{\partial \phi \rho}{\partial t} = \rho c_f \phi \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + \rho c \phi \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}, \quad \frac{\partial \phi \rho}{\partial t} = \rho (c_f + c)\phi \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\]

We obtain the following relationship: \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \phi \rho }{\partial t} = \rho c_{t} \phi \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} \end{equation*}\] where \[\begin{equation*} c_{t} = c_{f} + c \end{equation*}\]

Expanding the derivative on the right for the term in \(x\): \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\, \partial x} \left(\frac{k_{x} }{\mu } \rho \frac{\partial p}{\, \partial x} \right) = \frac{k_{x} }{\mu } \left(\rho \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial x^{2} } + \frac{\partial \rho }{\, \partial x} \frac{\partial p}{\, \partial x} \right) = \frac{k_{x} }{\mu } \left(\rho \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial x^{2} } + c \rho \left(\frac{\partial p}{\, \partial x} \right)^{2} \right) \end{equation*}\]

Since the term \(\left(\frac{\partial p}{\, \partial x} \right)^{2}\) is very small, it can be neglected.

A medium is considered homogeneous when its physical properties are the same at any position and isotropic when they are the same in any direction. Considering a homogeneous and isotropic porous medium and applying the same procedure for the other derivatives in \(y\) and \(z\), the equation becomes: \[\begin{equation*} c_{t} \phi \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = \frac{k}{\mu } \left( \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial x^{2} } + \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial y^{2} } + \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial z^{2} } \right) \\ \hspace{1cm} \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = \frac{k}{c_{t} \phi \mu } \left( \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial x^{2} } + \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial y^{2} } + \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial z^{2} } \right) \end{equation*}\]

This is defined as the hydraulic diffusivity equation: \[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{\eta } \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial x^{2} } + \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial y^{2} } + \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial z^{2} } \end{equation}\] where \(\eta = \frac{k}{c_{t} \phi \mu }\).

In cylindrical coordinates and assuming no variations in the \(\theta\) and \(z\) directions, the hydraulic diffusivity equation is represented as: \[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{\eta } \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial ^{2} p}{\, \partial r^{2} } + c\left(\frac{\partial p}{\, \partial r} \right)^{2} + \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial p}{\, \partial r} \end{equation*}\]

Since both \(c\) and \(\left(\frac{\partial p}{\, \partial r} \right)^{2}\) are very small, their product is even smaller and can be neglected from the equation. Thus, we obtain: \[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial }{\partial r} \left( r \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \right) = \frac{1}{\eta } \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} \end{equation}\]

There are analytical solutions to the above equation for three types of flow regimes: steady-state, pseudo-steady-state, and transient, depending on the boundary conditions adopted. In all the cases we will see below, it is considered that the reservoir has a constant height \(h\), the flow is radial, and the flow rate \(q\) is constant.

Steady-State Regime. In steady-state, the accumulation term (right-hand side) of the hydraulic diffusivity equation is zero. Assuming that the well pressure (at \(r = r_{w}\)) is \(p_{w}\) and the pressure at the reservoir boundary (at \(r = r_{e}\)) is the static pressure \(p_{e}\), the hydraulic diffusivity equation and boundary conditions become: \[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial }{\partial r} \left( r \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \right) = 0 \end{equation*}\] \[p(r_{w}) = p_{w}, \quad p(r_{e}) = p_{e}\] The solution to the above equation gives an expression for the pressure as a function of the radius \(r\), which allows determining the pressure at any point in the reservoir: \[\begin{equation} p(r) = p_{w} + \frac{p_{e} - p_{w}}{\ln \left( \frac{r_{e}}{r_{w}} \right)} \ln \left( \frac{r}{r_{w}} \right) \end{equation}\]

The pressure profile is shown in Figure 28.

Pseudo-Steady-State Regime. In this case, it is assumed that there is no external mass input into the reservoir. This condition is mathematically imposed by setting the pressure derivative at the reservoir boundary (at \(r = r_{e}\)) to zero, since, according to Darcy's law, there is no mass flow under this condition. Without external mass input, oil production will occur due to the reduction in pore volume. In this case, it can be shown that: \[\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\frac{q}{k \pi r_{e}^{2} h \phi } = \text{constant}\] Substituting this into the hydraulic diffusivity equation gives: \[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial }{\partial r} \left( r \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \right) = -\frac{q \mu }{k \pi r_{e}^{2} h} \end{equation*}\] \[p(r_{w}) = p_{w}, \quad \left. \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \right|_{r = r_{e}} = 0\] The solution is: \[\begin{equation*} p(r) = p_{w} + \frac{q \mu }{2\pi kh} \left[ \ln \left( \frac{r}{r_{w}} \right) - \frac{r^{2}}{2r_{e}^{2}} \right] \end{equation*}\]

It is possible to develop a time solution by considering the following boundary conditions: \[p(r, 0) = p_{i}, \quad \left. \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \right|_{r = 0} = \frac{q_{w} \mu }{k A}, \quad \lim_{x \to \infty} p(r, t) = p_{i}\] \[\begin{equation*} p(t) = p_{i} - \frac{q \mu }{2\pi kh} \left[ \frac{2t}{\eta r_{e}^{2}} + \ln \left( \frac{r}{r_{w}} \right) - \frac{3}{4} \right] \end{equation*}\]

The combination of the two equations allows an expression for the pressure over time and the radius of the reservoir: \[\begin{equation} p(r, t) = p_{i} - \frac{q \mu }{2\pi kh} \left[ \frac{2t}{\eta r_{e}^{2}} - \ln \left( \frac{r}{r_{w}} \right) - \frac{r^{2}}{r_{e}^{2}} + \ln \left( \frac{r_{e}}{r_{w}} \right) - \frac{3}{4} \right] \end{equation}\]

Because these equations are not solved simultaneously, this combination allows plotting a pressure curve for different times and radii. Therefore, it is considered pseudo-steady, but it would be better described as pseudo-transient (Figure 29).

Transient Regime. We can develop a transient solution by assuming that the reservoir is infinite, producing at a constant flow rate through a single well. In this case, the full hydraulic diffusivity equation must be considered. The boundary conditions are more complex (Figure 30). \[p(r, 0) = p_{i}, \quad \left. \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \right|_{r_{w}} = \frac{q_{w} \mu }{k A}, \quad \lim_{x \to \infty} p(r, t) = p_{i}\] \[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial }{\partial r} \left( r \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \right) = \frac{\phi \mu c_{t}}{k} \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} \end{equation*}\]

The solution is quite complex, and the curious reader may refer to Rosa (2007). The resulting equation is: \[\begin{equation} p(r, t) = p_{i} + \frac{q \mu }{2\pi kh} \left[ \frac{1}{2} E\left( \frac{4t}{\eta r^{2}} \right) \right] \end{equation}\]

Where \(E(x)\) is the exponential integral function: \[E(x) = -E(-x) = \int_{}^{} \frac{e^{-\xi }}{\xi } d\xi\]

For values of \(\frac{\eta r^{2}}{4t} < 0,025\), the above equation can be simplified to: \[\begin{equation} p(r, t) = p_{i} + \frac{q \mu }{2\pi kh} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{4t}{1,78 \eta r^{2}} \right) \right] \end{equation}\]

In summary, we have in the steady-state regime, there is a mass influx that maintains a constant pressure \(P_{E}\) at the reservoir boundary. The flow rate, \(P_{W}\), \(P_{E}\), and the pressure profile in the reservoir are constant. In the transient regime, there is a variation in the pressure profile over time. The reservoir is considered infinite, so there will always be a distant point with pressure \(P_{E}\). \(P_{W}\) varies with time. In the pseudo-steady-state regime, pressures \(P_{W}\) and \(P_{E}\) vary with time, but the pressure profile is maintained. There is no mass influx to maintain \(P_{E}\). Figure 31 presents a comparison of the pressure profiles in the different regimes.

Formation Damage. In porous media, fluid flow near the well can be affected by paraffin deposition, clogging of flow channels by suspended solids, clay swelling, reduced relative permeability to oil due to the advancement of the water front, etc. All these phenomena can reduce well productivity and are generally referred to as "formation damage."

When damage occurs near the well, the pressure profile changes as shown in Figure 32. The well pressure (\(P_{w}\)) is lower than the pressure (\(P_{w}'\)) without damage. If we solve the steady-state equation restricted to radius \(r_{a}\), and under the boundary conditions below: \[p(r_{w}) = p_{w}^{'} \quad p(r_{a}) = p_{a}\] we can deduce: \[\begin{equation*} \frac{2k\pi h}{q\mu}(p_{a} - p_{w}^{'}) = \ln \left( \frac{r_{a}}{r_{w}} \right) \end{equation*}\] Now, under the boundary condition: \[p(r_{w}) = p_{w} \quad p(r_{a}) = p_{a}\] In the damaged region, we have a local permeability \(k_{a}\): \[\begin{equation*} \frac{2k_{a}\pi h}{q\mu}(p_{a} - p_{w}) = \ln \left( \frac{r_{a}}{r_{w}} \right) \end{equation*}\] By combining the above equations, we obtain \(s\) (for "skin" in English): \[\begin{equation} s = \frac{2k_{a}\pi h}{q\mu}(p_{w}^{'} - p_{w}) = \left( \frac{k}{k_{a}} - 1 \right) \ln \left( \frac{r_{a}}{r_{w}} \right) \end{equation}\]

The expression for the difference between the pressures is dimensionless. Using the transient regime equation, we can associate the pressure loss of a well after the start of operation due to formation damage as follows: \[\begin{equation} \frac{2k_{a}\pi h}{q\mu}(p_{i} - p_{w}) = \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{4kt}{1,78\phi \mu c_{t} r_{w}^{2}} \right) + s \end{equation}\]

In all the above cases, it is considered that the reservoir has the shape of a cylinder, and the flow rate \(q\) is constant. These are idealizations that allowed the resolution of the differential equations but limit the applicability of the models.

It is noted that for linear equations, the combination of solutions is also a solution to the problem. Dittz solved the diffusivity equation for various geometries by incorporating these changes through an area factor in the equations, known as the shape factor. These models serve to verify the consistency of more complex models, often commercial simulators developed for the industry. They also form the basis for Formation Evaluation studies.

In real situations, representing the reservoir's behavior with multiple wells, permeability variations, pressure drop below the bubble point, etc., is very complex, and a set of nonlinear partial differential equations arises, which cannot be solved analytically. To solve them, computational algorithms based on numerical methods are used, developed to leverage the high capacity of computers for performing mathematical operations. In most cases of reservoir modeling, numerical methods, such as finite differences, are used for their resolution. In this case, the reservoir is subdivided into small cells whose properties reflect the geological and geophysical knowledge of the reservoir. The mass balance equations, transport phenomena, and thermodynamic equations are solved for each cell in a complex system that requires high computational power.

Given the nature of the reservoir modeling problem, it is necessary to incorporate uncertainties in the forecast of STOIIP and RF. This can be done by considering three scenarios: optimistic, base case, and pessimistic. The goal is to identify three scenarios, or three models, that correspond to different conceptions of the reservoir under study, considering the quality and quantity of available reservoir data at that time.

In probabilistic reservoir studies, it is common to define scenarios, perform realizations, and run simulations (Marcelo Blauth, Carlos Eduardo B. de Salles Abreu, Marcelo C. Monteiro, Olinto Gomes de Souza Jr) as follows:

Scenario. Represents a certain hypothesis considered for the reservoir under study. Examples of possible scenarios include:

  • Two seismic interpretations of the reservoir top.

  • Different velocity models for time-depth conversion.

  • Alternative seismic interpretations following different reflectors to define the same geological horizon, particularly in areas with a low signal-to-noise ratio, making it difficult to map the horizons of interest.

  • Recognition of different conceptual geological models, such as those associated with turbiditic or carbonate system deposition.

  • Different structural mapping criteria, considering the incorporation of different levels of structural complexity for each scenario. In the optimistic case, mapped fault displacements would allow for open communication between faulted blocks. In the pessimistic case, it would assume restrictions on communication between faulted blocks (this applies when the mapped displacements are close to or below the vertical resolution limit estimated for the study area).

  • Different generations of seismic cubes, resulting from applying distinct seismic data processing workflows, for the same modeling domain (time or depth).

Realization. Involves varying uncertain parameters within a scenario. It implies generating a probability distribution. Examples of realizations include:

  • Geostatistical simulations (realizations) of porosity.

  • Stochastic simulations, using, for example, an interpreted horizon as a reference case and the errors associated with predictions for wells drilled in the area as a conditioning variable.

  • Stochastic simulations associated with the variation of oil-water or gas-oil contact positions.

  • Stochastic simulations of a given facies model or petrophysical input model, etc.

Simulation. Involves generating multiple realizations of a given variable (e.g., porosity, permeability, saturation, etc.), each with an identical probability of occurrence. These realizations can be conditioned on known data and must honor that data, as well as its statistical and spatial distribution. In an exhaustive process with hundreds or thousands of simulations, it is possible to define their cumulative distribution. From this distribution, the percentiles representing P10, P50, and P90 are selected.

The Base Case (or Reference Case) should be the scenario or model that best represents the reservoir being studied in conceptual terms, considering the available data. Its definition will depend on various factors, such as the geophysicist's experience in the sedimentary basin or the depositional environment, the quality and quantity of available data, including different types of seismic data, and their derived attributes, as well as well logs, velocity models for time-depth conversion, seismic-well calibration, etc.

Uncertainties in Flow Modeling. Sources of uncertainty in flow modeling include PVT properties of fluids, relative permeability, aquifer behavior, calibration factor, vertical anisotropy, etc. (Figure 33). Simulations are then performed to determine production curves (volume produced, \(N_p\), over time). For flow simulations, the variables of interest are transmissibility in fault planes, fractures, permeability, vertical barriers (shale intercalations), tarmat, compressibility, etc.

Using experimental design techniques, the simulations to be carried out are defined. In practice, a simplified representation (called the response surface) of the simulator is adopted to perform the simulations more quickly.

The Base Case should incorporate all surfaces (horizons and faults) that define the external geometry of the studied reservoir, as well as its internal architecture, fluid contacts, and structural framework. The uncertainties are then grouped according to the probability of each scenario and ranked by \(N_p\) to obtain the \(N_p\) values for the P10, P50, and P90 percentiles.

UNCERTAINTY MANAGEMENT

The E&P industry invests significant resources in mitigating relevant uncertainties to support its oil field development plans. Some companies classify risk mitigation actions into information acquisition, flexibility, and robustness.

Information Acquisition. This involves collecting data to reduce uncertainty, aiming to select the appropriate strategy with greater predictability and lower risk. Additional information may require, for example:

  • An ADR well,

  • A new seismic survey,

  • A TFR (formation test),

  • A TLD (long-duration test),

The calculation of the value of information (VOI) is part of the methodology for evaluation and decision-making support for such investments, which can be very costly. VOI corresponds to the difference between the expected average value of two alternatives, one with more information and the other with less. It can also be understood as the difference in NPV (net present value) with and without information in each scenario, weighted by the probabilities of each scenario. If the cost of information is less than the VOI, the investment should be made (Figure 34).

In the VOI technique, the information must add value to the project, meaning that it must improve the assumptions and lead to changes in investment decisions. Thus, the relationship between the models representing the probabilistic production curves (P10, P50, and P90), obtained during the uncertainty analysis stage, and the scenarios to be considered in VOI is not direct. This is because the result of the uncertainty analysis was obtained after combining all uncertain parameters, and for VOI, only the variables that can be revealed by the proposed test should be considered.

Example: Suppose that one of the project's risks is associated with a block of the reservoir bounded by faults, not yet proven by a well, and we want to calculate the VOI of an ADR well in this region. In this case, what will be investigated is the occurrence of a reservoir with oil, perm-porous quality, top and bottom positions, contacts, and saturations only in the block region. However, nothing will be revealed about the uncertainties outside the block in question. Therefore, it would be more appropriate to consider the base case model (or the P50 realization model of the production curve) for the rest of the field and proceed to create scenarios for VOI, considering what can be revealed by the ADR in the investigated block and what can be optimized in the block in each scenario, if revealed.

Information acquisition should allow the drainage scheme (number, geometry, and location of wells) and the capacity of the FPSO, designed for the expected scenario without information, to be optimized.

However, there may be critical uncertainties for the project that cannot be reduced to an acceptable level, even if the company is willing to invest in information. Moreover, information acquisition actions often impact the project's schedule and revenue. As an alternative to information acquisition, the value of adding flexibility to the project should be calculated.

Flexibility. These are actions that provide the project with options and resources that allow future adjustments during implementation and operation, consequently reducing risk. Some examples of flexibility actions include:

  • Allowing the use of WAG (Water Alternating Gas) in water injectors,

  • Allowing the conversion of producers into injectors,

  • Altering producing intervals via intelligent well completions,

  • Providing spare slots in the FPSO for additional well installation,

  • Studying varied and flexible layouts, allowing adjustment of the drainage plan.

The value of flexibility (VOF) can be estimated as the difference in NPV with and without flexibility in each scenario, weighted by the respective probabilities (Figure 35).

If the cost of flexibility is less than the VOF, this option should be made available.

Robustness. These are actions aimed at making the project more adaptable to various scenarios, making it less sensitive to critical variables—those whose uncertainty primarily affects the project's response.

Examples of actions aimed at mitigating risks include:

  • Increasing the number of injector wells or introducing flexibility to ensure reservoir pressure is maintained in case of ineffective aquifer support,

  • Repositioning wells or adding new wells to ensure that fault-separated blocks are effectively drained, regardless of whether the fault is sealing or not,

  • Adjusting well design to act on different layers to ensure effective water injection for low vertical transmissibility layers,

  • Conditioning the drilling of a high-risk well on the drilling of a lower-risk well to provide additional information about the reservoir in a specific region.

The selection of the best drainage plan should correspond to the base case (deterministic). The next step is uncertainty analysis, considering various scenarios (pessimistic, moderate, optimistic), but always adopting the same well scheme.

Under conditions of high uncertainty, the so-called "optimal" plan, which maximizes the outcome for the moderate scenario, may not be the most appropriate for the other scenarios. In this case, alternative strategies to the initial optimal plan should be sought, aiming to reduce the risk, and the selection of the best drainage plan should consider the uncertainty of reservoir parameters to minimize the risks of the production curve.

The generation of alternative projects should use strategies or robustness actions that make the project more suitable for different scenarios and less sensitive to critical variables in terms of uncertainty, balancing economics and risk.

It is worth noting that the alternative drainage plan has a lower NPV in the base case (since the initial project is optimized for the base case), but produces significant improvements in other scenarios, especially in the pessimistic cases of critical variables, as seen in Figure 36.

In this methodology, the quantification of project robustness consists of calculating the expected monetary value (EMV), weighting the NPVs by the probabilities of each scenario. The project with the highest EMV is generally the most robust and has the lowest risk.

Besides EMV, each response should be individually analyzed with its respective probability, the greater or lesser variance in response, or the lower sensitivity to the critical parameter.

Therefore, after optimizing the base case and defining the P10, P50, and P90 probabilistic curves for the initial drainage plan, pessimistic and optimistic geological and flow simulation scenarios and models should be generated concerning the critical variables (those whose variation most affects hydrocarbon recovery).

Based on these results, varied strategies can be established for well positioning, the number of wells, perforation strategies, and well architectures to make this parameter less important.

Once geological models expressing the uncertainty of parameters and alternative projects aimed at reducing sensitivity to these parameters are generated, the next step is to quantify the economic value (NPV) of each plan/model. The EMV is obtained by weighting the NPVs in each scenario by their respective probabilities, which are also available in the uncertainty analysis.

Finally, the best project should be selected using the criteria of the highest EMV, lowest risk (least difference between optimistic and pessimistic NPVs), least negative impact (smallest reduction in NPV), and a combination of the previous criteria.

Uncertainty in Project Management. The uncertainties in the analyses decrease with each drilled well and each reprocessed seismic (Figure 37). The set of information (stratigraphic sections, structural sections, isopach maps, zoning, rock properties, hydrocarbon volume, seismic horizons, faults, attributes, seismic facies, geometry, etc.) is continuously interpreted. This interpretation depends on the complexity of the formation and the experience of the geologists involved. A uniform structure like a dipping homocline can be inferred from a few wells, but a structure like a salt dome may require many wells and several seismic lines.

Uncertainty in Production Systems. For existing production systems, Blauth suggests that "initially, all geological uncertainties should be considered in the scenarios and realizations until proven otherwise, which could occur, for example, when dynamic simulation eventually shows some incompatibility between a geological scenario and the production history, thus invalidating that geological scenario or realization."

4.4 MASS BALANCE

Among the various ways to perform a mass balance of a reservoir, we are interested in the one where the input variables are known or measured, such as the produced oil volume \(Np\), the formation factor \(Bo\), water saturation \(Sw\), etc. It is also important to know how much oil remains after a certain period of production. The fundamental equation for this balance is:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{dm}{dt} = \dot{m}_{in} - \dot{m}_{out} \end{equation}\]

Where:

\(m\) = mass contained in the reservoir

\(\dot{m}_{in}\) = mass flow rate injected into the reservoir

\(\dot{m}_{out}\) = mass flow rate produced by the reservoir

The mass variation in the reservoir over a given time interval is:

\[\begin{equation*} \Delta m = \int \dot{m}_{in} dt - \int \dot{m}_{out} dt \end{equation*}\]

The right-hand terms correspond to the injected and produced mass, which are accounted for over the productive life of an oil field. Thus, for a given time period, the equation can be understood as:

\[\begin{equation*} \text{final mass} - \text{initial mass} = \text{injected mass} - \text{produced mass} \end{equation*}\]

or

\[\begin{equation*} \text{produced mass} = \text{initial mass} - \text{final mass} + \text{injected mass} \end{equation*}\]

This equation can be written in volumetric terms. Let's consider the case of a reservoir containing only oil and water.

Oil Reservoir

Consider, for example, the material balance of an oil field with \(\rho_{O} = 900 \, \frac{kg}{m^{3}}\), \(Bo = 1,5\), producing 100,000 std bpd, without an active aquifer, and water injection, as shown in Figure 40. Suppose we want to determine the required water injection rate (\(\rho_{w} = 1.000 \, \frac{kg}{m^{3}}\)) at the start of operations (when only oil is produced) to avoid mass loss in the reservoir and maintain reservoir pressure.

Assume that specific masses are constant (incompressible fluid). In this case, to maintain reservoir pressure, the accumulation must be zero, and the oil outflow rate must equal the water inflow rate. The balance equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation*} \dot{m}_{W, in} = \dot{m}_{O, out} \end{equation*}\]

Where:

\(\dot{m}_{W, in}\) = mass flow rate of injected water

\(\dot{m}_{O, out}\) = mass flow rate of produced oil

The mass flow rates can be expressed in terms of volumetric flow rates. In this case, we have:

\[\begin{equation*} \rho_{w} q_{w} = \rho_{o} q_{o} B_{o} \end{equation*}\]

Where:

\(q_{w}\) = volumetric water injection rate at standard conditions

\(q_{o}\) = volumetric oil production rate at standard conditions

\(B_{o}\) = oil formation volume factor

Since the specific masses are considered to have the same value, they cancel out. In this case, the required water injection rate is:

\[\begin{equation*} q_{w} = \frac{\rho_{o} q_{o} B_{o}}{\rho_{w}} = \frac{900 \cdot 100.000 \cdot 1,5}{1.000} = 135.000 \end{equation*}\]

This calculation does not take into account pressure variations in the reservoirs, oil compressibility, water compressibility, or pore compressibility, which should be considered if we want a more accurate number for the water injection rate.

Since \(V_{oi} = V_{pi}\), these terms cancel out:

\[\begin{equation*} \text{produced volume} = \Delta V_{o} + \Delta V_{w} - \Delta V_{p} \end{equation*}\]

Note that the variation in porous volume is negative, meaning it contracts as pressure decreases. An alternative expression to avoid confusion is:

\[\begin{equation*} \text{produced volume} = \Delta V_{o} + \Delta V_{w} + |\Delta V_{p}| \end{equation*}\]

In summary, we have:

\[\begin{equation*} \text{produced volume} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Variation in oil volume} \\ + \text{Variation in water volume} \\ + \text{Variation in porous volume} \end{array} \right\} \end{equation*}\]

Consider a reservoir where, after 2 years of oil production, the pressure has decreased from \(p_{i} = 150\) kgf/cm\({}^{2}\) to \(p_{f} = 125\) kgf/cm\({}^{2}\). Determine the volume of oil produced considering the initial volumes and compressibilities below:

Using the compressibility equations, we can determine the volume variations of the pores, water, and oil.

\[\begin{equation*} \Delta p = p - p_{i} = (125 - 150) \cdot 14,2 = -355 \, \text{psi} \end{equation*}\]

The volume variations can be determined from their respective compressibilities. For the porous volume, we have:

\[\begin{equation*} c_{f} = \frac{1}{V_{p}} \left(\frac{dV_{p}}{dp}\right)_{T} \end{equation*}\]

Gas Reservoir

For a gas reservoir, there are times when it is convenient to express the mass balance equation in terms of the number of moles. Initially, assuming no fluid injection or water influx from an aquifer, the mass balance is:

\[\begin{equation*} \text{final moles} = \text{initial moles} - \text{produced moles} \end{equation*}\]

Let \(n\) be the final number of moles, \(n_{i}\) the initial number of moles, and \(n_{p}\) the produced moles:

\[\begin{equation} n = n_{i} - n_{p} \end{equation}\]

Using the ideal gas law, the number of final moles \(n\) present in the reservoir at \(p\) and \(T\) at any given moment is:

\[\begin{equation} n = \frac{pV}{ZRT} \end{equation}\]

Under initial conditions, \(n_{i}\) can be determined as:

\[\begin{equation} n_{i} = \frac{p_{i} V_{i}}{Z_{i} RT} \end{equation}\]

Assuming the reservoir temperature \(T\) remains constant.

For a produced volume \(G_{p}\) (at standard conditions) and considering that the compressibility factor at standard conditions is equal to 1, we have:

\[\begin{equation} n_{p} = \frac{p_{o} G_{p}}{RT_{o}} \end{equation}\]

Substituting into the mass balance equation, we get:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{p}{Z} = \frac{1}{V} \left(\frac{p_{i} V_{i}}{Z_{i}} - \frac{Tp_{o}}{T_{o}} G_{p}\right) \end{equation}\]

When a dry gas reservoir produces by gas mass expansion, without fluid injection or water influx from an aquifer, no water is produced. The variations in porous volume and connate water are negligible, and we can assume the reservoir volume remains constant, i.e., \(V = V_{i}\). The equation then becomes:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{p}{Z} = \frac{p_{i}}{Z_{i}} - \frac{Tp_{o}}{V_{i} T_{o}} G_{p} \end{equation}\]

The volume \(V_{i}\) can be converted to standard conditions \(G\) using the equation from Chapter 2:

\[\begin{equation*} V_{i} = G \left(\frac{T}{T_{o}}\right)\left(\frac{p_{o}}{p_{i}}\right)\left(\frac{Z_{i}}{Z_{o}}\right) \end{equation*}\]

Since \(Z_{o} = 1\), the equation above can be rearranged as:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{Tp_{o}}{V_{i} T_{o}} = \frac{p_{i}}{Z_{i} G} \end{equation}\]

Substituting into the previous mass balance equation, we have:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{p}{Z} = \frac{p_{i}}{Z_{i}} - \frac{p_{i}}{Z_{i} G} G_{p} \end{equation}\]

The values of \(p_{i}\), \(Z_{i}\) are measured at the beginning of production, and \(p\), \(Z\), and \(G_{p}\) are measured over time. The form of the above equation allows for generating a graph of \(p/Z\) versus \(G_{p}\), where the slope is \(-\frac{p_{i}}{Z_{i} G}\) and the intercept is \(\frac{p_{i}}{Z_{i}}\). Note that when \(p/Z = 0\), we have \(G = G_{p}\).

\(c_{w}\) = water compressibility

\(V_{pi}\) = initial pore volume

\(S_{wi}\) = initial water saturation

The change in pore volume is: \[\begin{equation*} \Delta V_{p} = c_{f} V_{pi} \Delta p \end{equation*}\]

Thus, the total volume change is: \[\begin{equation*} \Delta V_{o} + \Delta V_{w} + \Delta V_{p} = N(B_{o} - B_{oi}) + NB_{oi} \left(\frac{c_{f} + c_{w} S_{wi}}{1-S_{wi}} \right) \Delta p \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation} c_{o} = \frac{1}{V_{o}} \frac{\Delta V_{o}}{\Delta p} = \frac{B_{o} - B_{oi}}{B_{oi} \Delta p} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} B_{o} - B_{oi} = c_{o} B_{oi} \Delta p \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{p} B_{o} = Nc_{o} B_{oi} \Delta p + NB_{oi} \left(\frac{c_{f} + c_{w} S_{wi}}{1 - S_{wi}} \right) \Delta p \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N = \frac{N_{p} B_{o}}{B_{oi} \left(c_{o} + \frac{c_{f} + c_{w} S_{wi}}{1 - S_{wi}} \right) \Delta p} \end{equation}\]

Given that \[\begin{equation} S_{wi} + S_{oi} = 1 \end{equation}\]

Thus, \[\begin{equation} N = \frac{N_{p} B_{o}}{B_{oi} \left(\frac{c_{o} S_{oi} + c_{f} + c_{w} S_{wi}}{1 - S_{woi}} \right) \Delta p} \end{equation}\]

Thus: \[\begin{equation} NB_{oi} = \frac{N_{p} B_{o}}{c_{eo} \Delta p} \end{equation}\]

The equation resembles the compressibility equation: \[\begin{equation} c_{eo} = \frac{1}{NB_{oi}} \frac{N_{p} B_{o}}{\Delta p} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \Delta V_{g} = G \left( B_{g} - B_{gi} \right) \end{equation}\]

Where \(G\) is the gas cap volume under standard conditions. Defining

\[\begin{equation} m = \frac{G B_{gi}}{N B_{oi}} \end{equation}\]

We get the following expression for \(G\)

\[\begin{equation} G = m N \frac{B_{oi}}{B_{gi}} \end{equation}\]

Therefore:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta V_{g} = m N B_{oi} \left( \frac{B_{g}}{B_{gi}} - 1 \right) \end{equation}\]

Variation of connate water volume. Connate water is present both in oil and gas, so changes in its volume cause modifications in volumes, as illustrated in the figure below.

From the definition of isothermal compressibility, we obtain

\[\begin{equation*} \Delta V_{w} = c_{w} V_{pi} S_{wi} \Delta p \end{equation*}\]

The porous volume \(V_{pi}\) can be calculated from the initial oil porous volume \(V_{poi}\) and the initial gas porous volume \(V_{pgi}\):

\[\begin{equation} V_{pi} = V_{poi} + V_{pgi} = \frac{N B_{oi}}{1 - S_{woi}} + \frac{G B_{gi}}{1 - S_{wgi}} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} V_{pi} = \frac{N B_{oi}}{1 - S_{wi}} + \frac{G B_{gi}}{1 - S_{wi}} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \Delta V_{w} = c_{w} \left( \frac{N B_{oi}}{1 - S_{wi}} + \frac{G B_{gi}}{1 - S_{wi}} \right) S_{wi} \Delta p \end{equation}\]

Variation of porous volume. The variation in porous volume and connate water volume is

\[\begin{equation} \Delta V_{p} = V_{pi} c_{f} \Delta p \end{equation}\]

Substituting \(V_{pi}\), we find:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta V_{p} = \left( \frac{N B_{oi}}{1 - S_{wi}} + \frac{G B_{gi}}{1 - S_{wi}} \right) c_{f} \Delta p \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \Delta V_{p} = \left( 1 + m \right) N B_{oi} \left( \frac{c_{f}}{1 - S_{wi}} \right) \Delta p \end{equation}\]

Injected Gas Volume. This accumulated volume of injected gas is:

\[\begin{equation} G_{inj} B_{g} \end{equation}\]

Injected Water Volume. This volume corresponds to the total accumulated injected water, that is:

\[\begin{equation} W_{inj} B_{w} \end{equation}\]

Water Volume from Aquifer. The aquifer will act by bringing a certain volume of water into the oil and gas reservoir, as represented in Figure 51. In this case, it is necessary to determine this water influx (\(W_e\)).

We will see later some models for estimating \(W_e\). For now, let's assume it is possible to estimate it.

The material balance equation results in:

\[\begin{equation} \begin{split} N_{p} \left[ B_{o} + \left( R_{p} - R_{s} \right) B_{g} \right] + W_{p} B_{w} - G_{inj} B_{g} - W_{inj} B_{w} = \\ \\ N \left[ \left( B_{o} - B_{oi} + \left( R_{si} - R_{s} \right) B_{g} \right) + m N B_{oi} \left( \frac{B_{g}}{B_{gi}} - 1 \right) + \left( 1 + m \right) N B_{oi} \left( \frac{c_{f} + c_{w} S_{wi}}{1 - S_{wi}} \right) \Delta p \right] + W_e \end{split} \end{equation}\]

The above equation depends on various data that can be measured or correlated. With geological data, it is possible to estimate the original volumes of oil (N), gas cap (G), etc. Laboratory tests can estimate the formation volume factors (\(B_o\), \(B_g\), \(B_{oi}\), \(B_{gi}\)), solubility ratios (\(R_p\), \(R_s\)), and compressibilities (\(c_f\), \(c_w\)).

To simplify the solution, Rosa proposes that each term of the equation be defined as follows:

Term related to production and injection:

\[\begin{equation} F = N_{p} \left[ B_{o} + \left( R_{p} - R_{s} \right) B_{g} \right] + W_{p} B_{w} - G_{inj} B_{g} - W_{inj} B_{w} \end{equation}\]

Term related to the oil zone:

\[\begin{equation} E_{o} = B_{o} - B_{oi} + \left( R_{si} - R_{s} \right) B_{g} \end{equation}\]

Term related to the gas cap:

\[\begin{equation} E_{g} = B_{oi} \left( \frac{B_{g}}{B_{gi}} - 1 \right) \end{equation}\]

Term related to compressibilities:

\[\begin{equation} E_{f,w} = B_{oi} \left( \frac{c_{f} + c_{w} S_{wi}}{1 - S_{wi}} \right) \Delta p \end{equation}\]

Thus, equation 4.73 can be rewritten as:

\[\begin{equation} F = N \left[ E_{o} + m E_{g} + \left( 1 + m \right) E_{f,w} \right] + W_e \end{equation}\]

The terms \(F\), \(E_o\), \(E_g\), \(E_{f,w}\) can be calculated from operational data, and \(W_e\) (aquifer) can be correlated or modeled. In many situations, this equation can be represented by a straight line, adjusted by operational data that allow determining unknown parameters such as N or m, for example. ANALYSIS OF PRODUCTION DECLINE

The production curves of oil fields show a decline, which in some cases resembles an exponential drop.

For producing reservoirs, knowledge of the field (history of produced volume, pressure drop, variations in the formation volume factor, etc.) allows various inferences to predict its future behavior.

A very simplified approach is based on effective compressibility to relate the initial volume \(N_{i}\) and the produced volume \(N_{p}\):

\[\begin{equation*} c_{t} = \frac{\Delta V}{V} \frac{1}{\Delta p} = \frac{N_{p} B_{o}}{N_{i} B_{oi}} \frac{1}{(\bar{p}_{i} - \bar{p})} \end{equation*}\]

Where \(\bar{p}\) corresponds to the average reservoir pressure variation, \(\bar{p}_{i}\) is the initial average reservoir pressure at the beginning of the decline, and \(N_{i}\) is an initial estimate for oil in place in the drained area of the well. In this case, the produced volume can be expressed as:

\[\begin{equation*} N_{p} = \frac{c_{t} N_{i} B_{oi}}{B_{o}} \left( \bar{p}_{i} - \bar{p} \right) \end{equation*}\]

Volumetric decline methods are based on generating empirical correlations through the mathematical regression of produced volume data to predict future behavior. These models assume that the reservoir's past behavior will be maintained in the future, showing trends.

Assume that the production rate of a well begins to decline after reaching the critical bottomhole pressure \(p_{wc}\). In the pseudo-steady state, and considering that the reservoir has reached an average pressure \(\bar{p}\), the flow rate \(q\) after a certain time \(t\) of decline can be expressed as:

\[\begin{equation*} q = \frac{kh}{B_{o} \mu \left[ \ln \left( \frac{r_{e}}{r_{w}} \right) + s \right]} (\bar{p} - p_{wc}) \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{kh}{B_{o} \mu \left[ \ln \left( \frac{r_{e}}{r_{w}} \right) + s \right]} \frac{d \bar{p}_{t}}{dt} \end{equation*}\]

Deriving the expression for the accumulated oil production from the well \(N_{p}\), we obtain an estimate for the flow rate \(q\):

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{dN_{p}}{dt} = q = - \frac{c_{t} N_{i} B_{oi}}{B_{o}} \frac{d \bar{p}}{dt} \end{equation*}\]

It follows that:

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{d \bar{p}}{dt} = - \frac{q B_{o}}{c_{t} N_{i} B_{oi}} \end{equation*}\]

From the above equations, we can deduce that:

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{dq}{dt} = - \frac{kh}{\mu \left[ \ln \left( \frac{r_{e}}{r_{w}} \right) + s \right]} \frac{1}{c_{t} N_{i} B_{oi}} \, q \end{equation*}\]

If we define:

\[\begin{equation*} b = \frac{kh}{\mu \left[ \ln \left( \frac{r_{e}}{r_{w}} \right) + s \right]} \frac{1}{c_{t} N_{i} B_{oi}} \end{equation*}\]

Then:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{q} \frac{dq}{dt} = -b \end{equation}\]

In some situations, the assumptions made do not apply, leading to the development of a more generic equation of the form:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{q} \frac{dq}{dt} = -b \left( \frac{q}{q_{i}} \right)^{d} \end{equation}\]

Where the term \(-b \left( \frac{q}{q_{i}} \right)^{d}\) corresponds to the decline rate. Depending on the value of \(d\), different solutions are obtained. For \(d = 0\), we have exponential decline, for \(0 < d < 1\), we have hyperbolic decline, and for \(d = 1\), we have harmonic decline.

### Exponential Decline (d = 0):

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{q} \frac{dq}{dt} = b \cdot \left(\frac{q}{q_i}\right)^0 \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{q} \frac{dq}{dt} = b \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \int_{q_{i}}^{q_{t}} -\frac{dq}{q} = \int_{0}^{t} b dt \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \ln \left( \frac{q}{q_{i}} \right) = -b t \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q = q_{i} e^{-bt} \end{equation*}\]

The above equation shows that the production decline is exponential, as long as the initial assumptions hold.

### Hyperbolic Decline (0 \(<\) d \(<\) 1):

\[\begin{equation} \frac{-1}{q} \frac{dq}{dt} = b \left( \frac{q}{q_{i}} \right)^{d} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \int_{q_{i}}^{q} q^{-(d+1)} dq = \int_{0}^{t} \frac{-b}{q_{i}^{d}} dt \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{-1}{d} \left[q^{-d} - q^{-d}_i\right] = \frac{-b}{q^{d}_i}t \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \left[q^{-d} - q^{-d}_i\right] = \frac{b \cdot d \cdot t}{q^{d}_i} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{q^d} = \frac{b \cdot d \cdot t}{q^{d}_i} + \frac{1}{q^d_i} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q^d = \frac{q^d_i}{b \cdot d \cdot t + 1} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q = \frac{q_i}{\sqrt [d]{b \cdot d \cdot t + 1}} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q = \frac{q_{i}}{(1 + b d t)^{1/d}} \end{equation*}\]

### Harmonic Decline (d = 1):

\[\begin{equation*} q = \frac{q_{i}}{b t + 1} \end{equation*}\]

Most reservoirs exhibit hyperbolic behavior, and the assumptions for harmonic and exponential declines are extreme and rarely observed in practice. However, if a simplified model is needed for a field with limited data (such as an exploratory phase field), exponential decline can be used as an approximation. Decline models are applied both to gas and oil reservoirs.

Production Plateau Establishment. In offshore systems, it is common to have a first year where the oil production of a field gradually increases as wells are connected to the platform (ramp-up) and a plateau for several years, due to the maximum capacity of the processing plant, as shown in Figure 57.

To avoid production exceeding the processing plant's capacity, choke valves are used to limit the flow.

Volumetric decline models can be adjusted to incorporate the plateau. Consider the exponential decline model:

\[\begin{equation*} q = q_{i} e^{-bt} \end{equation*}\]

One way to account for the plateau assumes that the volume \(N_{p}\) produced between 0 and \(t_{p}\) must be equal to the volume produced by the exponential curve during this same time, as shown in Figure 58. We will ignore the initial ramp-up year.

After the plateau, the flow follows the decline curve, shifted in time. This curve shift is done by adding the time differential \(t^{*} - t_{p}\) to the decline equation, where \(t^{*}\) corresponds to the time when the flow (from the exponential curve) equals \(q_{p}\), and \(t_{p}\) corresponds to the duration of the plateau, as shown in the figure "Incorporating Plateau into the Volumetric Decline Curve (a)."

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{q_{p}}{q_{i}} = e^{-bt^{*}} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \ln \left( \frac{q_{p}}{q_{i}} \right) = -bt^{*} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} t^{*} = -\frac{1}{b} \ln \left( \frac{q_{p}}{q_{i}} \right) \end{equation*}\]

Since the volumes represented by the shaded areas in the figure "Incorporating Plateau into the Volumetric Decline Curve (a) and (b)" must be equal:

\[\begin{equation*} q_{p} t_{p} = \int_{0}^{t^{*}} q_{0} e^{-bt} dt \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q_{p} t_{p} = q_0 e^{-bt} \bigg|^{t^*}_0 = \frac{q_0}{b} \left( 1 - e^{-bt^{*}} \right) \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} t_{p} = \frac{1}{q_{p}} \left[ \frac{q_{0}}{b} \left( 1 - \frac{q_{p}}{q_{i}} \right) \right] \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} t_{p} = \frac{1}{b} \left( \frac{q_{i}}{q_{p}} - 1 \right) \end{equation*}\]

Finally, the curve for the time after the plateau will be:

\[\begin{equation*} q = q_{i} e^{-b(t - (t_{p} - t^{*}))} = q_{i} e^{-b(t - t_{p})} e^{-bt^{*}} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q = q_{i} e^{-b(t - t_{p})} \frac{q_{p}}{q_{i}} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q = q_{p} e^{-b(t - t_{p})} \end{equation*}\]

The new production curve will be:

\[\begin{equation*} q = q_{p}, \, 0 < t < t_{p} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} q = q_{p} e^{-b(t - t_{p})}, \, t_{p} < t < t_{f} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} t_{p} = \frac{1}{b} \left( \frac{q_{i}}{q_{p}} - 1 \right) \end{equation*}\]

If we want to calculate the total volume produced \(N_{p}\) by a field with exponential volumetric decline and plateau \(q_{p}\), we must perform the following integration: \[\begin{equation} N_{p} = \int_{0}^{t_{p}} q_{p} dt + \int_{t_{p}}^{t_{f}} q_{p} e^{-b(t - t_{p})} dt \end{equation}\] \[\begin{equation} N_{p} = q_{p} \left( t_{p} + \frac{1 - e^{-b(t_{f} - t_{p})}}{b} \right) \end{equation}\]

Example. Determine the volume produced over 30 years by a field where the oil production rate declines exponentially with \(b = \frac{0,2}{365} \frac{1}{d}\) and \(q_{i} = 150.000 \, \text{bpd}\). Assume a plateau of \(t_{p} = 4\) years with \(q_{p} = 100.000 \, \text{bpd}\).

Solution

\[\begin{equation*} N_{p} = 100.000 \left(4 \times 365 + \frac{1 - e^{-0,2(30 - 4)365}}{\frac{0,2}{365}} \right) = 328,5 \times 10^{6} \, \text{barrels} \end{equation*}\]

RESERVE ESTIMATION

Hydrocarbon reserves are the most important asset of an E&P company. This information forms the basis for the market value perception of the company.

The amount of recoverable hydrocarbons depends not only on specific characteristics of the reservoir but also on external factors such as oil prices, technologies used, etc., because reserves are economically viable volumes to produce. Publicly traded oil companies must not only prove the existence of hydrocarbons but also the technical and economic viability of producing them. With advances in the understanding of production mechanisms and technologies that increase the recovery factor of oil fields, companies can incorporate this additional oil into their reserves count, provided they demonstrate the technical and economic feasibility of applying these techniques.

International entities and regulatory agencies define criteria and rules for classifying reserves.

In Brazil, oil-producing companies must comply with regulations issued by the ANP, whose criteria are based on those of the Petroleum Resources Management System (SPE-PRMS) issued by the Society of Petroleum Engineers (SPE). Companies traded on the U.S. capital markets must comply with the classification of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). This ensures that investors can make an adequate assessment of different companies.

The criteria defined by the SEC are stricter than those disclosed by the ANP. In both cases, reserve classification allows the use of deterministic and probabilistic approaches for reservoir simulation to define recoverable oil volumes.

Proved Reserves. The ANP defines proved reserves as "the volumes of oil from known reservoirs that, through geological analysis and engineering data, can be estimated with reasonable certainty to be commercially recoverable from a given date and under existing economic conditions, operational methods, and government regulations."

If deterministic methods are used, the term reasonable certainty expresses a high degree of confidence that the quantities will be recovered. If probabilistic methods are used, it is recommended that there be at least a 90% (P90) probability that the actual recovered volumes will be equal to or greater than the estimate. It is important to note that, to be considered reserves, the volumes must be discovered, meaning there must be at least one well drilled confirming the presence of oil or gas.

Based on probabilistic analysis, the curve with a 90% probability can be used for the term "reasonable certainty." However, there is still a need to interpret the data and simulation results, and other criteria such as reservoir continuity or confirmation of the recovery method considered must also be analyzed. Depending on the assumptions made, the same field can have different P90 values.

The evaluation of the reservoir volume (thickness and area) is one of the critical items in reserve quantification. However, fluid contacts (between the top of the oil layer and the gas cap or between the base of the oil layer and the aquifer) are not always clearly defined during well drilling and logging. In such cases, pessimistic, expected, and optimistic contacts should also be considered for classifying different reserve volumes.

In general, reserves are considered proven if the reservoir's production capacity is supported by conclusive production or formation tests. Proven reserves can be assigned based on well logs and/or analysis that indicates the reservoir is analogous to reservoirs in the same area that are already producing or have demonstrated production capability through conclusive formation tests.

The area of a reservoir considered as proven includes: a) the area delineated by drilling and defined by fluid contact, if any; and b) the adjacent undrilled areas of the reservoir that can be technically considered as continuous to the drilled area and commercially productive, based on available geological and engineering data.

In the absence of data on fluid contacts, the lowest known hydrocarbon occurrence (in the case of oil/water contact) controls the proven limit, provided there is no indication to the contrary from geological, engineering, or production performance data.

For Proven Reserves, the recovery efficiency applied to the reservoirs must be based on: a) tests from a successful pilot project or favorable response from an installed program in the same reservoir or an analogous one with similar fluid properties that support the project basis; b) if there is reasonable certainty that the project will be implemented.

Unproven estimates can be based on reasonable alternative technical and commercial interpretations of the reservoir and/or project being evaluated. All alternative interpretations must be clearly documented, including comparisons with similar successful projects.

Probable Reserves are those unproven reserves that, based on geological and engineering data analysis, suggest a greater likelihood of being recoverable than not. When probabilistic methods are used, there is at least a 50% chance that the quantities actually recovered will be equal to or greater than the sum of proven and probable reserves.

Probable reserves may be associated with areas of the reservoir adjacent to the proven area, where data control or interpretations of the available data have greater uncertainty. Interpretations of reservoir continuity may not meet the reasonable certainty criterion.

Possible Reserves are those unproven reserves that, based on geological and engineering data analysis, suggest a lower likelihood of being recovered than probable reserves. When probabilistic methods are used, there is at least a 10% chance that the quantities actually recovered will be equal to or greater than the sum of proven, probable, and possible reserves.

Possible reserves may be associated with areas of the reservoir adjacent to the probable area, where data control or interpretations of the available data progressively have greater uncertainty. They may occur in areas where the geological and engineering data are insufficient to clearly define the area and vertical limits of the reservoir to define the commercial productivity of a project. They may also include incremental recoveries associated with recovery efficiencies beyond those considered for probable reserves.

Therefore, despite the advances in simulation and statistical methods for reserve quantification, these procedures still carry risks. Especially when assessing a company's total reserves, it must be noted that summing the P90 reserves of different fields tends to underestimate total reserves, while summing P10 reserves tends to overestimate total reserves. Only expected values of reserves should be used, as the sum of expected values equals the expected value of the sum. It is important to note that statistical concepts are valid when used many times. Thus, looking at a distribution like the one shown in the figure on page 22, we see that the expected value will only be achieved 10% of the time, implying that if we evaluate several similar fields, only 10% of them will be larger than the expected value. By multiplying the expected value by the number of fields, we obtain an estimate for the expected value of a company's or country's total reserves. This information should be supplemented by the standard deviation, as these estimates often have a wide prediction interval (high variance).

Below is a commonly used definition for reserves:

1P - Proven (P90 or P95)

2P - Proven + Probable (P50)

3P - Proven + Probable + Possible (P10 or P5)

Specialized companies certify the methods used for reserve estimation. The volume of reserves and their replacement index are extremely important factors in determining the "health" of an oil production company.
