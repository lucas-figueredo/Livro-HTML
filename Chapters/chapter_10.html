10.1 HEATING SYSTEM

The heat source of a platform usually comes from the exhaust gases of the compression system turbines or from power generation units. Heat recovery units, known as WHRUs (Waste Heat Recovery Units), transfer heat between the exhaust gases and the heating water in a closed system. To improve heat exchange efficiency, the heating fluid temperature is typically between 140°C and 180°C.

This system is pressurized at approximately 1300 kPa abs to ensure that water reaches temperatures above 100°C while remaining in the liquid phase. Such pressure is required to obtain hot water (liquid phase) at a temperature of 180°C.

Example

The expansion vessel of a heating system operates at a pressure of 13 bar abs to achieve a temperature of 180°C, which is required to supply hot water consumers (oil and gas heaters, among others). In case of an operational anomaly, such as a production shutdown, system pressure drops to 8 bar gauge. Under this condition, a temperature of 176°C is observed, but it is uncertain whether this favors or prevents vaporization (an event that increases operational safety risks). This condition can lead to a volume increase (vapor phase) of approximately 1000 times, potentially compromising equipment integrity.

Solution

At the expansion vessel’s operating pressure of 13 bar abs (12 bar g), the saturation temperature (start of vaporization) from the Steam Table is 190,7°C. Therefore, at 180°C, vaporization does not occur (an undesirable phenomenon).

However, under contingency conditions (production shutdown), at a pressure of 8 bar g, the vessel reaches a temperature of 176°C. According to the Steam Table, the saturation temperature is 174,5°C, which is lower than the observed temperature, indicating the formation of unwanted vapor.

Heavy oils are typically associated with high viscosities (requiring higher temperatures for separation) and low gas production (resulting in a small-scale compression system). Consequently, the thermal load available in the WHRUs of the heating water system may not be sufficient to heat the oil to the required temperature. In these cases, additional heating using furnaces may be necessary.

10.2 COOLING SYSTEM

The cooling system can be either an open or closed circuit. In an open system, the cooling fluid is seawater, which returns to the ocean after passing through heat exchangers where it absorbs heat. This system has a higher cost than the closed system because it requires special materials for heat exchangers. In small plants, this approach may be viable. However, as plants increase in complexity, the overall size of the cooling system and the number of cooling points also increase. In such cases, a closed system is preferred, where the cooling fluid is freshwater circulating in a closed loop, absorbing heat at various points in the process plant and transferring this heat to seawater. Freshwater allows for the use of lower-cost carbon steel. An expansion tank serves as a buffer for the system.

Unlike the heating system, the expansion tank in the cooling system operates at atmospheric pressure and has a vent to the environment. Its function is to allow for the replenishment of losses in the circulating freshwater. The following points are common users of cooling water:

Cooling Points in Classified Areas:1

  • TEG cooling after regeneration

  • Natural gas cooling after each compression stage

  • Vapor recovery unit

Cooling Points in Non-Classified Areas:

  • Bearing cooling

  • Turbogenerator cooling

  • Air conditioning unit

  • Instrument and service air compressor cooling

10.3 POWER GENERATION SYSTEM

The power generation system is designed to meet the electrical energy demands of UEP equipment, with the main consumers being compressors (primary, export, auxiliary, and gas injection) and pumps (injection, offloading, seawater intake, sulfate removal unit, cooling water system, heating water system, and oil transfer). Additionally, gas dehydration units use electrical heating for glycol regeneration.

The system consists of three main types: primary generation, emergency generation, and transient power system.

Primary Power Generation System: Designed to ensure electricity supply to all loads of the UEP. Generally, gas-fueled turbogenerators are used, which can also burn diesel fuel in emergencies.

Emergency Power Generation System: Must be capable of continuously supplying essential platform loads, such as ventilation and air conditioning, air compressor, emergency seawater intake pump, emergency cooling pump, etc. The system must be autonomous, start automatically, and assume the load within 45 seconds in case of main power failure. The generator should have a minimum operational autonomy of 18 hours without refueling. Diesel engine generators are typically used, with compressed air starting systems powered by dedicated diesel engines, and manual crank start options.

Transient Power System: Consists of batteries (uninterrupted power supply - UPS). This system maintains essential emergency services powered continuously, ensuring the safety of the UEP without interruptions during main power failure and subsequent emergency power generation startup. It also powers services that remain active after emergency generation shutdown, such as platform abandonment or evacuation scenarios.

In UEPs with capacities between 100 and 150 kbpd, there is a trend toward standardizing power generation with four 25 MW turbogenerators.

10.4 FIRE PROTECTION SYSTEM

The fire protection system consists of CO2, foam, and deluge systems to combat fires.

CO2 System: Designed to combat fires in enclosed areas by flooding them with CO2. It preserves equipment and is used in critical safety areas. The system should only be activated after a specific alarm and full evacuation of the area, due to the risk of asphyxiation.

Foam System: Used to extinguish fires in areas with flammable liquid spills, such as the helideck and oil processing areas.

Deluge System: Designed to cool potential fire sources in most platform areas, preventing structural collapse of vessels and pipelines. It consists of a seawater ring, continuously pressurized by jockey pumps, with distribution networks containing sprinklers, hydrant branches, and intake pumps.

10.5 INERT GAS SYSTEM

The inert gas used is primarily CO2, resulting from the stoichiometric combustion of fuel (diesel or gas). It is injected into cargo and slop tanks to maintain a safe atmosphere and pressure and facilitate purging. It is also used in ballast tanks, void spaces in the cargo area, and in emergency situations.

Although significant amounts of combustion gas (rich in CO2) are available from the power generation system, the inert gas generation system must be independent for operational safety.

The generated gases are seawater-washed to cool and remove sulfur oxides. Each cargo tank has independent inert gas supply and relief lines. Pressure and O2 concentration in the inert gas header are monitored and interlocked with the offloading system, potentially stopping cargo pumps.

Variables such as tank pressure and O2 content in the inert gas header are monitored and interlocked with the offloading system, which may result in the shutdown of cargo pumps.

10.6 RELIEF AND FLARE SYSTEM

The FPSO Flare System is designed based on the maximum gas flow rate under continuous or emergency burning conditions, without considering simultaneous events.

The flare system is designed to prevent the pressure of the FPSO systems from exceeding the maximum pressure of the equipment and accessories that compose them. Therefore, its function is to relieve pressure, disperse, and safely burn hydrocarbons from the Production Facilities in case of emergency or process disturbances. This system must be capable of burning any gas flow rate, from the minimum to the maximum design capacity, under continuous or emergency burning conditions. Additionally, it must allow for the flow of relieved and condensed liquid during depressurization, separating it from the gas before the latter is sent for combustion in the flare.

The main causes of overpressure considered in the FPSO Flare System design are: Blocked Discharge and Fire.

Fire is an overpressure cause that can only be minimized through a planned shutdown and the installation of depressurization devices. The external exposure of equipment to fire generates overpressure through two primary mechanisms: liquid vaporization and gas volume expansion.

The inadvertent closure of a manual valve at the discharge of a system or pressure vessel while operational can generate excessive pressure within the system or equipment. Any valve should be considered as potentially susceptible to inadvertent closure or opening.

To reduce system pressure during overpressure conditions, relief scenarios are considered, where Pressure Safety Valves (PSV) are used to lower the system pressure, and depressurization scenarios, where pressure reduction occurs through the opening of Blowdown Valves (BDV).
