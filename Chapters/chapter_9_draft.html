CHAPTER 9 GAS PROCESSING

In a typical offshore processing plant, gas from three-phase separators is sent to the gas processing system, consisting of compressors, heat exchangers, dehydration systems, and contaminant removal (H\({}_{2}\)S, CO\({}_{2}\), Hg) when these are present in the gas.

Compression aims to provide the gas with sufficient energy to flow from the production site to Natural Gas Processing Units (UPGN), where fractionation (separation into streams with different compositions) occurs. Typical discharge pressure values for compression systems in offshore plants located 100 to 300 km from the coast range between 100 and 300 kgf/cm\({}^{2}\). UPGNs operate at pressures between 40 and 70 kgf/cm\({}^{2}\). In cases where the arrival pressure is lower than the UPGN operating pressure, recompression is necessary.

Common contaminants include H\({}_{2}\)S, CO\({}_{2}\), Hg (mercury), and H\({}_{2}\)O. Treatment is the process of removing these contaminants from the gas. Dehydration is necessary to prevent hydrate formation in situations where the gas reaches low temperatures and high pressures. The removal of acid gases (sweetening) such as H\({}_{2}\)S and CO\({}_{2}\) is aimed at preventing equipment corrosion and meeting gas specifications for sale. Mercury must be removed when aluminum equipment is present, as in certain types of heat exchangers.

In gas systems, pressure drops are accompanied by temperature reductions, which can generate liquids. This phenomenon is known as the Joule-Thomson effect. An example is offshore gas-producing wells where pressure loss along the riser and across the choke valve causes very low temperatures at the plant inlet. Conversely, when pressure increases (such as in the compression system), the temperature rises.

In gas-liquid separation processes, gas streams are commonly defined by their molecular composition. Since the liquid phase accumulates a variety of components, it is common to group them as a single pseudo-component. For example, C5+ is defined as all hydrocarbons with a molecular weight equal to or above C5. The characterization of pseudo-components was presented in Chapter 2.

The richness of a gas is the sum of the molar percentages of heavier gas components starting from propane (C\({}_{3}\)+). The richer the gas, the greater the amount of liquids (condensates) generated during processing.

As a mixture of hydrocarbons (methane, ethane, propane, etc.), its physical state (liquid or gas) depends on pressure, temperature, and composition. The higher the pressure and the lower the temperature, the greater the tendency for liquid formation.

9.1 COMPRESSION SYSTEM

Figure 1 shows the typical configuration of a compression stage.

During the gas compression process, its temperature increases, making it necessary to cool it down. Heat exchangers perform this cooling to approximately 40°C after each compression stage, generating liquid (condensate and water), which is removed in scrubber vessels. In offshore systems, cooling is carried out using seawater.

9.1.1 Compression

To design a compression system, we need to predict the behavior of hydrocarbon mixtures at different pressures and temperatures. Therefore, theoretical models that assist in the energy balance are necessary.

There are several definitions of work. In gas processing, we are interested in the definition of energy transferred by force F, which acts over a distance \(\ell\) at the system boundaries. In industrial processes, work can be completely transformed into heat, but heat cannot be completely converted into work. Only an ideal, hypothetical process can be reversible, and in this case, the entropy variation \(S\) is zero; the process is called isentropic. In gas compression, the reversible work in a closed system (Figure 2a) is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} W_{\text{isentropic}} = \int -p dV \end{equation}\]

For open systems (Figure 2b), the work is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} W_{\text{isentropic}} = \int V dP \end{equation}\]

Reversible (\(\Delta S = 0\)) and adiabatic (Q = 0) processes serve as a reference for determining the efficiency of an industrial process, which is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} \eta = \frac{W_{\text{isentropic}}}{W_{\text{real}}} \end{equation}\]

The correction for real work is made by dividing the reversible work by the efficiency \(\eta\). For an ideal gas, internal energy and enthalpy are functions of temperature only. Starting from the ideal gas equation for one mole of substance, \(pV = RT\), the expression for molar enthalpy becomes:

\[\begin{equation} H = U + RT \end{equation}\]

The differential with respect to temperature is:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial H}{\partial T} dT = \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} dT + R dT \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} C_p dT = C_v dT + R dT \end{equation}\]

From which it follows that:

\[\begin{equation} C_p = C_v + R \end{equation}\]

Defining \(k = \frac{C_p}{C_v}\), we obtain:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{R}{C_V} = k - 1, \quad C_p = R \left(\frac{k}{k-1} \right) \end{equation}\]

The Figure 3 shows the values of \(k\) for different substances. Since natural gas is a mixture in which methane is the predominant component, the \(k\) value of natural gas should approximate that of methane. Thus, \(k = 1,25\) is a reasonable approximation.

For a gas mixture, the value of \(k\) can be estimated by:

\[\begin{equation} k = \frac{\sum y_i C_{p_i}}{\sum y_i C_{p_i} - R} \end{equation}\]

In an adiabatic compression process in a closed system with no heat exchange, internal energy corresponds to the work done on the fluid:

\[\begin{equation} dU = dW = -p dV \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} dU = C_V dT = -p dV \end{equation}\]

Substituting \(p = \frac{RT}{V}\):

\[\begin{equation} \frac{dT}{T} = -\frac{R}{C_V} \frac{dV}{V} \end{equation}\]

Integrating:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\frac{R}{C_V}} \end{equation}\]

The above equation can be rewritten as:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{k-1} \end{equation}\]

Using the ideal gas equation:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{{k-1}} = \left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}} \end{equation}\]

Comparing the two right-hand terms, we see that \(p_1 V_1^k = p_2 V_2^k = pV^k\). Thus, in processes with reversible work for an ideal gas, the product \(pV^k\) remains constant. For this type of process, the relationship between temperatures and pressures is:

\[\begin{equation} T_{2\text{isentropic}} - T_1 = T_1 \left[\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}} - 1\right] \end{equation}\]

where \(T_{2\text{isentropic}}\) represents the final temperature of a reversible, or isentropic, process. Multiplying by \(C_p\), we obtain an expression for the isentropic enthalpy variation:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta H_{\text{isentropic}} = C_p (T_{2\text{isentropic}} - T_1) = C_p T_1 \left[\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}} - 1\right] \end{equation}\]

Using the definition of \(C_p\) and correcting it with \(Z\) for a real gas:

\[\begin{equation} C_p = ZR \left(\frac{k}{k-1} \right) \end{equation}\]

which leads to:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta H_{\text{isentropic}} = ZR \left(\frac{k}{k-1} \right) T_1 \left[\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}} - 1\right] \end{equation}\]

Given a real compressor that receives gas at a certain pressure and temperature (\(p_1\) and \(T_1\)) at the suction side and delivers it at a certain discharge pressure and temperature (\(p_2\) and \(T_2\)), the enthalpy change \(\Delta H_S\) represents the energy required by a reversible and adiabatic (isentropic) compressor that receives the gas under the same conditions (\(p_1\) and \(T_1\)) and delivers it at the same discharge pressure (\(p_2\)), but at a lower temperature (\(T_{2\text{isentropic}} < T_2\)). Note that in this case, the discharge temperature of the real process will be higher than that of the isentropic process. In Figure 4, we observe that starting from the same suction pressure and temperature (\(p_1\) and \(T_1\)), both the isentropic and real compressors have the same discharge pressure \(p_2\), but their discharge temperatures are different. In the isentropic compressor, the discharge temperature is \(T_{2\text{isentropic}}\), while in the real compressor, the discharge temperature is \(T_2\).

The isentropic efficiency is determined according to the following equation:

\[\begin{equation} \eta_{\text{isentropic}} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{isentropic}}}{\Delta H} = \frac{C_p (T_{2S} - T_1)}{C_p (T_2 - T_1)} = \frac{\left( \frac{p_2}{p_1} \right)^{\frac{k-1}{k}} -1}{\frac{(T_2 - T_1)}{T_1}} \end{equation}\]

The discharge temperature can be determined by:

\[\begin{equation} T_d = T_s \left( \frac{p_d}{p_s} \right)^{\frac{k-1}{k} \times \frac{1}{\eta_{\text{polytropic}}}} \end{equation}\]

Compressor manufacturers often use the polytropic equation instead of the isentropic equation. The polytropic efficiency is based on a reversible but not adiabatic process. It seeks to relate the actual temperature increase to the pressure increase. In this case, the polytropic exponent \(n\) replaces \(k\).

\[\begin{equation} \frac{T_{2\text{polytropic}}}{T_1} = \left(\frac{p_2}{p_1} \right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}} \end{equation}\]

This exponent \(n\) is a function of the gas properties and the efficiency of the compression process. It is determined experimentally through compressor tests.

The enthalpy variation in a polytropic process is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta H_{\text{polytropic}} = ZR \left(\frac{n}{n-1} \right) T_1 \left[\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1} \right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}} -1\right] \end{equation}\]

Assuming that \(\Delta H_{\text{real}}\) can be approximated by:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta H_{\text{real}} = ZR \left(\frac{k}{k-1} \right) T_1 \left[\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1} \right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}} -1\right] \end{equation}\]

The polytropic efficiency can be estimated as:

\[\begin{equation} \eta_{\text{polytropic}} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{polytropic}}}{\Delta H} = \frac{\frac{n}{n-1}}{\frac{k}{k-1}} \end{equation}\]

From which it follows that:

\[\begin{equation} \eta_{\text{polytropic}} \frac{k}{k-1} = \frac{n}{n-1} \end{equation}\]

or

\[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{\eta_{\text{polytropic}}} \frac{k-1}{k} = \frac{n-1}{n} \end{equation}\]

The polytropic efficiency can be understood as the ratio between the ideal work performed by the compressor for compression (Figure 5), as if it were carried out through a series of isentropic processes with infinitesimal pressure increments, and the actual adiabatic work required for the same \(\Delta p\), due to internal losses.

The above equations allow us to determine the discharge temperature of a polytropic compression process:

\[\begin{equation} T_d = T_s \left(\frac{p_d}{p_s} \right)^{\frac{k-1}{k} \times \frac{1}{\eta_{\text{polytropic}}}} \end{equation}\]

where \(T_s\) is the absolute suction temperature, \(T_d\) is the absolute discharge temperature, \(p_D\) is the discharge pressure, and \(p_S\) is the suction pressure. The ratio between them is defined as the compression ratio \(RC\):

\[\begin{equation} RC = \frac{p_D}{p_S} \end{equation}\]

The entropy and enthalpy determination is obtained from:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta S = \Delta S^{GI} + S^{R} (T_d, p_d) - S^{R} (T_s, p_s) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \Delta S^{GI} = \ln \left(\frac{p_d}{p_s} \right) - \int_{T_s}^{T_d} \frac{C_p}{T} dT \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} W = \Delta H = \Delta H^{GI} + H^{R} (T_d, p_d) - H^{R} (T_s, p_s) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \Delta H^{GI} = \int_{T_s}^{T_d} \frac{C_p}{T} dT \end{equation}\]

From the Peng-Robinson state equation, residual entropy and enthalpy are derived as follows:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{S^{R}}{R} = \ln Z + \ln (1 - bp) + \frac{d \ln \alpha}{d \ln T_r} q_m I \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \frac{H^{R}}{RT} = \frac{d \ln \alpha}{d \ln T_r} q_m I + Z -1 \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \frac{d \ln \alpha}{d \ln T_r} = \frac{mT_r^{0,5}}{m(T_r^{0,5} -1) -1} \end{equation}\]

To determine the power required to drive the compressor, mechanical losses from gears, sealing systems, shaft transmission, etc., must be considered. The power can be calculated as follows:

\[\begin{equation} \text{Power} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{isentropic}} \cdot \dot{m}}{\eta_{\text{isentropic}} \cdot \eta_{\text{mec}}} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{polytropic}} \cdot \dot{m}}{\eta_{\text{polytropic}} \cdot \eta_{\text{mec}}} \end{equation}\]

\(\dot{m}\) = Mass or molar flow rate

The efficiency of the compression process depends on several factors. Generally, it tends to decrease as pressure increases. For \(\eta_{\text{polytropic}}\), the following values can be used:

\[\begin{equation*} \eta_{\text{polytropic}} = \begin{cases} \text{Alternative} & 0,65 \text{ to } 0,85 \\ \text{Centrifugal} & 0,6 \text{ (pressure } > 100 \text{ bar) to } 0,8 \text{ (pressure } < 40 \text{ bar)} \end{cases} \end{equation*}\]

The mechanical efficiency is typically:

\[\begin{equation*} \eta_{\text{mec}} = 0,95 \text{ to } 0,98 \end{equation*}\]

Due to the high flow rates involved, centrifugal compressors are the most common in oil production systems. For small flow rates, screw compressors are used.

There is a practical limit to gas discharge temperature due to the metallurgy used. Generally, it is desired to keep the temperature below 180°C, which imposes a limitation on the compression ratio. Therefore, in many cases, multiple compression stages are required to meet the compression demand.

9.1.2 Cooling

After compression, the gas is cooled to proceed to the next compression stage. When a hydrocarbon gas mixture is cooled, condensation occurs, mainly of the heavier components.

In general terms, the most commonly used heat exchangers in oil processing are shell-and-tube exchangers. Exchangers for gas streams require large heat exchange areas due to the low film coefficient of gases. Because gas streams are free of incrusting elements and because gas pressure losses are small, it is possible to use printed circuit heat exchangers, which are very compact and can reach very low temperatures. These are composed of plates whose millimeter channels are chemically etched onto aluminum or steel sheets, which are then inserted into the exchanger side by side. Subsequently, the equipment is heated to high temperatures (close to the melting point of the material), resulting in the accommodation of the metallic parts into a single block. They do not have joints or gaskets, and to open them, they must be cut; therefore, they should be used with clean fluids or those that can be chemically cleaned. They can have multiple inlets and outlets, allowing energy integration of different streams in the gas processing plant. Comparatively, printed circuit exchangers are much smaller than shell-and-tube exchangers, as seen in Figure 9.

Their use in offshore systems is not yet widespread. Shell-and-tube exchangers are still the norm. Heat exchangers are usually designed for a pressure loss of 0,5 to 1 bar.
9.1.3 Condensate Removal
Gas scrubbers (scrubbers or knock-out drums in English) are responsible for separating the condensate from gas streams and removing liquid droplets entrained by the gas. Liquid entrainment in gas can damage compressors by eroding their blades, degrade polymeric membranes, cause foaming in amine or glycol regeneration systems, etc. Gas entrainment in condensates can cause pump cavitation or carry water, overloading the oil treatment system.
These devices are designed to remove small amounts of liquid from gas streams. In streams with a predominance of gas (high GORs), gas-liquid separation is performed using vertical separators. This is because when there is little liquid volume, level indication in vertical vessels becomes easier. Thus, the scrubbers found in the compression system are vertical. These devices have level and pressure control, as represented in Figure 10.

As with three-phase separators presented in Chapter 8, it is common to use mist removal devices. The American standard API 12J recommends \(K = 0,055\) to \(0,107\) (m/s) for vessels without internals. For vessels with wire mesh, a typical value is \(0,07\) m/s.

As gas is compressed and loses heavier components, it becomes leaner. Consequently, the temperature at which this gas begins to liquefy decreases. This is referred to as a lower dew point. The leaner the gas, the lower its dew point and the lesser the tendency to form liquids, both water and hydrocarbons. The water dew point is a typical performance control and design specification parameter for dehydration systems, ensuring that no hydrates form in gas-lift or the gas export pipeline, as we will see later.

In compression processes like the one shown in Figure 11, where the gas is sent to a pipeline, there is no control over the hydrocarbon dew point, only water. In these cases, hydrocarbon condensation is not considered a problem since the Natural Gas Processing Units (NGPUs) receiving the gas are designed to handle both the gas and liquids coming from the pipeline.

Joule-Thomson Process
There are occasions when it is necessary to control the hydrocarbon dew point of the gas to avoid the formation of condensates that could damage the separation process, such as polymeric membranes used for CO\(_2\) separation or molecular sieves used for natural gas dehydration. These processes will be discussed later.
Among the dew point adjustment processes, the Joule-Thomson (J-T) process is one of the simplest. The Joule-Thomson effect results from a temperature drop in the gas due to pressure drop in a control valve (Figure 12); therefore, it is necessary that the gas stream be at the appropriate pressure. It is assumed that there is no (substantial) heat exchange with the environment, so the gas enthalpy remains constant. This is known as an isenthalpic expansion (\(\Delta H=0\)).

After expansion in the valve, the two-phase mixture flows into a vessel where phase separation occurs: the vapor phase, consisting of gas with a lower dew point than the feed gas, and the liquid phase of natural gas (NGL), which is usually sent for further fractionation. To maximize cooling of the feed gas, both the NGL and the processed gas streams can exchange heat with the feed. This optimization is called energy integration.
This process has limitations in cooling and, consequently, in liquefying heavy components and is therefore applied to lean natural gas.
When J-T is used to specify the hydrocarbon dew point for membrane protection, the discharge pressure of J-T is limited by the maximum operating pressure of the membranes, typically 60 bar.
Another dew point adjustment process is Simple Refrigeration, where propane is usually used as a refrigerant fluid in a process similar to household refrigerators. This process can reach temperatures as low as -40\(^{\circ}\)C. However, in offshore environments, where only a 5\(^{\circ}\)C dew point is required for membranes, J-T has been preferred for its simplicity. Most propane refrigeration applications are found in NGPUs, as we will see later.

9.2 Dehydration

As presented in Chapter 4, water dissolved in natural gas can lead to the formation of hydrates when it reacts with hydrate formers (methane, ethane, propane, isobutane, CO\({}_2\), and N\({}_2\)), especially under high pressure and low temperature conditions. These hydrates, also called "burning ice," have a flaky ice consistency and contain hydrocarbons in their structure. Their presence in facilities is highly undesirable as they can clog pipelines, equipment, and instruments, leading to production losses. Hydrates can form in export gas pipelines, gas lift injection lines or even in gas field production lines in low-temperature environments, such as the seabed or in cold regions of the planet. Figure 13 shows a blockage caused by the occurrence of hydrates in a pipeline. One of the most widely used techniques for unblocking hydrates is by depressurizing the lines, which means stopping the well’s production, which is always undesirable.

Prevention of hydrates is achieved through gas dehydration. One of the most widely used methods in natural gas processing is absorption with glycol, particularly Triethylene Glycol (TEG) and Monoethylene Glycol (MEG). Molecular sieves and chemical additives such as methanol or ethanol, which act as thermodynamic inhibitors, are also used.

Glycols and molecular sieves can be regenerated. In contrast, methanol and ethanol are not recovered and are generally used as a complementary step to account for inefficiencies in glycol dehydration units.

9.2.1 Glycol – Absorption

In glycol-based dehydration, water is removed via physical absorption (no chemical reaction). An absorber tower contacts the gas stream with glycol, which absorbs water. The glycol containing absorbed water, called rich glycol, is sent to a regeneration tower, where it is heated to remove water and hydrocarbons. The regenerated lean glycol returns to the absorber. Regeneration temperatures are limited by the glycol degradation temperature as per the table in Chapter 4.

In Figure 14 we can see, in summary, a glycol dehydration unit composed of an absorber and regenerator typical of a TEG system.

The absorption process relies on the affinity of the liquid phase for a component in the gas phase — in this case, water with TEG. Regeneration is the inverse process, in which water is removed from the glycol through heating, pressure reduction, and injection of dry gas (stripping).

The minimum TEG concentration required to dehydrate the inlet gas is determined from TEG-water equilibrium data using as input variables the gas-TEG contact temperature and water dew point in the gas. Water dew point data in natural gas are presented graphically in Chapter 2.

To maximize absorption, a substance with a high attraction to water is sought, as is the case of TEG. In this case, the solute (H\(_2\)O) is very soluble in the liquid phase (TEG). Its representation is seen in the graph y (mole fraction in the gas phase) versus x (mole fraction in the liquid phase) in Figure 15. When the curve approaches a straight line, then equilibrium can be approximated by Henry’s equation.

\[\begin{equation} y = Kx \end{equation}\]

Many authors define Henry’s equation as being y = Hx but in this text we chose to use K instead of H in order to maintain coherence with the definitions already made for liquid-vapor equilibrium.

Consider a natural gas stream \(V_{2}\) from which we want to remove water using a liquid TEG stream \(L_{0}\). We can establish total and component mass balances (for water, for example) as shown in Figure 16.

Total mass balance: \[\begin{equation} L_{0} + V_{2} = L_{1} + V_{1} \end{equation}\]

Component balance for TEG: \[\begin{equation} L_{0} x_{0} + V_{2} y_{2} = L_{1} x_{1} + V_{1} y_{1} \end{equation}\]

If equilibrium occurs between the liquid and gas phases, we can use the equation: \[\begin{equation} y_{1} = K x_{1} \end{equation}\]

As the amount of water transferred between phases is small, we can consider that the solvent flow rates remain constant: \(L_{0} = L_{1} = L_{s}\) and \(V_{1} = V_{2} = V_{s}\). For very low water concentrations in the liquid or vapor phase, it’s acceptable to substitute mole fractions by molar ratios: \[\begin{equation} X = \frac{x}{1 - x} \end{equation}\] \[\begin{equation} Y = \frac{y}{1 - y} \end{equation}\]

\(Y\) = mol of water in gas stream per mol of solute-free gas
\(X\) = mol of water in liquid stream per mol of solute-free liquid

In this case, we should use the component balance, as the total balance yields the trivial identity \(L + V = L + V\): \[\begin{equation} L_{s} X_{0} + V_{s} Y_{2} = L_{s} X_{1} + V_{s} Y_{1} \end{equation}\]

From this, we derive the operating line: \[\begin{equation} Y_{2} = \frac{L_{s}}{V_{s}} X_{1} + \frac{V_{s} Y_{1} - L_{s} X_{0}}{V_{s}} \end{equation}\]

Example: A gas stream of \(V_{s} = 100\) kmol/h at 200 bar must meet a water content specification of 100 mg/std m³. We have 0,24 kmol/h (\(L_{s}\) = 0,24) of lean glycol (assumed water-free, \(X_{0} = 0\)) for dehydration. Determine the maximum gas concentration \(Y_{2}\) and temperature using the chart for saturated water content in natural gas. Assume equilibrium constant \(K = 0,001\) and negligible mass transfer resistance.

Solution: There are two unknowns: \(Y_{2}\) and \(X_{1}\). From equilibrium: \[\begin{equation*} 100\,\text{mg/m}^{3} \Rightarrow Y_{1} = 0,000131 \end{equation*}\] \[\begin{equation*} X_{1} = \frac{Y_{1}}{K} = \frac{0,000131}{0,001} = 0,131 \end{equation*}\] \[\begin{equation*} Y_{2} = \frac{0,24}{100} \times 0,131 + \frac{100 \times 0,000131}{100} = 0,00049 = 376\,\text{mg/std m}^{3} \end{equation*}\]

To meet the 100 mg/std m³ water specification, the inlet stream must not exceed 376 mg/std m³. From the saturation chart, at 200 bar, this corresponds to approximately 30°C.

If a higher inlet temperature is desired, water content increases and more water must be removed. We can use multiple flash vessels in series (Figure 17):

Let’s determine \(Y_{3}\). Assume \(K = 0,001\) and same gas specification: \[\begin{equation} L_{s} X_{0} + V_{s} Y_{3} = L_{s} X_{2} + V_{s} Y_{1} \end{equation}\] \[\begin{equation} Y_{3} = \frac{L_{s}}{V_{s}} X_{2} + \frac{V_{s} Y_{1} - L_{s} X_{0}}{V_{s}} \end{equation}\]

From previous calculation, \(Y_{2} = 376\) mg/std m³ implies \(X_{2} = 0,494\): \[\begin{equation*} Y_{3} = 0,0284 = 2162\,\text{mg/std m}^{3} \end{equation*}\]

The gas stream was reduced from 2162 mg/std m³ to 376 mg/std m³, then to 100 mg/std m³ through two flash separators. This assumes perfect equilibrium, which is idealized. We define an equilibrium stage as this ideal behavior within a vessel.

Instead of multiple vessels, one can use a single tower with perforated trays, allowing the gas to make contact with the liquid, bettering its performance.

To determine the number of equilibrium stages and the composition of the outlet streams from each vessel, it is necessary to use the K constants. In the given example, a single K value was considered, but in practice, it depends on pressure, temperature, and composition. This process (either absorption or regeneration) can be optimized by manipulating these variables. To improve absorption, higher pressures and lower temperatures are desirable, which is why a cooler is commonly used, as seen in Figure 1.

Using these concepts, towers are designed for various purposes. A representation of a tower with \(N\) stages is shown in Figure 2.

To determine the number of equilibrium stages, Kremser and Brown (1930) started with a mass balance between stages \(n+1\) and \(N\) to derive a useful correlation. From the figure above, we have:

\[\begin{equation} LX_n + VY_{N+1} = LX_N + VY_{n+1} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} V(Y_{n+1} - Y_{N+1}) = L(X_n - X_N) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} K = \frac{Y}{X} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} A = \frac{L}{KV} \end{equation}\]

where \(A\) is the absorption factor, considered constant throughout the tower. Then:

\[\begin{equation} Y_{n+1} - Y_{N+1} = \frac{L}{V}(X_n - X_N) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} Y_{n+1} - \frac{L}{V} X_n = Y_{N+1} - \frac{L}{V} X_N \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} Y_{n+1} - A Y_n = Y_{N+1} - AK X_N \end{equation}\]

The equation above can be rewritten as a discrete difference equation:

\[\begin{equation} (D - A)Y_n = Y_{N+1} - AKX_N \end{equation}\]

where \(D\) is the differential operator such that:

\[\begin{equation} DX_n = X_{n+1} \end{equation}\]

The characteristic equation \(D - A = 0\) has the solution \(D = A\). Solving the equation (Treybal) and setting \(n = N\), we get:

\[\begin{equation} N = \frac{\ln \left( \frac{Y_{N+1} - KX_0}{Y_1 - KX_0} \left(1 - \frac{1}{A} \right) + \frac{1}{A} \right)}{\ln A} \quad A \neq 1 \end{equation}\]

Defining absorption efficiency \(E_a\) as:

\[\begin{equation} E_a = \frac{Y_{N+1} - Y_1}{Y_{N+1} - KX_0} = \frac{A^{N+1} - A}{A^{N+1} - 1} \end{equation}\]

the equation can be rewritten as:

\[\begin{equation} N = \frac{\ln \left(\frac{E_a - A}{A(E_a - 1)} \right)}{\ln A} \end{equation}\]

Where:

  • \(Y_{N+1}\) = moles of water in the rich gas stream per mole of rich gas entering the tower

  • \(Y_1\) = moles of water in the lean gas stream per mole of lean gas exiting the tower

  • \(KX_0\) = moles of water in the gas stream in equilibrium with the lean solvent per mole of rich gas entering the tower

  • \(A = \frac{L}{KV}\) or \(A = \frac{L_0}{KV_{N+1}}\)

  • \(V_{N+1}\) = total moles of rich gas entering the tower

  • \(L_0\) = moles of liquid entering the tower

The efficiency equation represents the fraction of the component that is effectively removed relative to the maximum that could have been removed if equilibrium were achieved.

For stripping (regeneration), the design procedure follows the same equations as absorption, with necessary adjustments:

\[\begin{equation} N = \frac{\ln \left(\frac{X_0 - X_N}{X_0 - \frac{Y_{N+1}}{K}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{A} \right) + A \right)}{\ln A} \end{equation}\]

The stripping process efficiency equation is:

\[\begin{equation} E_e = \frac{X_0 - X_N}{X_0 - \frac{Y_{N+1}}{K}} = \frac{S^{N+1} - S}{S^{N+1} - 1} \end{equation}\]

Where:

  • \(\frac{Y_{N+1}}{K}\) = moles of component in equilibrium with the gas stream per mole of liquid entering the tower

  • \(S = \frac{KV}{L}\) or \(S = \frac{KV_{N+1}}{L_0}\)

These equations are known as the Kremser and Brown equations. Figure 3 shows their graphical representation.

Treybal demonstrates that the Kremser and Brown equations are valid for both mass fractions (\(Y\), \(X\)) and mole fractions (\(y\), \(x\)).

Packed Towers

Alternatively, packings (structured or random) can be used instead of trays, as shown in Figure 5 (a). There are several correlations in the literature (Navaes), specifically developed by suppliers, that provide the packing height equivalent to one equilibrium stage. The standard representation for a packed column is shown in Figure 5 (b).

Let us now examine the calculations related to the packing height \(z\).

Let \(V\) and \(L\) be the molar flow rates of gas and liquid, respectively, at a point inside the tower as shown in Figure 6. Assuming steady-state, the mass balance over \(dz\) yields:

\[\begin{equation} V + L = V + dV + L + dL \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} dV = dL \end{equation}\]

For a component:

\[\begin{equation} d(Vy) = d(Lx) \end{equation}\]

Integrating from the base of the tower to any height:

\[\begin{equation} \int_{V_1 y_1}^{Vy} d(Vy) = \int_{L_1 x_1}^{L_2 x_2} d(Lx) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} Vy - V_1 y_1 = Lx - L_1 x_1 \end{equation}\]

The equation above is known as the operating line. From it, we obtain:

\[\begin{equation} Vy = Lx + V_1 y_1 - L_1 x_1 \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} y = \frac{L}{V}x + \frac{V_1 y_1 - L_1 x_1}{V} \end{equation}\]

In the process of H2O absorption from a natural gas stream, the water concentration is very low. The mass transfer does not significantly affect the values of \(L\) (glycol) and \(V\) (natural gas), making it convenient to perform calculations for the dry natural gas and glycol flow rates, \(V_s\) and \(L_s\), respectively. Thus, letting \(V_s\) be the flow rate of pure natural gas (without water), we have:

\[\begin{equation} V_s = V(1 - y), \quad V = \frac{V_s}{(1 - y)}, \quad Vy = \frac{V_s y}{(1 - y)} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} d(Vy) = d\left(\frac{V_s y}{1 - y}\right) = V_s d\left(\frac{y}{1 - y}\right) = V_s \frac{dy}{(1 - y)^2} = V \frac{dy}{1 - y} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} d(Vy)=V \frac{dy}{1 - y} \end{equation}\]

Let \(d\text{Vol} = Sdz\) be a differential volume element of the tower. The area available for mass transfer is:

\[\begin{equation} d(a\, \text{Vol}) = a' Sdz \end{equation}\]

\(S\) corresponds to the cross-sectional area of the tower and depends on its diameter, i.e., \(S = \frac{\pi D^2}{4}\), and \(a'\) is the specific area for mass transfer, i.e., the interfacial gas-liquid area per unit volume. The value of \(a'\) depends on the type of packing and the amount of liquid present. Defining \(a = a'S\), the mass transfer in the gas phase over an infinitesimal volume element is given by:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{d(Vy)}{adz} = N_A = k_{oy}(y^* - y) \end{equation}\]

where \(N_A\) is the molar flux of species A per unit area.

Substituting into the previous equation:

\[\begin{equation} d(Vy) = V \frac{dy}{1 - y} = k_{oy} a (y^* - y) dz \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} dz = \frac{V}{k_{oy} a} \frac{dy}{(1 - y)(y^* - y)} \end{equation}\]

Multiplying and dividing by \((1 - y)_{ml}\):

\[\begin{equation} \int_0^z dz = \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{V}{k_{oy} a} \cdot \frac{1}{(1 - y)_{ml}} \cdot \frac{(y - y)_{ml}}{(1 - y)(y^* - y)} dy \end{equation}\]

It is demonstrated (Foust, 1982) that in practical cases, the first term of the integral varies little with composition and can thus be taken outside the integral:

\[\begin{equation} z = \underbrace{\frac{V}{k_{oy} a} \cdot \frac{1}{(1 - y)_{ml}}}_{H_{OG}} \underbrace{\int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{(y - y)_{ml}}{(1 - y)(y^* - y)} dy}_{N_{OG}} \end{equation}\]

The first term corresponds to the height of a global gas-phase transfer unit, \(H_{OG}\), and the second to the number of global gas-phase transfer units, \(N_{OG}\):

\[\begin{equation} H_{OG} = \frac{V}{k_{oy} a} \cdot \frac{1}{(1 - y)_{ml}} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{OG} = \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{(y - y)_{ml}}{(1 - y)(y^* - y)} dy \end{equation}\]

According to Weigand: \[\begin{equation*} (1 - y)_{ml} \approx \frac{(1 - y) + (1 - y^*)}{2} \end{equation*}\]

Thus:

\[\begin{equation} N_{OG} = \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{(1 - y) + (1 - y^*)}{2(1 - y)(y^* - y)} dy = \frac{1}{2} \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{(1-y^*)+(1-y)+(1-y)}{(1 - y)(y^*-y)} dy \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} =\frac {1}{2}\int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{1}{(1-y)}dy + \frac{1}{2}\int_{y_1}^{y_2}\frac{1}{(y^*-y)}dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{y_1}^{y_2}\frac{1}{(y^*-y)}dy \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{OG} = \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{1}{y^* - y} dy + \frac{1}{2} \ln \left(\frac{1 - y_2}{1 - y_1} \right) \end{equation}\]

For dilute solutions:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{1 - y_2}{1 - y_1} = 1 \Rightarrow N_{OG} = \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \frac{1}{y^* - y} dy \end{equation}\]

From the operating line:

\[\begin{equation} dy = \frac{L}{V} dx \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{OG} = \frac{L}{V} \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{1}{x^* - x} dx \end{equation}\]

It can be shown (see Appendix) that:

\[\begin{equation} N_{OG} = \frac{\ln \left[\frac{y_1 - Kx_2}{y_2 - Kx_2} (1 - A) + A \right]}{1 - \frac{1}{A}} \end{equation}\]

where \(A = \frac{L}{KV}\). For stripping, the corresponding equation is:

\[\begin{equation} N_{OL} = \frac{\ln \left[\frac{x_2 - y_1/K}{x_1 - y_1/K} (1 - A) + A \right]}{1 - A} \end{equation}\]

Figure 7 shows the previous equations graphically.

The above equation relates \(N_{OG}\) and \(A\) with the inlet and outlet concentrations (of the absorbed component) in the tower.

The tower height is then the product:

\[\begin{equation} h = N_{OG} H_{OG} \end{equation}\] or \[\begin{equation} h = N_{OL} H_{OL} \end{equation}\]

For the regeneration tower, the design procedure is based on the same equations shown for absorption with the necessary adjustments. The graphs of \(N_{OG}\) versus \(A\) can be used as presented here, provided that the value of \(N_{OL}\) is used on the ordinate axis.

As the first towers used were tray towers, the development of design criteria was based on calculations of the number of equilibrium stages and the number of equivalent stages associated with tray efficiency. For packed towers, the concept of height equivalent to a theoretical plate (HETP) was adopted. Thus, the required packing height is determined by:

\[\begin{equation} z = HETP \cdot N \end{equation}\]

Determining HETP is not a trivial task since a packed tower is a continuous contact device and the mass balance must be carried out through a differential element of packing. Although there is much literature attempting to provide correlations, it is often more effective to use data from existing operational plants. Some companies operate pilot plants to generate specific correlations.

Therefore, just like in tray tower sizing, the height of packed towers is determined by calculating the number of equilibrium stages and then applying corrections for the height equivalent of structured packings.

The relation between the number of transfer units \(N_{OG}\) and the number of theoretical stages \(N\) can be calculated using the following relation (Treybal):

\[\begin{equation} \frac{N}{N_{OG}} = \frac{1 - \frac{1}{A}}{\ln(A)} \end{equation}\]

Tower Diameter

The tower diameter depends on the gas flow rate, as an ideal gas velocity must be maintained. To determine the tower diameter, we use an equation similar to that presented in Chapter 7:

\[\begin{equation} v_{\text{MAX}} = K \sqrt{\frac{\rho_L - \rho_G}{\rho_G}} \end{equation}\]

Where \(K\) depends on the type of contactor. For structured packing, an initial estimate is \(0{,}09 < K < 0{,}105\) m/s.

An alternative to calculate the diameter is:

\[\begin{equation} v_{\text{MAX}} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{\rho_G g}} \end{equation}\]

A heat source is required in the tower reboiler. Offshore systems use electric heaters, while onshore systems (gas processing plants) use thermal oil.

As we go down the column, the equilibrium constant of water in the TEG-water system increases, since the water fraction in the system increases and the TEG concentration decreases. Similarly, the L/V ratio increases since water is transferred from the gas phase (decreasing \(V\)) to the liquid phase (increasing \(L\)). As both effects happen simultaneously, they cancel each other, keeping the absorption factor constant, which validates the previously developed equations.

Typical values for the TEG circulation rate, which approach the economic optimum, range between 15 and 40 liters of TEG per kg of water absorbed. Higher values result in very large regenerators, high energy consumption, and increased absorption of aromatics. On the other hand, lower values require taller absorber columns.

In fixed platforms, the contactors used in the absorber column are trays (more commonly bubble cap trays), while in floating platforms structured packing is used due to its lower sensitivity to movement and reduced liquid flow requirements.

Dehydration units are installed after the 2nd or 3rd compression stage, where the pressure is higher than in the 1st stage. This is because at higher pressures the gas volume is smaller, which reduces the unit size. The choice between the 2nd or 3rd stage should be made after a techno-economic analysis, as higher pressures may require thicker-walled but smaller equipment. The decision depends on pressure levels in each stage. Other operational aspects should also be considered. Dehydration at pressures above the critical point avoids condensate or foam formation in the bed. Moreover, at higher pressures, there is less water to remove, reducing the required TEG flow rate and consequently the regenerator size.

9.2.2 Molecular Sieves – Adsorption

Molecular sieves are used when extremely low water content specifications must be met. They have also been used in offshore production systems with high CO2 content, where CO2 is often reinjected to avoid atmospheric release and enhance oil recovery by acting as a diluent. However, due to the high CO2 concentration and the high pressures required for reinjection into the reservoir, the corrosiveness of this stream can be significant in the presence of water. Dehydrating this gas stream to very low water content (around 1 ppm) is an alternative to using expensive corrosion-resistant materials in pipelines, flexible lines, and injector well columns. This level of dehydration is unachievable with conventional technologies like TEG. In such cases, physical adsorption technology using solids such as molecular sieves or silica gel is applied.

Molecular sieves can dehydrate gas to water contents as low as 1 ppm. These are aluminosilicate crystals whose crystalline structure acts as a sieve, allowing contaminant molecules (such as water) of smaller diameter to be retained, while larger hydrocarbon molecules pass through.

Molecular sieves are substances whose crystalline structures (Figure 30) have pores appropriately sized for the physical adsorption of small polar molecules such as water, H2S, or CO2. They are formed from sodium or calcium aluminosilicates (e.g., Na12[(AlO2)(SiO2)]). By varying the relative amounts of components, their crystalline structure and adsorption capacity can be modified. Therefore, there are different types and formulations of sieves with various selectivities. However, since CO2, H2O, and H2S molecules have similar sizes and polarities, they compete during adsorption, affecting performance.

The dehydration process using molecular sieves is reversible, allowing sieve regeneration, typically done by passing hot dry gas. It is a cyclic process in which saturation occurs after a period of operation (e.g., 8, 12, 16 hours). After this period, a heated gas stream (up to 290°C) passes through the sieve to regenerate it in a process known as desorption (removal of water adhered to the sieve surface).

Figure 31 shows a complete molecular sieve dehydration system. The unit consists of three adsorption towers, two operating and one regenerating.

The gas to be dehydrated is first cleaned in vessels and coalescing filters to remove droplets that could contaminate the sieve, then flows downward through the sieve bed in pressure vessels. At the outlet, the gas is dehydrated and proceeds to the next system after being filtered to retain fine sieve particles.

After the sieve saturation period, the bed is isolated for regeneration.

Figure 32 shows an example of a 12-hour dehydration cycle with 6 hours for regeneration, cooling, and standby. The dark segments represent regeneration, cooling, and standby, while the light segments represent dehydration.

In the example, the regeneration gas stream is a portion of already dehydrated gas, which must be compressed to overcome system pressure losses. The regeneration gas is heated and flows upward through the sieve during a period sufficient to remove the adsorbed water. In offshore systems, heating is usually electric, while in onshore units (e.g., gas processing units), heating is achieved with a furnace and thermal fluid in a closed loop.

Since regeneration occurs at high temperatures, the gas is cooled after leaving the sieve, passes through a knockout vessel to collect the condensed liquid, and returns to the dehydration unit inlet.

If the regeneration gas contains CO2 and H2S, special attention must be paid to the reaction (catalyzed by Al2O3) forming COS and H2O.

The adsorption rate of a gas solute depends on diffusion to the surface of solid adsorbent particles, diffusion into internal pores, the interaction at the gas-solid interface, and diffusion of the adsorbed solute into the solvent (for reversible reactions). In this case, the solute is water and the solvent is natural gas.

In adsorption, the mass transfer rate is finite, and only part of the solute is adsorbed in the upper bed layer. As gas flows, some solute advances downstream before the upstream layer saturates. Since adsorption rate decreases as saturation is approached, this effect becomes more pronounced over time. After saturation, the bed layer no longer performs adsorption. Consequently, three zones are formed: an equilibrium zone, an adsorption zone, and an active zone. The adsorption zone features a concentration gradient, as shown in Figure 33.

When the mass transfer zone reaches the end of the bed, the solute concentration in the gas outlet stream increases following this concentration profile.

This curve, called the breakthrough curve, can be obtained by continuously measuring the solute concentration. In industrial plants, this is not feasible as it would result in off-spec gas. Once the outlet stream concentration reaches the allowed limit, the bed must be regenerated. Therefore, a second bed is needed to treat the gas while the first is being regenerated. Suppliers perform lab tests to obtain the breakthrough curve, but such tests must be evaluated for their dynamic similarity to the actual industrial process. From this curve, it is possible to estimate the length of the adsorption zone.

Let

  • \(V_s\): solute-free gas flow rate in kg/s

  • \(Y_1\): solute mass per mass of solute-free gas

  • \(\rho_b\): bulk density of adsorbent in kg/m3

  • \(X_1\): solute mass per mass of adsorbent in equilibrium with the gas

To determine the adsorption zone, a low concentration value \(Y_b\) is used. At this solute concentration, the accumulated mass in the outlet stream is \(M_b\). The bed saturation concentration \(Y_{sat}\) is close to the inlet stream concentration \(Y_1\), corresponding to accumulated mass \(M_{sat}\). It’s reasonable to adopt \(Y_b = 0{,}05 Y_1\) and \(Y_{sat} = 0{,}95 Y_1\).

The adsorption zone can be defined as the bed region in which the concentration varies from \(Y_b\) to \(Y_{sat}\). This zone can be considered constant if the heat of adsorption is low (isothermal adsorption), the equilibrium curve is concave upward, and the feed concentration is dilute. These conditions are met in natural gas dehydration using molecular sieves. Thus, assuming a constant adsorption zone, we seek to determine the breakthrough curve.

The residence time of any section of the adsorption zone in the bed is:

\[\begin{equation} t_s = \frac{M_s}{V_s} \end{equation}\]

As a certain time is required to form the adsorption zone at the beginning of operation, we subtract it from the total time. Then, \(t_s - t_f\) represents the time the zone travels through the bed after forming. The accumulated mass during the saturation interval is:

\[\begin{equation} M_a = M_s - M_b \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} t_a = \frac{M_a}{V_s} \end{equation}\]

The bed depth will be traversed after \(\frac{t_s - t_f}{t_a}\) shifts of length \(z_a\):

\[\begin{equation} z = z_a \cdot \frac{t_s - t_f}{t_a} \end{equation}\]

or:

\[\begin{equation} z_a = z \cdot \frac{t_a}{t_s - t_f} \end{equation}\]

The solute mass in the adsorption zone corresponds to:

\[\begin{equation*} \int_{M_b}^{M_s} Y \, dM \end{equation*}\]

The final section of the bed could still adsorb the mass related to the difference up to \(Y_1\):

\[\begin{equation*} \int_{M_b}^{M_s} (Y_1 - Y) \, dM \end{equation*}\]

The unused adsorption capacity in this region as a fraction of total capacity is:

\[\begin{equation*} f = \frac{\int_{M_b}^{M_s} (Y_1 - Y) \, dM}{M_a Y_1} \end{equation*}\]

Then \(t_f\) is given by:

\[\begin{equation*} t_f = (1 - f)t_a \end{equation*}\]

We can now express the adsorption zone length as:

\[\begin{equation} z_a = z \cdot \frac{M_a}{M_s - (1 - f)M_a} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} F = z \cdot \frac{(z - z_a)A \rho_b X_1 + z_a A \rho_b (1 - f)X_1}{z A \rho_b X_1} = \frac{z - f z_a}{z} \end{equation}\]

The adsorption process is analogous to packed-bed absorption presented earlier. To reuse the methodology, we consider a moving coordinate system with the adsorption zone stationary (Figure 35). In this model, the solid bed moves vertically at the right velocity to keep the adsorption zone fixed. The solute-free gas stream \(V_s\) flows countercurrent. The solid stream \(L_s\) leaving the tower is in equilibrium with the incoming gas. To maintain similarity with actual operation, we assume the bed exiting the tower top is in equilibrium with the incoming gas, and the gas leaving the bottom has zero concentration (all solute removed). This implies an infinite tower—a simplification used because the focus here is on the adsorption zone length and endpoint concentrations.

The operating line can be obtained from the mass balance between the tower base and an intermediate position in the adsorption zone (dashed line in the figure):

\[\begin{equation} L_s X_2 + V_s Y = L_s X + V_s Y_2 \end{equation}\]

As \(Y_2\) and \(X_2\) are zero:

\[\begin{equation} V_s Y = L_s X \end{equation}\]

Mass transfer in the gas phase over an infinitesimal volume element is:

\[\begin{equation} d(V_s Y) = K_Y a(Y - Y^*) \, dz \end{equation}\]

whose integration between 0 and \(z_a\) yields:

\[\begin{equation} z_{a} = \int_{0}^{z_{a}} dz = \frac{V_{s}}{K_{y} a} \int_{Y_{b}}^{Y_{s}} \frac{dY}{(Y - Y^{*})} = H_{OG} N_{OG} \end{equation}\]

Integrating between 0 and an intermediate position within the adsorption zone, where the concentration is \(Y\), we get:

\[\begin{equation} z_{Y} = \int_{0}^{z_{a}} dz = \frac{V_{s}}{K_{y} a} \int_{Y_{b}}^{Y} \frac{dY}{(Y - Y^{*})} \end{equation}\]

Assuming that \(H_{OG} = \frac{V_{s}}{K_{y} a}\) remains constant despite changes in concentration, we have:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{z_{Y}}{z_{a}} = \frac{\int_{Y_{b}}^{Y} \frac{dY}{(Y - Y^{*})}}{\int_{Y_{b}}^{Y_{s}} \frac{dY}{(Y - Y^{*})}} \end{equation}\]

The equation above allows the determination of the thickness of the adsorption zone through numerical integration. Figure 8 shows a typical fixed-bed adsorber setup.

The dehydration processes described using glycol (Physical Absorption) and Molecular Sieves (Physical Adsorption) cover the entire range of desired water content for treated gas and, as mentioned, are selected according to the specifications of each project.

Molecular sieves are little affected by pressure. The maximum water content adsorbed at the end of the dehydration cycle ranges from 11% to 13% (w/w), with an adsorption capacity of about 20%. In the saturation zone, the sieve typically retains 13 kg of water per 100 kg of sieve.

For sizing the sieve for dehydration, the cycle time for water-saturated gas is 8 to 12 hours. Considering a 12-hour cycle, we have:

  • Bed heating = 5 hours;

  • Bed cooling = 4,5 hours;

  • Stand-by = 2,5 hours.

In the design, the following equation can be adopted for calculating gas velocity (Campbell):

\[\begin{equation} v = \frac{A}{\sqrt{\rho_{\text{gás}}}} \end{equation}\]

Where the velocity is in m/min, and:

\[A = \begin{cases} 67 & \text{(spheres 1/8)} \\ 48 & \text{(spheres 1/16)} \end{cases}\]

The sieves typically have a specific mass of 705 kg/m³.

In determining the height of the tower, the length of the adsorption zone should be considered as part of the bed. The height will affect the cycle time.

The presence of oils, glycols, amines, salty water, etc., causes bed contamination and leads to efficiency loss over time. The presence of hydrocarbons may lead to coke formation during regeneration, which is one of the main causes of premature loss of bed efficiency. Therefore, the project must ensure an adequate dew point for the gas to be dehydrated.

9.3 REMOVAL OF CO2 AND H2S

CO2 and H2S contaminants are called "acid gases" because they can form acids in the presence of liquid water, making the medium highly corrosive. In the case of H2S, there is the added inconvenience of high toxicity, posing a risk to human life, as shown in the table below. Even in streams with low H2S content, care must be taken regarding its potential accumulation in process plant vessels.

"Sweetening" is the process of removing these contaminants; hence, a sweet gas is one that is free (or has irrelevant levels) of acid gases.

9.3.1 Amines - Absorption

Just like in dehydration, the removal of CO2 and H2S can be done through absorption, specifically with amines (DEA, MEA, or MDEA), where the reaction is acid-base. Since this reaction occurs in an aqueous medium and because amines are viscous, aqueous amine solutions are used. Consequently, the gas leaves this process saturated with water and must be dehydrated afterward.

The design of these systems follows a procedure similar to that already presented for water absorption with glycol. Knowledge of the equilibrium relationships between CO2, amine, and gas is essential. Suppliers usually provide these correlations.

The absorption process with amines is a well-established technology, capable of achieving very low acid gas levels (a few ppm) in the treated gas. The process is similar to glycol absorption, using absorber and regenerator towers. Absorption is favored by high pressures and low temperatures, while regeneration is carried out at high temperatures and low pressures, as shown in the block diagram in Figure 38.

For natural gas streams with CO2 content up to 10%, absorption with amines is usually the best technical-economic solution. This differs from TEG processes because of the high solute concentration and the chemical reaction between the amine and CO2.

Just like TEG, acid gas removal with amines is carried out between the 2nd and 3rd stages of compression.

The maximum allowable CO2 content in treated gas is determined by each project’s needs (e.g., corrosion potential, sales specifications). According to ANP requirements (see Appendix I), the maximum CO2 content allowed for commercialized gas in Brazil is 3%. For high CO2 fields, this is often set as the export specification. Exporting gas with over 3% CO2 for later onshore treatment is not recommended due to increased corrosiveness and the need for CO2 disposal infrastructure. Onshore units rarely have reservoirs for CO2 injection.

9.3.2 Membranes

To separate CO2 from natural gas streams where the contaminant concentration exceeds 10%, membrane permeation through polymeric membranes becomes more competitive than amine-based systems. However, membrane technology is limited to a minimum achievable CO2 content of about 3% in the treated gas.

The principle of gas separation through membranes is based on the different permeabilities of the natural gas components through polymeric films. The amount of a component that permeates the membrane depends on its concentration and the pressure. The membrane material is designed to selectively allow CO2 molecules to pass while repelling hydrocarbons. However, due to its small size, methane molecules also partially permeate. Higher pressures increase the permeation of both CO2 and methane. Figure 10 shows an illustrative example of chemical species ordered by decreasing permeability in commercial membranes. Note that different membrane types may follow a different permeability order.

As CO2 permeates the membrane, this stream loses pressure. Being polymeric, these membranes have operational pressure limitations and are sensitive to the presence of liquid hydrocarbons. Since efficiency increases with pressure, the goal is to operate at the maximum pressure allowed by the membrane supplier. A typical operating condition is 50 bar at the inlet and 2 bar on the permeate side (CO2-rich stream). This large pressure drop causes a temperature reduction, which may lead to hydrocarbon condensation. To prevent condensate formation, the gas dew point is typically specified between 5°C and 10°C, using a Joule-Thomson system upstream of the membranes. Alternatively, the gas may be heated. The treated gas pressure drop is usually small, around 2 bar.

Figure 11 shows a simplified diagram of a membrane unit for CO2 removal. If a CO2 content lower than 3% is required in the treated gas, the process can be combined with amine treatment: membranes for bulk separation followed by polishing with amines.

Example. Consider a gas stream with 17% CO2 that must be reduced to 5%. A membrane supplier informs you that, under these conditions, the permeate will contain 48% CO2. Determine the treated and permeate flow rates for a feed of 5 MM Nm3/d:

\[\begin{equation*} \begin{array}{l} F_{CO_2} = G_{CO_2} + P_{CO_2} \\ F y_{F} = G y_{G} + P y_{P} \\ F y_{F} = (F - P) y_{G} + P y_{P} \\ 5 \cdot 0{,}17 = (5 - P) \cdot 0{,}05 + P \cdot 0{,}48 \\ P = 1{,}37\;\text{MMm}^3/\text{d} \\ \end{array} \hspace{0.7cm} \begin{array}{r} F = G + P\\ G = F - P\\ G= 3{,}63\;\text{MMm}^3/\text{d} \end{array} \end{equation*}\]

The membrane permeation rate can be modeled analogously to a heat transfer process:

\[\begin{equation} L_{i} = U_{i} A \Delta \pi_{i} \end{equation}\]

Where:

\[\begin{equation} \Delta \pi_{i} = \frac{\Delta p_{1} - \Delta p_{2}}{\ln \left(\frac{\Delta p_{1}}{\Delta p_{2}} \right)} \end{equation}\]

9.3.3 Solid Bed - Adsorption

Other alternatives for H2S removal include physical adsorption using molecular sieves (regenerable beds) and chemical adsorption with non-regenerable beds that irreversibly react with H2S. In the latter case, the bed must be replaced once saturated. Both methods can achieve very low concentrations of H2S in the treated gas.

Zinc oxide and iron oxide are used to remove H2S. These processes are very selective, even in streams with high CO2 concentrations. The adsorption is chemical, and the bed is replaced after saturation. Usually, the supplier handles the replacement and disposal.

Iron oxide reacts with H2S to form iron sulfide (FeS). In the presence of oxygen, FeS converts back to iron oxide but releases solid sulfur, which contaminates the solid bed. Oxygen presence is often due to small air leaks into the adsorber vessel.

The oxides used are hydrated (e.g., \(\alpha \mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3 \cdot \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)), so temperatures must stay below their decomposition temperature (i.e., \(<49\,^{\circ}\text{C}\)).

Quemical Reaction: \[\begin{equation*} \mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3 + 3 \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{S} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{S}_3 + 3 \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \end{equation*}\]

Each kilogram of iron oxide reacts with approximately 0,64 kg of H2S. In designing such systems, it’s common to use 80% of the bed’s maximum adsorption capacity. If oxygen ingress is expected, it must be considered in the design.

The disadvantage of using molecular sieves for H2S removal is the need to define a destination for the regenerated gas (contaminated with H2S). One option is to use it as fuel gas, but this produces SO3, which can react with water to form sulfuric acid (H2SO4) when released into the atmosphere.

Figure 42 shows a schematic of an H2S removal unit with chemical adsorption (usually with iron or zinc oxides), used in onshore units and more recently applied in some offshore units

In this unit, the gas contaminated with H2S is sent to vessels containing the solid reactive beds, after first passing through a gas scrubbing system composed of a scrubber and coalescing filter to remove liquid droplets that could hinder the H2S removal process.

The system comprises three parallel vessels in a "Lead & Lag" setup, where each main vessel has a guard bed in series, aligned after the saturation of the first to allow bed replacement. After passing through the first vessel, the gas already has the desired contaminant concentration and can proceed to the next process stage, passing through a filter to prevent solid carryover. Once the first bed is saturated, the gas is redirected to the second vessel to allow replacement of the saturated bed.

Alternatively, H2S can be removed by injecting chemical scavengers (like Triazine or Glyoxal) into the wells. These scavengers react irreversibly with H2S, are water-soluble, and non-recoverable. However, for streams with high H2S concentrations, the cost of scavengers becomes disadvantageous compared to solid beds or amine absorption.

On FPSOs, the long residence times in storage tanks favor the growth of sulfate-reducing bacteria that generate H2S, so antibacterial agents may be injected along with scavengers.

9.4 GAS PROCESSING PLANTS

Gas processing plants comprise the processes required to meet the necessary specifications. The configuration may vary greatly depending on whether the field produces associated or non-associated gas.

9.4.1 Associated Gas Fields

Figure 12 shows a flowchart example of an oil and gas processing plant. The gas streams from the flashes occurring in the three-phase and two-phase separators are directed to the compression system. The condensates generated are sent to the oil processing plant. The light components are sent to the gas pipeline, avoiding evaporation in the cargo tanks of FPSOs or terminal tanks when oil is exported by pipeline. Note that the condensate generated in the compression system is directed to the oil plant.

The pressure upstream of the main compressor is approximately equal to the three-phase separator, around 10 bar. The two-phase separators (located downstream), operate at a lower pressure (about 4 to 5 bar), so a booster compressor is required to raise these streams to the suction pressure of the main compressor. The final pressure of the compression system is the one required to ensure gas flow up to the UPGN operating pressure (between 40 and 70 bar).

When additional contaminants need to be removed, the technologies presented in this chapter should be applied according to their attributes and limitations.

9.4.2 Non-Associated Gas Fields

Gas fields often have high pressure, and in some cases, a gas compression system is not needed as the reservoir energy is sufficient to transport the gas to the UPGN. In such cases, the entire gas processing plant must operate at high pressure, increasing capital costs. When wells are dry-completed, heat exchange between the produced fluids and seawater is low. As a result, the temperature of the produced fluids is relatively high, usually above the hydrate formation boundary. When the completion is wet (Figure 13), the produced fluid exchanges heat with seawater, reaching lower temperatures, which typically leads to hydrate formation in production wells.

To prevent hydrate formation in production lines, dehydration is commonly initiated by injecting glycol (MEG) into the wells. Therefore, the plant includes a three-phase separator to separate gas, condensate, and the rich MEG phase (typically 60% MEG and 40% water).

Depending on reservoir pressure and water depth, the plant feed may reach low temperatures due to pressure losses (Joule-Thomson effect) during well-to-platform flow, making it difficult to separate condensate from the rich MEG phase. In plants where the inlet temperature is low (\(<50^{\circ}\text{C}\)), heating is required, and a liquid residence time of at least 30 minutes is recommended in the three-phase separator. The rich MEG is sent for regeneration. Figure 14 illustrates the main processes used in gas fields.

Initially, a three-phase separator separates the three streams: gas, condensate, and rich MEG. Since the MEG volume is usually small compared to the condensate and gas, three-phase separation with a boot is typically used to better control the MEG-condensate interface.

Condensate processing (Figure 15) consists of dehydration since it is usually mixed with gas for pipeline export. The use of a single pipeline is due to the high acquisition and installation costs. Dehydration is done via stripping with dry gas. The gas effluent (containing water vapor and dissolved gas) is cooled to condense the water, which is then sent to the water treatment system. The gas is sent to the gas processing unit.

Hydrate Prevention with MEG

In non-associated gas fields, hydrate formation in production lines is common, so MEG is injected into wells and later recovered in the process plant. MEG is preferred over TEG due to its lower viscosity (less flow resistance), facilitating injection through umbilicals. It is also less soluble in liquid hydrocarbons and has a lower freezing and hydrate formation temperature. Although it does not fully remove water from the gas stream, it shifts the equilibrium to prevent hydrate formation. A second dehydration step is usually required to ensure gas export via pipelines and its use in gas lift systems without hydrate formation.

In gas fields, water from the formation is often carried with the produced gas, introducing salts into the rich MEG stream. These salts retain water and MEG in a brine. A vacuum flash is applied to evaporate water and MEG, separating them from the brine. Centrifuges are used to recover residual MEG from the brine.

MEG regeneration is hindered by its low degradation temperature, so vacuum operation is common in the regeneration tower. Up to 97% regeneration can be achieved, but values around 80% are typically used to avoid hydrates while reducing energy consumption.

The MEG regeneration process (Figure 16) differs from the TEG process because of salt entrainment. Thus, rich MEG undergoes vacuum distillation to separate it from the brine. The gas phase (H2O + MEG) is sent to a distillation column with a top condenser to liquefy MEG and separate it from water. The brine is sent to centrifuges for MEG recovery.

9.5 NATURAL GAS PROCESSING UNIT (UPGN)

In UPGNs, gas is fractionated into several products (Figure 17), such as Residual Gas (or Natural Gas, or Sales Gas), Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG), and Light Naphtha. These fractions predominantly contain the following components: Sales Gas (C1 and C2), LPG (C3 and C4), and Light Naphtha (C5+). These are predominant components because the separation is not perfect—there are always mixtures within the fractions. Where petrochemical units use ethane for polyethylene production, UPGNs separate ethane from residual gas. Otherwise, ethane may be added to the sales gas1.

The liquid generated from the partial condensation of gas is called NGL (Natural Gas Liquid). When methane is cooled to liquefaction, the resulting liquid is known as LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas).

Since the exported gas from production units contains heavy components (C3+), their condensation in pipelines is common. Slug catchers are used to separate gas and liquid phases before processing. Figure 18 shows an example UPGN processing system where gas is fractionated into sales gas (mostly methane) and ethane (C2), and the condensate is separated into LPG and C5+.

Condensate accumulated in pipelines is removed using pigs, and all condensate must be collected in slug catchers (Figure 19) before being sent to the UPGN for processing.

From the slug catchers, the gas phase is sent to the UPGN, and the liquid phase is sent to the Natural Gas Condensate Processing Unit (UPCGN).

Methane and ethane, being lighter, accumulate in the gas phase. Propane and butane distribute between the gas and liquid phases, while heavier components (C5+) accumulate in the liquid phase.

Operating pressures in UPGNs typically range from 40 to 70 bar. Depending on the gas inlet pressure, recompression may be required.

The most common gas fractionation processes are:

  • Joule-Thomson

  • Simple Refrigeration

  • Turboexpansion

  • Refrigerated Absorption

The Refrigerated Absorption process is now obsolete and has largely been replaced by Turboexpansion, and will not be described here.

9.5.1 Joule-Thomson

The Joule-Thomson process was described in Section 9.1.3 on Condensate Removal (Figure 12). It is considered a dew point adjustment process and is one of the simplest and lowest-capital solutions. The Joule-Thomson effect refers to the gas temperature drop due to pressure reduction across a control valve.

9.5.2 Simple Refrigeration

This process achieves temperatures as low as \(-40\,^{\circ}\text{C}\), making it more efficient than J-T for separation. It is widely used to meet gas sales specifications.

In Simple Refrigeration, propane is typically used as the refrigerant, hence the name Propane Refrigeration. Other refrigerants such as ammonia, freon, and propylene can also be used. Due to the low temperatures involved, hydrate formation must be prevented—this can be done by injecting MEG at cold points or dehydrating the gas using TEG, molecular sieves, or silica gel.

An example of Simple Refrigeration application in UPGNs is shown in Figure 20. As with Joule-Thomson, the natural gas stream exchanges heat with cold process streams—specifically in a gas-gas exchanger and a propane chiller.

To prevent hydrate formation that could clog equipment and lines, MEG is injected before cooling. The resulting three-phase mixture (gas, NGL, and rich MEG) is separated in a three-phase separator. The rich MEG is sent to regeneration, the gas is compressed and exported, and the NGL is sent to further fractionation where LPG and natural gasoline (C5+) can be produced.

The deethanizer and debutanizer towers are distillation columns. Take the debutanizer, for example: at the top of the tower, above the feed inlet, the objective is to maximize the removal of LPG components (C3 and C4). Cooling the overhead gas leads to preferential condensation of C5+. This overhead stream is partially recycled to the column. The top temperature depends on the available cooling medium (air or water), which may be around 38 °C in tropical countries. It is thus reasonable to assume a top temperature of around 45 °C. The top pressure corresponds to the bubble pressure at that temperature. Likewise, the bottom liquid stream is vaporized to recover C3 and C4 that may be present in it.

Figure 52 presents an example of a propane refrigeration cycle with dual-stage compression and an economizer.

In this cycle, propane is evaporated at constant pressure and temperature in a chiller-type heat exchanger, in this case, a gas refrigerator. The vapor passes through a scrubber to remove entrained liquid propane, and is then compressed in two stages and condensed. The condensed propane is accumulated in a vessel and sent to the economizer.

At the inlet of the economizer vessel, the pressure of the liquid stream is reduced to match the discharge pressure of the first compression stage, causing partial vaporization of propane and a consequent temperature reduction. The vapor formed is fed between the two compressors, thus acting as an intercooler. The liquid undergoes another pressure drop and reaches the temperature at which it will be fed into the chiller, thus closing the cycle.

9.5.3 Turbo Expansion

This process uses the kinetic energy generated during the gas expansion through a nozzle to produce shaft work by the gas striking the turbine blades. Thermodynamically, it is the most efficient of the processes.

Typically, the turbo-expansion process can reach temperatures as low as -110°C. Therefore, the gas must be treated to remove water and CO2 to avoid hydrate formation. The initial step of the process involves dehydrating the gas using molecular sieves since glycol systems do not dehydrate sufficiently to prevent hydrates at such low temperatures. Subsequently, the gas is cooled using a (usually) propane refrigeration system and may also exchange heat with the residual gas itself to remove heavier hydrocarbons. This allows for a smaller turbo-expander design and increases turbo-expansion efficiency, reaching the lowest possible temperatures and liquefying ethane in the condensate. Heat exchange is typically performed with printed circuit heat exchangers, known as cold boxes.

Next, the gas undergoes an isentropic expansion with work output through the turbo-expander. Typically, the temperature achieved here is around -100°C, which is sufficient to recover hydrocarbons heavier than methane. The resulting liquid stream has a high ethane content due to the extremely low temperatures reached.

The inlet gas stream, partially condensed, then passes through a separator vessel. The liquid stream is fed into the demethanizer column, while the vapor phase is sent to the turbo-expander.

The turbo-expander simultaneously provides cooling and condensation of the gas, as well as useful work that can be used for recompression. It is common to install a Joule-Thomson valve as a bypass to the turbo-expander to provide operational flexibility. This Joule-Thomson valve is important during plant startup when there is excess gas and for maintaining unit operation in case of turbo-expander shutdown.

The two-phase stream generated in the turbo-expander flows to the demethanizer column for fractionation. Methane and lighter components, such as nitrogen, are the main products of the overhead stream, while ethane, propane, and heavier hydrocarbons constitute the bottom products.

The gas from the demethanizer column is used beforehand in the cooling system before being compressed for transportation, as its temperature is very low. Generally, the first compression stage is powered by the turbo-expander and is followed by another compressor responsible for generating most of the processed gas pressure.

The demethanizer column is a distillation column that differs from others by:

  • Having a larger top diameter to accommodate the vapor feed;

  • Lacking a top condenser with reflux;

  • Having multiple feed points along the column, from low-temperature condensate separators;

  • Featuring side reboilers mainly intended to aid in cooling the inlet natural gas by exchanging heat with the descending NGL;

  • Possessing a temperature gradient: around 75°C.

Typical operating conditions of a demethanizer include temperatures between -115°C and -110°C and pressures between 14 bar and 28 bar.

In the entropy versus enthalpy diagram shown in Figure 55, a comparison is made between the isentropic process (closely approximating turbo-expansion) and the isenthalpic process (approximating Joule-Thomson expansion) for a gas initially at \(T_1 = 37,8\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and \(p_1 = 862\,\mathrm{kPa}\) expanded to a pressure \(p_2 = 138\,\mathrm{kPa}\). The cost of a turbo-expansion unit is the highest among the three processes mentioned because it requires special steels to ensure the mechanical resistance of equipment exposed to very low temperatures.

The feed pressure adopted for a UPGN depends on the composition of the feed and the product delivery specifications. The pressure typically ranges from 40 to 90 bar.

9.6 DISTILLATION

At the Natural Gas Processing Units (UPGNs), the products are separated through a process commonly known as distillation. It consists of separating different chemical species based on their different boiling points. In a simplified manner, the process can be understood as the separation of two components, referred to as key components. The light key corresponds to the more volatile component, while the heavy key corresponds to the less volatile component. For example, if we want to recover methane from natural gas, we use a demethanizer, where the separation occurs mainly between methane and ethane.

In a UPGN, gas fractionation is based on temperature reduction to liquefy the heavier fractions. The lower the temperature, the greater the fractionation. If we want to liquefy C₂ (to separate it from C₁), we need a temperature of \(\mathrm{<}~110~^\circ\)C. If we want to liquefy C₁ (for storage and transport), it is necessary to reach temperatures of \(\mathrm{<}~161~^\circ\)C. In this case, the process is called cryogenic.

Distillation columns perform both absorption (upper section) and stripping (bottom section), and can be understood as the combination of these two processes, as illustrated in Figure 22 below. Bottom reboilers and top condensers assist in this task.

For the design of distillation columns, there are currently sophisticated computational routines that take into account the various phenomena involved. Here, we will address simplified methods, known as short-cut methods, which, in addition to being didactic, are useful for quick calculations and for verifying the consistency of results obtained through computational simulations.

9.6.1 Column Height – McCabe-Thiele Method

We begin with McCabe-Thiele, who developed his method for the distillation of a binary mixture (two components). In this case, representing the mole fraction of one of the components is sufficient to determine the mass balance, since \(x_1 = 1 - x_2\). Therefore, it is not necessary to use the subscript. Consider the distillation column in the figure, where:

\(F\) = molar flow rate of the feed
\(D\) = molar flow rate of the distillate (top product)
\(B\) = molar flow rate of the bottom product

Let us assume that the feed \(F\) is composed of its liquid fraction \(L_F\) and vapor fraction \(V_F\). These streams are incorporated into the liquid and vapor streams that flow through the column. Assuming the feed enters the column between stages \(f\) and \(f+1\), we then have:

\[\begin{equation} V_{f+1} = V_F + V_{f+1}' \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} L_{f}' = L_F + L_f \end{equation}\]

The overall mass balance of the upper section of the column (dashed in Figure 23) with the lower boundary at tray \(n\) is given by:

\[\begin{equation} V_{n+1} = L_n + D \end{equation}\]

The component mass balance in the upper section of the column is:

\[\begin{equation} V_{n+1} \, y_{n+1} = L_n \, x_n + D \, x_D \end{equation}\]

From which we conclude:

\[\begin{equation} y_{n+1} = \frac{L_n}{V_{n+1}} x_n + \frac{D}{V_{n+1}} x_D \end{equation}\]

McCabe and Thiele assumed that the flow rates of the liquid and vapor streams are constant along the column, as shown in Figure 23(b). In this case, the previous equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} y_{n+1} = \frac{L}{V} x_n + \frac{D}{V} x_D \end{equation}\]

Substituting \(D = V - L\):

\[\begin{equation} y_{n+1} = \frac{L}{V} x_n + \left(1 - \frac{L}{V} \right) x_D \end{equation}\]

In a graph of \(y\) versus \(x\), the top section equation represents a straight line, called the operating line, with slope (angular coefficient) \(\frac{L}{V}\) and \(y\)-intercept (at \(x = 0\)) equal to \(\left(1 - \frac{L}{V} \right) x_D\).

The McCabe-Thiele method proposes that the number of stages be determined graphically using the equilibrium curve (\(y\) vs. \(x\)), as shown in Figure 59a, starting from the distillate composition \(x_D\). Substituting this value into the operating line equation yields \(y_1 = x_D\). With \(y_1\), one determines \(x_1\) on the equilibrium curve (Figure 59b). Substituting \(x_1\) into the operating line then gives \(y_2\). This procedure continues through the absorption section, as shown in Figure 59c.

Similarly, the mass balance around stage \(m\) in the bottom section (Figure 60) leads to:

\[\begin{equation} y_{m} = \frac{L'}{V'} x_{m-1} - \frac{B'}{V'} x_{B} \end{equation}\]

Substituting \(B = L' - V'\), we obtain:

\[\begin{equation} y_{m} = \frac{L'}{V'} x_{m-1} + \left(1 - \frac{L'}{V'}\right) x_{B} \end{equation}\]

For the stripping section, the same reasoning applies. Starting from \(x_{B}\), the diagram in Figure 61 is constructed.

The total number of stages results from combining the absorption and stripping diagrams, as shown in Figure 62. Note that there are multiple possible paths. The feed stage corresponds to the intersection point of the operating lines.

Feed Operating Line. Consider that the intersection of the operating lines for the absorption and stripping sections occurs at stage \(f\), at point \((x_{f}, y_{f})\). In this case, the top and bottom section mass balance equations are, respectively:

\[\begin{equation} V y_{f} = L x_{f-1} + D x_{D} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} V' y_{f} = L' x_{f-1} - B x_{B} \end{equation}\]

Subtracting the equations:

\[\begin{equation} (V - V') y_{f} = (L - L') x_{f-1} + D x_{D} + B x_{B} \end{equation}\]

Since the overall component balance for the column is:

\[\begin{equation} D x_{D} + B x_{B} = F z \end{equation}\]

Then:

\[\begin{equation} (V - V') y_{f} = (L - L') x_{f-1} + Fz \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \frac{V - V'}{F} y_{f} = \frac{L - L'}{F} x_{f-1} + z \end{equation}\]

If we consider that the feed is two-phase, then: \[\begin{equation*} F = L_{F} + V_{F} \end{equation*}\]

Let us define \(v = V_f/F\): \[\begin{equation*} \upsilon = \frac{V_{F}}{F} = \frac{V' - V}{F}, \quad \upsilon - 1 = \frac{L_{F}}{F} = \frac{L' - L}{F} \end{equation*}\]

Then the equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} y_{f} = -\frac{1 - \upsilon}{\upsilon} x_{f-1} + \frac{1}{\upsilon} z \end{equation}\]

The above equation represents the feed operating line, which defines the set of points where the operating lines from the absorption and stripping sections intersect. This has the same form as the flash equation presented in Chapter 2, where the physical state of the feed was discussed.

If the feed is two-phase, then its liquid and vapor fractions will be added to the internal streams \(L\) and \(V\) of the column, as shown in the figure. If the feed is subcooled (below its bubble point), it will absorb heat from the system, partially condensing the vapor stream. On the other hand, if it is superheated (above its dew point), it will vaporize part of the liquid stream L.

In practice, the feed may be either subcooled or superheated. From the energy balance, it is demonstrated (Wankat, 2012) that the parameter: \[\begin{equation*} q = 1 - \upsilon \end{equation*}\] corresponds to the heat required to convert one mole of feed into saturated vapor, divided by the molar latent heat of the feed. Wankat presents the necessary adjustments to the McCabe-Thiele method to account for such situations.

Reflux Ratio. The reflux ratio \(R = \frac{L}{D}\) is an important parameter to be defined in column design. It is observed that a higher reflux ratio improves the separation. The same occurs when the number of stages in the column is increased. Therefore, the number of stages \(N\) is related to the reflux ratio \(L\), and there are infinite combinations that can result in the desired separation, as illustrated in the figure (a) below.

The reflux rate alters the L/V ratio in the column. In this case, the intersection points of the operating lines shift, but all these points will lie along the feed operating line, as shown in Figure 63(b).

It is intuitive to see that the minimum number of theoretical stages corresponds to the condition where the reflux ratio is maximum. The theoretical performance limits of the column lie between two extremes: total reflux (no distillate stream \(D\)), which corresponds to the **minimum number of stages**, and minimum reflux, which corresponds to **an infinite number of stages**.

Note that under total reflux, the operating lines coincide with the 45-degree diagonal, resulting in the fewest stages possible. On the other hand, under minimum reflux conditions, the operating lines meet the equilibrium curve at the feed composition \(x_{F}\), leading to an infinite number of stages. These are not practical conditions, but theoretical references. Figures (a) and (b) below illustrate these two scenarios.

At the minimum reflux condition, the mole fractions \(x\) and \(y\) near tray \(n\) become very close. This is known as the "pinch point", where:

\[\begin{equation} x_{n-1} = x_{n} = x_{n+1} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} y_{n-1} = y_{n} = y_{n+1} \end{equation}\]

9.6.2 Height of Distillation Columns – FUG Method

Based on the McCabe-Thiele method, several other short-cut methods have been developed. One of them is called the FUG method, named after the Fenske, Underwood, and Gilliland equations. Unlike the McCabe-Thiele method, the FUG method applies to mixtures with more than two components. In this methodology, only two substances (key components) are considered to distribute between the top and bottom streams. These are referred to as the light key (LK) and heavy key (HK).

This means that in the distillate stream (\(D\)), there will be no components heavier than the heavy key, and in the bottoms stream (\(B\)), there will be no components lighter than the light key. Therefore, the binary equilibrium diagrams between LK and HK can be used as an approximation for the separation.

The first step involves selecting the key components of the separation and setting the design conditions, i.e., the desired recovery rates of the key components in the top and bottom of the column.

The method estimates the number of ideal equilibrium stages \(N\) using Gilliland’s correlation, which relates the reflux ratio \(R\), the minimum reflux ratio \(R_{\text{min}}\), and the minimum number of stages \(N_{\text{min}}\) as follows:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{N - N_{\text{min}}}{N + 1} = 0,75 \left[1 - \left(\frac{R - R_{\text{min}}}{R + 1} \right)^{0,566} \right] \end{equation}\]

Where:

\(N\) = number of ideal stages

\(N_{\text{min}}\) = minimum number of ideal stages

\(R\) = \(L/D\) = reflux ratio

\(R_{\text{min}}\) = \(L_{\text{min}}/D\) = minimum reflux ratio

To estimate the minimum number of stages \(N_{\text{min}}\), the Fenske equation is used:

\[\begin{equation} N_{\text{min}} = \frac{\log \left[\left(\frac{x_{LK}}{x_{HK}} \right)_{D} \left(\frac{x_{HK}}{x_{LK}} \right)_{B} \right]}{\log \bar{\alpha}_{LK,HK}} \end{equation}\]

Where:

\(x_{LK}\) = mole fraction of the light key component

\(x_{HK}\) = mole fraction of the heavy key component

\(\bar{\alpha}_{LK,HK}\) = average relative volatility between LK and HK

The relative volatility \(\alpha\) is a parameter widely used to characterize the difficulty of separating components. The average relative volatility of the column between LK and HK is defined as:

\[\begin{equation} \bar{\alpha}_{LK,HK} = \frac{\bar{K}_{LK}}{\bar{K}_{HK}} \end{equation}\]

Since the \(K\) values vary with temperature and pressure, an average value should be used. The average relative volatility \(\bar{\alpha}_{LK,HK}\) can be calculated using different means (arithmetic, geometric, etc.), and the \(K_i\) values for each component (as introduced in Chapter 2) are needed for this calculation.

To calculate \(R_{\text{min}}\), Underwood’s equations are used. The actual reflux ratio \(R\) is estimated by multiplying \(R_{\text{min}}\) by a factor:

\[\begin{equation*} R = \text{factor} \cdot R_{\text{min}} \end{equation*}\]

It is recommended that this factor be between 1,2 and 1,5. Underwood derived his equations for the condition of minimum reflux. Under this condition, the operating lines intersect at a stage, say \(n\).

In this condition, the mass balance for the absorption section (Figure 65) for all components \(i\) is:

\[\begin{equation} V_{\text{min}} \cdot y_{n+1,i} = L_{\text{min}} \cdot x_{n,i} + D \cdot x_{D,i} \end{equation}\]

Dividing by \(V_{\text{min}} \cdot y_{n+1,i}\), we get:

\[\begin{equation} 1 - \frac{L_{\text{min}} \cdot x_{n,i}}{V_{\text{min}} \cdot y_{n+1,i}} = \frac{D \cdot x_{D,i}}{V_{\text{min}} \cdot y_{n+1,i}} \end{equation}\]

And:

\[\begin{equation} V_{\text{min}} \cdot y_{n+1,i} = \frac{D \cdot x_{D,i}}{1 - \frac{L_{\text{min}} \cdot x_{n,i}}{V_{\text{min}} \cdot y_{n+1,i}}} \end{equation}\]

The equilibrium condition for component \(i\) at stage \(n+1\) is:

\[\begin{equation} y_{n+1,i} = K_{i} \cdot x_{n+1,i} \end{equation}\]

Then, under the minimum reflux (pinch point) condition, we can assume \(x_{n+1,i} \approx x_{n,i}\) and the equation 9.127 becomes:

\[\begin{equation} y_{n+1,i} = K_{i} \cdot x_{n,i} \end{equation}\]

The equilibrium constant \(K_i\) can be expressed using the relative volatility \(\alpha\), referring to a reference component. In this case, we take the heavy key (HK) as reference:

\[\begin{equation} K_{i} = \alpha_{i,HK} \cdot K_{HK} \end{equation}\]

Combining the 9.128 and 9.129 expressions, we obtain:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{y_{n+1,i}}{x_{n,i}} = K_{i} = \alpha_{i,HK} \cdot K_{HK} \end{equation}\]

Substituting the 9.130 equation into the 9.126 equation and summing over all components \(i\):

\[\begin{equation} \sum V_{\text{min}} \cdot y_{n+1,i} = \sum \frac{D \cdot x_{D,i}}{1 - \frac{L_{\text{min}}}{V_{\text{min}} \cdot \alpha_{i,HK} \cdot K_{HK}}} \end{equation}\]

Recalling that \(\sum y_{n+1,i} = 1\), the equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} V_{\text{min}} = \sum \frac{\alpha_{i,HK} \cdot D \cdot x_{D,i}}{\alpha_{i,HK} - \frac{L_{\text{min}}}{V_{\text{min}} \cdot K_{HK}}} \end{equation}\]

Similarly, for the stripping section (Figure 66), a corresponding expression can be written:

\[\begin{equation} -V'_{\text{min}} = \sum \frac{\alpha'_{i,HK} \cdot B \cdot x_{B,i}}{\alpha'_{i,HK} - \frac{L'_{\text{min}}}{V'_{\text{min}} \cdot K'_{HK}}} \end{equation}\]

Assuming constant relative volatility along the column:

\[\begin{equation} \alpha_{i,HK} = \alpha'_{i,HK} \end{equation}\]

Underwood argued that since the absorption factors \(L / VK\) are uncertain, they can be represented by a parameter \(\phi\), so that:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{L_{\text{min}}}{V_{\text{min}} \cdot K_{HK}} = \frac{L'_{\text{min}}}{V'_{\text{min}} \cdot K'_{HK}} = \phi \end{equation}\]

Substituting the 9.135 equation into the 9.132 equation:

\[\begin{equation} V_{\text{min}} = \sum \frac{\alpha_{i,HK} \cdot D \cdot x_{D,i}}{\alpha_{i,HK} - \phi} = L_{\text{min}} + D \end{equation}\]

Dividing by \(D\) gives the second Underwood equation:

\[\begin{equation} \sum \frac{\alpha_{i,HK} \cdot x_{D,i}}{\alpha_{i,HK} - \phi} = \frac{L_{\text{min}}}{D} + 1 = R_{\text{min}} + 1 \end{equation}\]

From 9.133 and 9.136 equations, we also find:

\[\begin{equation} V_{\text{min}} - V'_{\text{min}} = \sum \frac{\alpha_{i,HK} \cdot (D \cdot x_{D,i} + B \cdot x_{B,i})}{\alpha_{i,HK} - \phi} \end{equation}\]

From the global mass balance:

\[\begin{equation} D \cdot x_{D,i} + B \cdot x_{B,i} = F \cdot z_{i} \end{equation}\]

So the 9.138 equation becomes:

\[\begin{equation} V_{\text{min}} - V'_{\text{min}} = \sum \frac{\alpha_{i,HK} \cdot F \cdot z_{i}}{\alpha_{i,HK} - \phi} \end{equation}\]

And recalling that for a two-phase feed:

\[\begin{equation} F = V_{F} + L_{F} \end{equation}\]

The mass balance around the feed stage gives:

\[\begin{equation} V_{\text{min}} - V'_{\text{min}} = V_{F} \end{equation}\]

Thus:

\[\begin{equation} V_{F} = \sum \frac{\alpha_{i,HK} \cdot F \cdot z_{i}}{\alpha_{i,HK} - \phi} \end{equation}\]

And dividing by \(F\), we obtain the final expression of the first Underwood equation:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{V_{F}}{F} = \sum \frac{\alpha_{i,HK} \cdot z_{i}}{\alpha_{i,HK} - \phi} \end{equation}\]

Where:

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{V_{F}}{F} = 0 \quad \text{(at bubble point)} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{V_{F}}{F} = 1 \quad \text{(at dew point)} \end{equation*}\]

9.6.3 Packed Distillation Column Height

Unlike trays that have a specific path for liquid flow, packing fills the entire cross-sectional area of the column.

For a packed column, height determination begins with a mass balance over the column, as shown in Figure 67. The absorption and stripping sections have heights \(z_{A}\) and \(z_{B}\), respectively.

The mass transfer for an infinitesimal volume element is:

\[\begin{equation} A \, d(Vy) = N_{A} \, a \, A \, dz \end{equation}\]

Where:

\(V\) = molar flow rate of the component

\(a\) = interfacial area per unit volume

\(A\) = cross-sectional area of the column

\(z\) = height

Assuming \(V\) is constant along the column:

\[\begin{equation} d(Vy) = V \, dy \end{equation}\]

The distillation process can be approximated by equimolar counter-diffusion, where vaporization of one species is accompanied by condensation of another. The fundamental equation for mass transfer between points 1 and 2 is:

\[\begin{equation} N_{A} = k'_{y} (y_{1} - y_{2}) \end{equation}\]

where \(k'_{y}=\frac{cD_{AB}}{z_2-z_1}\)

From the general molar flux expression:

\[\begin{equation} N_{A} = k'_{y} (y_{i} - y) \end{equation}\]

where yi = molar fraction at the gas-liquid interface. We obtain:

\[\begin{equation} N_{A} = \frac{V \, dy}{a \, dz} = k'_{y} (y_{i} - y) \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} dz = \frac{V}{k'_{y} \, a} \cdot \frac{dy}{(y_{i} - y)} \end{equation}\]

Integrating over the absorption section:

\[\begin{equation} \int_{0}^{z_{A}} dz = \int_{y_{q}}^{y_{2}} \frac{V}{k'_{y} \, a} \cdot \frac{dy}{(y_{i} - y)} \end{equation}\]

Where \(y_q\) corresponds to the vapor composition obtained at the intersection of the material balance line with the \(q\)-line. Since the first term of the integral on the right varies little with concentration, it can be taken out of the integral.

\[\begin{equation} z_{A} = \frac{V}{k'_{y} \, a} \int_{y_{q}}^{y_{2}} \frac{dy}{(y_{i} - y)} \end{equation}\]

The first term corresponds to the height of a gas-phase transfer unit \(H_{G}\), and the second to the number of gas-phase transfer units \(N_{G}\):

\[\begin{equation} H_{G} = \frac{V}{k'_{y} \, a} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{G} = \int_{y_{q}}^{y_{2}} \frac{dy}{(y_{i} - y)} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} z_{A} = H_{G} \cdot N_{G} \end{equation}\]

Similarly, for the liquid phase:

\[\begin{equation} H_{L} = \frac{L}{k'_{x} \, a} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{L} = \int_{x_{q}}^{x_{2}} \frac{dx}{(x - x_{i})} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} z_{A} = H_{L} \cdot N_{L} \end{equation}\]

For the stripping section, the same equations apply with integration limits between \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{q}\) for \(N_{G}\), and \(x_{1}\) and \(x_{q}\) for \(N_{L}\). Using global mass transfer coefficients:

\[\begin{equation} z_{A} = H_{OG} \cdot N_{OG} = H_{OL} \cdot N_{OL} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} H_{OG} = \frac{V}{k'_{Oy} \, a} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} H_{OL} = \frac{L}{k'_{Ox} \, a} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{OG} = \int_{Y_{q}}^{Y_{2}} \frac{dy}{(y^{*} - y)} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} N_{OL} = \int_{X_{q}}^{X_{2}} \frac{dx}{(x - x^{*})} \end{equation}\]

From Chapter 2, we know:

\[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{k'_{Oy}} = \frac{1}{k'_{y}} + \frac{m}{k'_{x}} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} \frac{1}{k'_{Ox}} = \frac{1}{m \, k'_{y}} + \frac{1}{k'_{x}} \end{equation}\]

Therefore:

\[\begin{equation} H_{OG} = H_{G} + \frac{mV}{L} \cdot H_{L} = H_{G} + \lambda \cdot H_{L} \end{equation}\]

\[\begin{equation} H_{OL} = H_{L} + \frac{L}{mV} \cdot H_{G} = H_{L} + \frac{H_{G}}{\lambda} \end{equation}\]

Where:

\[\begin{equation} m = \frac{y^* - y_{i}}{x - x_{i}} \end{equation}\]

9.6.3 Diameter of Distillation Columns

The diameter of the columns depends on the gas flow rate, as it is necessary to maintain an ideal gas flow velocity in the column. In this case, the diameter is calculated from the cross-sectional area of the column:

\[\begin{equation*} A = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} = \frac{V}{v} \end{equation*}\]

To determine the maximum velocity for a tray column, the Souders and Brown equation can be used, similar to that used for gas-liquid separators:

\[\begin{equation*} v_{\text{MAX}} = K \sqrt{ \frac{\rho_L - \rho_G}{\rho_G} } \end{equation*}\]

For sieve trays, for example, an initial estimate is \(K = 0{,}055 \text{ m/s}\).

An alternative criterion commonly used to determine the column diameter is to ensure that the velocity is below the flooding velocity. Flooding occurs when the liquid blocks gas flow through the column. It is common to adopt a velocity between 60% and 80% of the flooding velocity.

Starting from the equation (8.4) for the terminal velocity of a droplet in a gas medium (adapted):

\[\begin{equation*} v = \sqrt{ \frac{4g \cdot d_p}{3} \cdot \frac{(\rho_L - \rho_G)}{\rho_G} } \end{equation*}\]

Souders and Brown proposed the following equation for the flooding velocity:

\[\begin{equation*} v_{\text{flooding}} = C_{SB} \left( \frac{\sigma}{20} \right)^{0{,}2} \cdot \sqrt{ \frac{(\rho_L - \rho_G)}{\rho_G} } \end{equation*}\]

They developed the correlation in Figure 69 to determine \(C_{SB}\). This correlation was developed for an air-water system, which is why the term \(\left( \frac{\sigma}{20} \right)^{0{,}2}\) was included as a correction for surface tension when using different fluids.

The abscissa ratio \(L\)/\(V\) in the graph above corresponds to the mass flow rate ratio. However, in cases of total condensation, the mass flow rate ratio is identical to the molar flow rate ratio, since the molecular weight is the same for both streams. Therefore, in such cases, the following relation can be used:

\[\begin{equation*} \frac{L}{V} = \frac{L}{L + D} = \frac{R}{R + 1} \end{equation*}\]

9.6.4 Condenser and Reboiler

To size condensers and reboilers, an energy balance must be performed based on the inlet and outlet temperatures of the heat exchangers. Some distillation columns use air coolers or cooling water for overhead condensation. In this case, the temperature of the cooling fluid is an input parameter, and by adopting typical approaches, the condensate temperature can be determined. When the cooling fluid is water, it is typically assumed to be at 25ºC. If air cooling is used, the air is assumed to be at 40ºC.

If the overhead condensation is total, then the pressure can be determined from the bubble point of the condensate, as this is the pressure required for the fluid to be in the liquid phase. The following typical approaches are commonly used:

Air Coolers 10–25ºC
Cooling water for hydrocarbon liquids and gases 8–12ºC

To determine the thermal loads of the top condenser and bottom reboiler of a distillation column, we must carry out the energy balance for the entire column. The total balance, represented in Figure 70, is:

\[\begin{equation*} Q_B - Q_C = h_F F - h_D D + h_B B \end{equation*}\]

Where:

\(Q_B\) – heat duty of the reboiler

\(Q_C\) – heat duty of the condenser

\(h_D\) – enthalpy of the distillate

\(h_B\) – enthalpy of the bottom product

\(h_F\) – enthalpy of the feed

The energy balance for the condenser is:

\[\begin{equation*} Q_C = L (h_L - h_V) + D (h_D - h_V) \end{equation*}\]

Where:

\(h_L\): enthalpy of the reflux stream

\(h_V\): enthalpy of the vapor from the top tray

Thus:

\[\begin{equation*} Q_C = V h_V - L h_L - D h_D \end{equation*}\]

Since \(h_D = h_L\), we get:

\[\begin{equation*} Q_C = V h_V - L h_L - D h_L \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} Q_C = V h_V - (L + D) h_L \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} Q_C =Vh_V - Vh_L= V(h_V - h_L) \end{equation*}\]

Calculation of the reboiler heat duty \(Q_B\). The heat duty of the reboiler can be obtained from the difference between the total balance and the condenser balance:

\[\begin{equation*} Q_B = Q_C + h_D D + h_B B - h_F F \end{equation*}\]
