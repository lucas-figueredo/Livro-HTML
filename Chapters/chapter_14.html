CHAPTER 14 CASE STUDIES

14.1 STANDARDIZATION OF FPSOs: THE REPLICANTS

The standardization of equipment is an old practice in the industry. Heat exchangers, pumps, vessels, valves, pipelines, flexible lines, and well casing are examples of equipment that have been standardized to facilitate the construction process. Even equipment whose sizing is usually customized for process conditions, such as vessels, have standardized heads and inspection openings.

Flexible lines are now standardized in sizes of 4, 6, and 8 inches. Wells also have standard casing diameters.

However, standardizing an FPSO is much more difficult to achieve. The process plant comprises a large number of different systems designed to meet the specific characteristics of each oil field, such as gas-oil ratio, densities, viscosities, flow rates, etc. The water depth affects both the mooring system and the subsea pipelines. Flow assurance issues and reservoir characteristics are also field-dependent. Consequently, the design of such units is usually customized for each field, known as "tailor-made." Designing for the worst conditions is a trivial engineering solution but is not feasible from a business perspective, as it implies excessive costs and waste. For these and other reasons, standardization of such units has never been widely implemented.

After analyzing operational data, lessons learned from Petrobras’ long history of implemented projects, and evaluating engineering methods and criteria used for FPSO design, in 2008, the author conceived a new design methodology that enabled the achievement of these ambitious goals. The principle was that the many uncertainties inherent in the development of oil production systems should be incorporated into FPSO design—something not previously done. The aim was to design a flexible FPSO in terms of flow rate, oil type, contaminants, water depth, etc.

A radical change in design philosophy was proposed, where major equipment would have fixed dimensions, and to accommodate different flow rates, they would be installed in a modular plug-and-play style, similar to a Lego system. As an example, consider the sizing of a seawater injection system. To maintain reservoir pressure, it is necessary to inject a volume of water similar to the liquid production. For production systems ranging between 150,000 and 200,000 barrels of oil per day, the maximum liquid flow rate must be estimated. If preliminary estimates for a project predict 300,000 bpd of injection water, the designer should plan for the installation of three pumps, leaving the fourth slot empty. If, during the project, it is concluded that the demand will be 350,000 bpd instead of 300,000 bpd, the designer should now foresee the installation of four pumps. This would result in an injection capacity of 400,000 bpd, which is above the necessary demand. At first glance, this excess capacity may seem like a waste and would not occur in a tailor-made project, which would adopt 3 x 117,000 bpd. However, the benefits of standardizing pump capacities and power ratings outweigh this apparent inefficiency.

The philosophy demonstrated in the example above was applied as much as possible in the conceptual design of the entire process plant, always aiming to make it as flexible as possible. Entire modules, such as the CO\(_2\) separation module, were dimensioned this way. In this new design philosophy, this module would not be installed if CO\(_2\) were absent in the target field. The space reserved for its installation on the ship’s deck would remain empty, but the design would remain unchanged.

Later, the ship’s hull was standardized to accommodate the plant. The chosen mooring system was spread mooring, designed for operation in ultra-deep waters. The standardization of the process plant and the vessel resulted in a flexible FPSO that could be used in fields with different characteristics. The conceptual design was named Replicant. Subsequently, the basic design was executed. However, the proposal to leave empty slots was not adopted, which reduced the final project’s flexibility.

The discovery of the Pre-Salt area, with its vast reserves, combined with the flexibility of the standards, presented an opportunity to advance projects by contracting a large number of ships without predefined locations. Preliminary data showed that differences in GOR, API, field depth, etc., could be accommodated by the standard vessel. Petrobras then decided to contract eight similar units, an unprecedented move in the history of the oil industry.

The Rio Grande shipyard was chosen to carry out the construction of the Replicants. Several companies were contracted to supply the modules and equipment.
